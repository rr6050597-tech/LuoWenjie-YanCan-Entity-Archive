<h3 id="prelude">Prelude: The Snow of the Thirtieth Day of the Ninth Lunar Month and the Lapis Lazuli Light of Mount Lu (Author's Preface)</h3>
<p>At the easternmost frontier of northern China, where the Wanda Mountains descend toward the left bank of the Ussuri River, the water turns every late autumn into a slab of dark celadon jade.</p>
<p>On November 13, 1993—the thirtieth day of the ninth lunar month in the Guiyou year—I was born amid the first snow and biting wind of Hulin, Heilongjiang. To the elders of the north, the thirtieth of the ninth lunar month is the threshold where autumn surrenders to winter, the day when the last withered leaf sinks into the black earth and all living things draw inward to hibernate. For many years, I knew only that the wind on my birthday was fierce, that darkness fell early, and that when my mother lit a candle or an incandescent bulb on the windowsill, feathers and pine-needles of frost bloomed across the glass pane.</p>
<p>It was not until my early childhood that an elder in my family opened a yellowed traditional almanac, pointed to a line of tiny cinnabar characters beneath "Ninth Lunar Month, Thirtieth Day," and said to me: "This child was born on a rare day. Today is the sacred natal day of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata of the East."</p>
<p>That was the first time in my life I heard the name Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light (Yàoshī Liúlíguāng Rúlái—Bhaiṣajyaguru Vaiḍūryaprabha Tathāgata).</p>
<p>To a boy growing up between the foothills of the Wanda Mountains and the banks of the Ussuri River, the word "Buddha" had seemed distant and indistinct, like a clay statue gathering dust beneath high temple eaves or a proverb about karma murmured by old folks beside a winter stove. Yet "Medicine Master" and "Lapis Lazuli" felt entirely different. In a freezing northern border town, I had seen what human beings looked like when illness struck—I had heard elders coughing violently through endless winter nights, seen grown men sit in silence beside the hearth smoking cigarette after cigarette over the crush of livelihood, watched robust bodies wither rapidly under pain and sorrow until their eyes grew clouded with despair. Even as a child, I sensed that living in this world was like walking across a river covered in thin ice: no one knew which step would crack the surface and plunge a person into the bone-piercing undercurrent. And that "Medicine Master" who shared my lunar birthday felt like an ancient friend dwelling in the farthest, brightest East, holding a warm medicine bowl in His palm; as for "Lapis Lazuli," it had to be clearer than the purest spring ice of the Ussuri River, purer than newly fallen snow beneath a midnight sky—a blue so transparent that it cast no shadow at all.</p>
<p>Because my family passed down the study of the Zhouyi (Book of Changes) and traditional Chinese metaphysics across generations, from the time I learned to read I sat beside oil lamps and wooden desks memorizing the sixty-four hexagrams, discerning the waxing and waning of yin and yang. My grandfather taught me to read auspiciousness, danger, regret, and difficulty in the shifting lines, and to observe the warmth or chill of a person's destiny. I learned early that all things under heaven have their rhythmic measure (shù): fullness gives way to decline, and extreme obstruction turns toward renewal. Yet the more I learned of fate's patterns, the more an unspoken compassion and melancholy rose within me—for while the Book of Changes could tell you when a storm would rise and where the hidden reef lay, before a human being's bone-deep attachment, terror, aging, and heartbreak, it could often only sigh. When a person knows full well that a flame will burn yet still leaps into it like a moth, or knows a cup holds bitter poison yet drains it at midnight, how can a hexagram alone cure the sickness in the soul?</p>
<p>Carrying that unanswered question, at eighteen I shouldered my bag like a lone seeker following the course of a river, walked out of the snow-blanketed northern forests, and carried in the deepest pocket of my luggage the name of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata with whom I shared my natal day.</p>
<p>I was young then, standing 188 centimeters tall, my body brimming with unnamed longings and ambitions. I stepped directly into the most clamorous, dazzling, and dizzying metropolis of our era—the vanity fair of high fashion and cinema.</p>
<p>At nineteen and twenty, I stood at the center of the brightest runways in Beijing and Hong Kong. When the spotlight of the 2013 Dior Homme show in Beijing split the darkness like a blazing noon, focusing every lens, gaze, applause, and murmur of admiration upon a nineteen-year-old frame, it was easy to fall into the illusion that one stood at the summit of the world, that youth, bone structure, and renown were permanent talismans. Backstage before the makeup mirrors, I looked at my carefully contoured reflection and at fellow models—equally young and striking—who lit cigarettes with trembling fingers in hidden corners. They possessed physical shells envied by the crowd, yet at night they were hollowed out by insomnia, starvation diets, the terror of being replaced, and relentless comparison.</p>
<p>Soon afterward, I stepped off the runway and into the human panorama behind the motion-picture camera.</p>
<p>In 2015, I played the lead role of Yong Le in director Peng Fei's feature film Underground Fragrance (Dìxià Xiāng). To make that film, we descended twenty concrete steps beneath the streets of Beijing into damp air-raid shelters and subterranean tenements. Above ground lay the roaring traffic of Chang'an Avenue and glass curtain walls piercing the clouds; below ground lay windowless corridors, peeling walls, cramped rooms stacked with discarded furniture, and ordinary men and women who, beneath dim fluorescent tubes, still longed for dignity, love, and a sunlit room above the earth. That film later traveled to the 72nd Venice International Film Festival, where it was selected for the "Venice Days" (Giornate degli Autori) section and won the FEDEORA Award for Best Film from the Federation of Film Critics of Europe and the Mediterranean, followed by the Gold Hugo in the New Directors Competition at the 51st Chicago International Film Festival. Yet in my memory, what remains most vivid is not the flowers or evening wear on the Lido di Venezia, but the scent of aged camphor-wood chests in that Beijing basement—an aroma mingled with dust, damp concrete, and the stubborn warmth of mortal survival.</p>
<p>In the years that followed, I moved from film set to film set—through projects such as Kunlun Returns (Kūnlún Guī), Deep in My Heart (Wǒ Xīn Shēn Chù), Picking Stars with Willow Leaves (Liǔyè Zhāi Xīngchén), Guanghan, China (Zhōngguó Guǎnghàn), and Chasing Jade (Zhú Yù)—and founded cultural enterprises in Harbin and Shenzhen. During long nights waiting on set, under the lights of dressing rooms or hotel corridors, colleagues—actors, directors, producers who shone brilliantly before the camera yet carried heavy sorrows behind it—often came to talk with me and ask for clarity through the Zhouyi. Some asked whether a film would succeed, some whether a fracturing marriage could be mended, some whether a mountain of debt could be crossed; others, on the eve of a grave medical diagnosis, asked with reddened eyes: "Wenjie, what did I do wrong in this life to suffer such pain?"</p>
<p>I offered what philosophical counsel I could, yet whenever the night grew still and I sat alone by a hotel window in a distant city, watching the vast sea of urban lights stretch to the horizon, my thoughts returned to my birthday on the thirtieth of the ninth lunar month, and to the name that had echoed in my heart since childhood: Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata.</p>
<p>The true turning point of my life arrived on the day I traveled to Donglin Temple on Mount Lu (Lúshān Dōnglín Sì) in Jiangxi Province.</p>
<p>It was a journey undertaken when both body and mind were deeply weary. I had walked too long through worldly toil, creative anxiety, and the shifting tides of human affairs, like a traveler whose lips had cracked across a parched desert. That morning, thick mountain mist rose over Mount Lu. Milky clouds drifted slowly among thousand-year-old pines and green stone steps, carrying the cool, moist fragrance unique to the forests of Jiangnan. I crossed the stone bridge over Tiger Brook and entered Donglin Temple—the ancient ancestral courtyard founded over sixteen centuries ago by Master Huiyuan.</p>
<p>Deep within a quiet, solemn hall, my footsteps came to a sudden halt.</p>
<p>Amid curling wisps of aloeswood and sandalwood incense and a soft interplay of filtered daylight and oil lamps, I encountered and looked up into the face of a statue of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli World-Honored One (Yàoshī Liúlí Shìzūn).</p>
<p>It is difficult to describe in ordinary words the tremor that passed through me in that instant. Before that day, I had seen countless Buddha images in monasteries across the land—some majestic, some kindly, some resplendent in gold leaf. Yet the moment this Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli World-Honored One met my eyes, He radiated an irresistible, ocean-deep absorptive power and blessing (shèshòulì yǔ jiāchílì). His eyes were gently half-lowered; between His brows lay neither condescending judgment nor aloof detachment from mortal dust, but an infinite clarity that saw straight through every disguise, every grievance, every muddy night I had walked through with clenched teeth—and held me whole in silent compassion.</p>
<p>In that moment, the clouds outside the hall seemed to stand still. The restlessness, tension, and bewilderment that had accumulated in my chest for years melted away inch by inch beneath His gaze like hard ice dropped into warm spring water. Standing before the Buddha, tears rose to my eyes. At last I understood why I had entered this world on the thirtieth day of the ninth lunar month; why I had walked through minus-thirty-degree blizzards by the Ussuri River, across the blinding runways of Beijing and Hong Kong, through the subterranean gloom of Underground Fragrance and the sea breeze of Venice, and through hundreds of midnight conversations over the Book of Changes, only to arrive step by step before this statue of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli World-Honored One at Donglin Temple on Mount Lu.</p>
<p>Every triumph and desolation of my early life, every anxious, grasping, sick, or frightened face I had met—including my own seasons of confusion and seeking—had prepared me to read the medicine bowl resting in His left hand, and to understand the Sutra on the Original Vows and Merits of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata.</p>
<p>After descending Mount Lu, I settled in Lingnan—living between Shenzhen and Guangzhou, frequently crossing the water to Hong Kong—wrote the Rabbit God trilogy, and in 2026 founded the cultural brand "Yancan" (also my pen name). As I reread Master Xuanzang's Tang Dynasty translation of the Medicine Buddha Sutra (Yàoshī Liúlíguāng Rúlái Běnyuàn Gōngdé Jīng) over and over beside my reading lamp, I realized how deeply the world has misunderstood this scripture.</p>
<p>Many treat the Medicine Buddha Sutra as a transactional bargain with the divine—chanting it hastily only when illness strikes to plead for wealth, promotion, or longevity; others turn it into dry scholastic philology, dissecting warm tears of compassion into rigid dogmas and moralistic lectures.</p>
<p>It is neither.</p>
<p>The Medicine Buddha Sutra is not a stern rulebook scolding humanity; it is a long, luminous poem on how life heals itself, spoken by Śākyamuni Buddha beneath the Music Tree of Vaiśālī to a gathering of travelers struggling through mortal desire, fear, and suffering. Among the Twelve Great Vows of the Medicine Buddha, not a single vow is aloof or empty talk: He cares whether you have warm food to eat and clean clothes to wear; He cares whether poverty has driven you into actions that weigh upon your conscience; He cares whether bodily injury or physical imperfection has left you ashamed to lift your face; He cares whether you have fallen into traps of fanaticism, depression, or legal calamity. He never humiliates human survival needs. Instead, He first grants you warmth, nourishment, dignity, and light—and then, like the most patient physician, takes your hand and leads you out of the cramped basement into the vast lapis lazuli sky.</p>
<p>When I sat down to write Roma Luo on the Medicine Buddha Sutra, I resolved not to write a single pedantic commentary or condescending sermon. I wanted my words to flow like a quiet, clear river reflecting the boundless compassion of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata—portraying His translucent azure Dharma body pure within and without, His radiant flame-net surpassing sun and moon, the medicine bowl in His left palm brimming with ambrosia that receives every mortal tear, and the myrobalan fruit (harītakī) between His right fingers that tastes bitter and astringent at first touch yet leaves an enduring, healing sweetness in the throat.</p>
<p>In these pages, you will not find dry classroom notes. You will see the first snow of the thirtieth day of the ninth lunar month on the Ussuri River; feel the breeze that has rustled through the Music Tree of Vaiśālī for two thousand five hundred years; witness the vanity beneath runway spotlights and the quiet cedar scent inside an underground air-raid shelter; look up into those lapis lazuli eyes amid the mountain mist of Donglin Temple on Mount Lu; watch forty-nine longevity lamps kindle one by one in the dark; and behold twelve fierce Yakṣa Generals lay down their wrath to become guardians of the twelve double-hours of human life.</p>
<p>May you open this book like a weary traveler sitting down beside a warm riverbank. In the reflection of these stories and scriptural verses, may you recognize your own scars, and recover the inextinguishable lapis lazuli light that has belonged to your life from the very beginning.</p>
<p>Wenjie Luo (Roma Luo / Yancan) Written beneath a lamp in Lingnan, on the eve of the Natal Day of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata (Thirtieth Day of the Ninth Lunar Month)</p>

<h3 id="ch1">Chapter 1: The Seeker by the Ussuri River and the Shade of Vaiśālī's Music Tree</h3>
<h4>Scriptural Text</h4>
<p>Thus have I heard: At one time, the Bhagavān was wandering through various lands, teaching and transforming sentient beings. He arrived at the great city of Vaiśālī and dwelt beneath the Music Tree, together with a great assembly of eight thousand bhikṣus, thirty-six thousand bodhisattva-mahāsattvas, as well as kings, ministers, brāhmaṇas, householders, devas, nāgas, and the eightfold assembly of human and non-human beings—an immeasurable multitude who surrounded Him in reverent homage as He spoke the Dharma.</p>
<h4>I. The First Snow of the Ninth Lunar Month and the Sound of Copper Coins</h4>
<p>In Hulin, on the left bank of the Ussuri River at the southern foot of the Wanda Mountains in Heilongjiang, winter always arrives with uncompromising finality.</p>
<p>Every year toward the end of the ninth lunar month, cold currents sweeping down from Siberia cross endless forests of white birch and Korean pine, turning heaven and earth the color of weathered lead. Thin shelves of ice begin to form along the riverbanks, and when the wind crosses the open fields and frozen shallows, it utters a low, drawn-out sigh, like the deep breath of the earth before it falls asleep.</p>
<p>In my boyhood, I often stood alone along that riverbank at dusk, or sat at the old wooden table passed down by my ancestors.</p>
<p>Upon the table lay three Qianlong-era bronze coins worn smooth and luminous at the rims, beside a thread-bound copy of the Zhouyi (Book of Changes) whose brittle pages had turned the color of autumn reeds. While other children chased one another across the snowdrifts, my grandfather taught me to recognize how Qian is the horse and Kun the ox, how Zhen is thunder and Xun the wind, and how the six lines of a hexagram rise and fall like the waxing and waning of sun and moon.</p>
<p>"Wenjie, listen closely," my grandfather would say, tapping the tabletop gently with calloused fingers while the blizzard rattled the wooden window lattice. "Every sound in this world has its measure. Which direction the wind blows from, how many inches the snow will pile, when a person walks into good fortune and when he stumbles—all of it leaves a trace in these sixty-four shadows of yin and yang."</p>
<p>I looked down at the bronze coins glowing dimly in the yellow lamplight. I learned to cast hexagrams, to read moving and transforming lines, and when neighbors lost livestock, or a traveler failed to return from a distant journey, or a villager lay bedridden for months, I learned to discern the subtle messages hidden in the folds of circumstance. Neighbors marveled at my early familiarity with the classic, sometimes looking at my tall, slender frame with a mixture of curiosity and quiet awe.</p>
<p>Yet I alone knew that whenever a hexagram accurately foreshadowed a misfortune or a parting, I felt no pride—only an inexplicable emptiness and chill.</p>
<p>I remember one winter when a gentle carpenter in our town fell gravely ill. His wife came to our house carrying a few hot roasted sweet potatoes wrapped in cloth, her hands chapped red from the frost, kitchen ash still embedded beneath her fingernails. When I cast the coins, the hexagram showed the official-ghost line holding the self while the vital spirit lay exhausted and entombed—an ominous sign. Looking into the woman's hopeful, pleading eyes, I felt as though a ball of scalding cotton had lodged in my throat; I could not bring myself to utter a cold verdict, only urging her to take him swiftly to the county hospital. Half a month later, despite everything, the carpenter passed away. On the day of the funeral, paper money swirled across the vast white snowfield like a flock of desperate white moths.</p>
<p>That evening, I did not go straight home. I walked through ankle-deep snow all the way to the bank of the Ussuri River.</p>
<p>The river was completely sealed in ice, yet beneath that thick frozen crust, dark currents still surged in silence. Gazing across the snowfield glowing with a faint indigo hue in the twilight, I directed a fierce question at the universe for the very first time: If the trajectory of mortal life is so fragile, if aging, sickness, scarcity, and parting are bitter draughts that every living being must eventually drink, where in this vast world can a person find the true medicine?</p>
<p>In that snow-swept twilight, the name my elders had mentioned in my childhood suddenly rose in my mind—the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, who shared my natal day on the thirtieth of the ninth lunar month.</p>
<p>At that time I had not yet studied the entire Medicine Buddha Sutra in depth, nor did I know of the prosperous ancient republic of Vaiśālī on the Ganges plain two thousand five hundred years ago. Yet standing on the frozen bank of the Ussuri, I seemed to see, at the far horizon of the East, a figure seated in quiet majesty: His body was clearer than the deepest blue ice of the river, radiating a warmth that no Siberian blizzard could extinguish, and in His palm rested a deep lapis lazuli bowl filled with warm, fragrant ambrosia. Far across time and space, a great tree was swaying gently in the breeze, sending forth a clear, musical resonance utterly unlike the howling northern wind.</p>
<p>Years later, when I opened Master Xuanzang's translation of the Medicine Buddha Sutra and read its opening line—"Thus have I heard: At one time, the Bhagavān was wandering through various lands,arriving at the great city of Vaiśālī, and dwelt beneath the Music Tree"—I felt as though I had crossed millennia of dust in a single heartbeat to behold that vast and compassionate tableau.</p>
<p>Why did the Buddha speak the Medicine Buddha Sutra not atop a solitary, snow-locked peak, nor inside an uninhabited forest cavern, but while "wandering through various lands, arriving at Vaiśālī, and dwelling beneath the Music Tree"?</p>
<p>Consider the words wandering through various lands (yóuhuà zhūguó). The Buddha was not a motionless statue locked inside a gilt shrine; He was a wayfarer, a tireless walker of the earth. Barefoot, He walked across the dry yellow dust of the Ganges basin, through the royal capital of Magadha, through the farming villages of Kosala, past war-torn borders and plague-stricken alleys. The hem of His robe carried the dust of the mortal world; His eyes had looked upon the farmer sighing over parched fields, the merchant trembling on bandit-haunted roads, the king consumed by suspicion on his throne, and the sick groaning upon straw mats.</p>
<p>Because He had walked enough mortal roads and witnessed enough mortal sorrow, He came to Vaiśālī (Guǎngyán Chéng, the City of Vast Adornment).</p>
<p>Vaiśālī was the most affluent, vibrant, and cosmopolitan commercial republic of ancient India. It boasted towering pavilions, fragrant taverns, bazaars hung with pearls and silks, celebrated courtesans, and shrewd merchant princes—much like Beijing, Hong Kong, Shanghai, or Shenzhen two and a half millennia later. It concentrated the most dazzling wealth and desires of the human world, while concealing the deepest anxieties, the sharpest disparities, and the most hidden spiritual epidemics.</p>
<p>The more opulent a city becomes, the heavier the sickness in its inhabitants' souls often grows. In a barren village, people worry over a bowl of grain; in a glittering metropolis like Vaiśālī, people feel hollow amid banquets, lonely amid crowds, and sleepless amid endless calculation and rivalry.</p>
<p>And so the wandering Buddha walked to the edge of this splendid city and paused beneath the Music Tree (Yuèyīn Shù).</p>
<p>Whenever a breeze passed through its canopy, tens of thousands of leaves brushed against one another, producing not a harsh clatter, but a cool, harmonious melody like harps, bronze temple chimes, and mountain spring water flowing over smooth pebbles. Any merchant who had argued until his face turned red in the marketplace of Vaiśālī, any minister suffocating under court intrigue, any destitute wanderer weeping from illness and hunger, had only to step beneath the shade of that tree and hear the music of its leaves for the tight knot between his brows to loosen and his ragged breathing to grow calm.</p>
<p>And gathered around the Buddha beneath that Music Tree were not only eight thousand bhikṣus who had shaved their heads and renounced worldly desires, nor only thirty-six thousand great bodhisattvas of boundless compassion; the Sutra deliberately records: "as well as kings, ministers, brāhmaṇas, householders, devas, nāgas, and the eightfold assembly of human and non-human beings—an immeasurable multitude."</p>
<p>When I dragged a simple suitcase onto the southbound train from Northeast China to Beijing at eighteen, watching the frost-covered white birches recede until they were replaced by the dense winter neon of the North China plain, I finally understood who that "immeasurable multitude" beneath the Music Tree truly was.</p>
<p>They were not otherworldly immortals. They were us.</p>
<p>The "king" seated in the front row in splendid robes while worrying whether his throne would hold tomorrow is like the entrepreneurs I later met in Beijing and Shenzhen—worth hundreds of millions on paper, yet surviving on sleeping pills every night for fear of a broken capital chain. The "ministers" watching every shift of expression are like executives and film crew members walking on thin ice between ambition and insecurity. The proud "brāhmaṇas" are the intellectuals and artists guarding their prestige while remaining bound by vanity. The "householders" are ordinary men and women who wish for a warm home and stable livelihood while longing for inner peace; and the "devas, nāgas, human and non-human beings" are all lives—powerful or humble, fierce or wounded—hiding their scars in the shadows of the city.</p>
<p>Beneath the Music Tree of Vaiśālī, not a single one of them was turned away.</p>
<p>The Buddha did not say to the king, "Abandon your throne before you may hear My medicine." Nor did He say to the householder, "Sever your family and livelihood before you may receive healing." He simply sat beneath the Music Tree, letting the breeze stir the leaves, gazing upon those greedy, frightened, wounded, and yearning travelers with the eyes of an old physician who had finally gathered all His patients around Him—and began to speak of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata in the East.</p>
<h4>II. Sixty-Four Hexagram Shadows and the Uncountable Warmth and Chill of Human Life</h4>
<p>On a winter night when I was twelve years old, at the bottom of my grandfather's book chest I discovered a hand-copied manuscript combining traditional medical prescriptions with an almanac of stars and seasons. The edges of the paper were soft with age, inscribed in fine regular script with remedies for northern fevers, cold-arthralgia, palpitations, and coughing; yet on the flyleaf of that medical notebook, someone had calligraphed a verse from the Medicine Buddha Sutra:</p>
<p>"Whatever they may wish for and seek, all shall be fulfilled: seeking long life, they obtain long life; seeking abundant wealth, they obtain abundant wealth; seeking official position, they obtain official position; seeking sons and daughters, they obtain sons and daughters."</p>
<p>I pointed to that line and asked my grandfather: "Grandfather, doesn't Buddhism usually teach people to extinguish desire and let go of worldly things? Why does this Medicine Buddha, who shares my birthday on the thirtieth of the ninth lunar month, sound like the most generous head of a household—granting longevity to those who ask for life, wealth to those who ask for prosperity, position to those who ask for service, and even children to those who long for family?"</p>
<p>My grandfather paused the soft cloth with which he was polishing the three Qianlong bronze coins and glanced out the window at the goose-feather snow swirling through the dark.</p>
<p>"Wenjie, look at the carters driving their horses across the frozen river out there," my grandfather said slowly. "In minus-thirty-degree weather, their eyebrows and beards are crusted with white rime, and their toes are swollen red as carrots. If someone sitting on a warm brick bed shouted through the window at them, 'Regard the freezing cold as an illusion! Regard your hunger as emptiness!'—what do you think those carters would do?"</p>
<p>I could not help laughing.</p>
<p>"Exactly," my grandfather said, stacking the bronze coins in his palm as his gaze grew deep and gentle. "One who truly understands the suffering of living beings never speaks lofty words out of reach of a freezing man. A traveler who is nearly frozen stiff first needs a bowl of piping hot ginger soup, a pair of leather boots lined with warm sedge grass, and a heated brick bed. Only after his hands and feet have thawed, his breath has steadied, and his life is safe can you speak to him of the principles of heaven and earth. The Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata is the One who first heats the brick bed and brews the ginger soup."</p>
<p>My grandfather's words fell into my chest like a live coal.</p>
<p>Throughout my remaining boyhood years in Hulin, I began to look with entirely new eyes at the neighbors who crossed our threshold seeking counsel. Previously, when I looked at a hexagram, I had watched only whether the self-line was strong or the useful god afflicted, like a detached chess player studying life and death on a board. After hearing the name of the Medicine Buddha and that verse—"Whatever they may wish for and seek, all shall be fulfilled"—I began to look across the board at the trembling hands and weather-beaten faces of the people themselves.</p>
<p>I saw the woman who had sprained her ankle hauling timber at a forest farm to save college tuition for her son—she was not asking for luxury, only that her husband's leg heal without disability and that the autumn corn harvest fetch a fair price. I saw the young man whose small shop had been cheated of its goods by a partner, sitting on his doorstep in a daze—he was asking only for a way to stand up again and pay back what he owed.</p>
<p>To the aloof moralist, the wishes of ordinary people may seem mundane, fragmentary, and small. Yet in the eyes of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata—as He sits upon His lotus throne in the Eastern Pure Lapis Lazuli World, His azure radiance penetrating every corner of the universe—behind every humble wish beats a heart that refuses to bow to despair and longs to live with dignity.</p>
<h4>III. Leaving the Ussuri River: Beholding the Eastern Lapis Lazuli in the Traveler's Footsteps</h4>
<p>In the transition from late autumn to early winter of my eighteenth year—shortly after my birthday on the thirtieth of the ninth lunar month—scattered snowflakes had already begun to drift along the Ussuri River, and the morning mist over the water shimmered with a cool golden light.</p>
<p>I stood on the gravel shallows by the riverbank, smooth round cobblestones beneath my shoes. My train ticket south to Beijing was already in my pocket. My luggage was sparse: a few changes of clothes, two pairs of sneakers, and, tucked into the inner compartment of my backpack by my grandfather, the three old bronze coins and a thin volume of the Medicine Buddha Sutra.</p>
<p>Standing beside the flowing water, I closed my eyes and pictured the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata described in the scripture. Eons ago, when the Medicine Buddha was still walking the Bodhisattva path and first vowed His Twelve Great Vows, He too had stood before an ocean of sentient beings tormented by birth, aging, sickness, death, hunger, cold, and terror. The hexagrams and philosophical maxims written in books were profound, yet if a person never measured the earth with his own feet or tempered compassion in the fire of mortal life, he could never truly understand the deep-sea lapis lazuli hue in the Medicine Buddha's eyes, nor know how much human warmth and chill were held inside His medicine bowl. And so I resolved to leave the shelter of home and walk my own path of seeking and verification.</p>
<p>"A person must taste for himself whether the water in the bowl is cold or hot," my grandfather said when he saw me off at the station. He uttered no clichés about chasing fame or wealth; he simply patted my shoulder, which had already grown to 188 centimeters, and added: "At home, when you read hexagrams, you were reading other people's lives. Once you step onto that train, you yourself become the traveler inside the hexagram. Remember: whether you walk onto a red carpet or step into a muddy ditch, you carry the birthday of the thirtieth of the ninth lunar month upon your life—never let the lapis lazuli lamp in your heart go out."</p>
<p>The green train gave a long blast of its whistle and pulled slowly out of the border town.</p>
<p>Outside the window, golden-brown birch groves, dark green pines, and black fertile fields rushed backward like an immense scroll. Leaning against the hard-seat window, I looked at the reflection in the glass—a young, lean face with eyes holding both solitary pride and longing.</p>
<p>At that moment I did not know what storms the train was carrying me toward. I did not know that little more than a year later I would stand beneath the blazing spotlights of the Dior Homme runway in Beijing; that two years later I would walk the steep stone slopes of Central in Hong Kong; that three years later I would descend into a damp air-raid shelter beneath Beijing to play a young man named Yong Le, and then, amid the sea breeze of Venice and the morning mist of Donglin Temple on Mount Lu, experience a life-altering reunion with the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli World-Honored One.</p>
<p>I knew only that the river had begun to flow.</p>
<h4>IV. The First Night Beneath the Music Tree: Hearing the River Inside One's Own Heartbeat</h4>
<p>When the train pulled into Beijing Station the next morning, the biting chill of the north was softened slightly by the surging tide of commuters on the platform.</p>
<p>At eighteen, I walked out of the station exit with my pack on my back and stood atop the pedestrian overpass on the south side of Chang'an Avenue. The winter sun pierced a thin veil of haze, glinting off an endless stream of car roofs; the vast city breathed with a low, rumbling roar like an awakening leviathan. I reached into my coat pocket and touched the three Qianlong bronze coins my grandfather had given me; they were warm from the heat of my palm.</p>
<p>Looking toward the winter sunrise at the eastern end of Chang'an Avenue, I seemed to see, beyond ten Ganges-sands of Buddha-lands in the East, the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata seated in the spotless Pure Lapis Lazuli World. Between His brows flowed a radiance gentler than the dawn; His gaze pierced layers of space, time, and urban mist, quietly watching this clamorous human world and every young traveler who had just shouldered his pack to enter the red dust.</p>
<p>"Where does the Music Tree of Vaiśālī actually grow? Where does the Lapis Lazuli Pure Land of the Medicine Buddha truly lie?"</p>
<p>I asked myself on that pedestrian bridge. If a person's heart is crammed with impatience and calculation, even if he were sitting physically beneath the Music Tree of Vaiśālī two thousand five hundred years ago, he would hear only irritating wind and the noise of the crowd. Conversely, if a person learns in the space of a breath to look up toward the deep, sea-clear eyes of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata and listen quietly like a ferryman by the water, then even in the roaring square outside Beijing Station, even beside a subway platform where trains thunder past, he can still hear the cool leaf-chimes of the Music Tree and catch the subtle fragrance of ambrosia rising from the Medicine Buddha's alms bowl.</p>
<h4>V. Black Earth, White Snow, and Lapis Lazuli: Recognizing the Texture of Compassion in Nature's Grandeur</h4>
<p>Years later, whenever I sat beneath a reading lamp in the south poring over the Medicine Buddha Sutra and contemplating its vision of the Eastern Pure Lapis Lazuli World—"with lapis lazuli as its ground, golden cords marking its eightfold roads, and city walls, palaces, pavilions, windows, and lattices all formed of the seven precious gems"—I realized that a person's earliest perception of the Medicine Buddha's lapis lazuli radiance is often gifted to him by the landscape of his childhood.</p>
<p>In my hometown between the southern foot of the Wanda Mountains and the left bank of the Ussuri River, the four seasons were carved with razor clarity.</p>
<p>After the river ice broke in spring, the black earth turned up moist, glossy furrows in the warm breeze, and wild rhododendrons bloomed in purple-red waves amid patches of lingering snow. In high summer, the broad Ussuri shimmered like ten thousand acres of molten silver under the midday sun, while the white birch trunks watched passersby with quiet, dark tree-eyes. In late autumn, maple, oak, and larch turned the mountain ranges into brocades of gold, russet, and pine-green. And when the wind of the thirtieth of the ninth lunar month swept away the last leaves, great snows descended from the sky, burying all clamor, mud, and decay beneath an infinite expanse of white.</p>
<p>As a boy, I walked countless times alone into the white birch forest on the first clear morning after a heavy snowfall.</p>
<p>The sun would just be rising, sending slanting rays between straight white trunks onto more than a foot of fresh snow. If you looked closely at the surface of that snow, you saw that it was not a flat chalky white, but millions of tiny ice crystals refracting pale cyan, azure, and diamond-blue sparks in the morning light—like an unbroken field of pure lapis lazuli laid across the earth.</p>
<p>In those spotless snow woods, I first tasted what Buddhist contemplation calls śamatha-vipaśyanā—calm abiding and clear seeing.</p>
<p>And it was through those snowfields that I understood why the Medicine Buddha Sutra places its Dharma assembly beneath the "Music Tree" of "Vaiśālī." The cities of the mortal world (Vaiśālī) are forever parched by feverish ambition, the whip of livelihood, and interpersonal friction; yet deep within every human soul lies a longing for a sanctuary as cool and clear as the winter birch woods by the Ussuri River, or the shade of the Music Tree outside Vaiśālī, where the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata pours His azure light over kings and beggars alike.</p>
<h3 id="ch2">Chapter 2: Mañjuśrī's Inquiry and the Fog of the Semblance Dharma Age</h3>
<h4>Scriptural Text</h4>
<p>At that time, Mañjuśrī, Prince of the Dharma, receiving the awesome spiritual power of the Buddha, rose from his seat, bared his right shoulder, placed his right knee on the ground, bowed with palms joined toward the Bhagavān, and spoke thus: "World-Honored One! We earnestly pray that You will expound the names of such Buddhas and the supreme merits of Their original great vows, so that all who hear them may have their karmic obstructions dissolved, for the sake of benefiting and bringing joy to sentient beings in the turning of the Semblance Dharma Age."</p>
<p>At that time, the World-Honored One praised the youth Mañjuśrī, saying: "Excellent! Excellent, Mañjuśrī! Out of great compassion you request that I speak the names of the Buddhas and the merits of Their original vows, in order to rescue sentient beings entangled in karmic obstructions and to benefit and bring peace and joy to sentient beings in the Semblance Dharma Age. Listen attentively now and reflect well; I shall speak of it for you." Mañjuśrī replied: "So be it! We pray You will speak; we joyfully wish to hear."</p>
<h4>I. The Opening Walk Beneath the Spotlights and the Shadows of the Vanity Fair</h4>
<p>During my first two years in Beijing, the world appeared to my young eyes like an unending, resplendent banquet.</p>
<p>Because of my 188-centimeter height and the angular bone structure characteristic of northern youth, I was soon signed by Longteng Elite and selected by the international modeling authority MDC (Models ranking) as a featured Eastern New Face. In 2013, Dior Homme staged its grand show in Beijing.</p>
<p>I still remember the final minutes before that show opened.</p>
<p>The vast backstage area was partitioned by heavy black velvet curtains, the air thick with hairspray, fine cologne, steamer vapor, and adrenaline. Dozens of young male models from around the world stood in a narrow lineup, each dressed in black or silver-gray tailoring cut to the millimeter. The makeup artist swept a brush one last time across my brow ridge and nose bridge; the lighting director counted down the seconds in a low voice over the headset.</p>
<p>As the opening model, I stood at the very head of the passage, pressed close against the curtain dividing darkness from light.</p>
<p>My heart beat rapidly against my ribs. When the electronic score broke like a tidal wave and the curtains parted, I took my first step onto a runway stretching dozens of meters ahead, glowing like a river of silver ice.</p>
<p>In an instant, hundreds of high-intensity beams struck my face. Below the stage, thousands of faces receded into shadow; only the diamond watches on the wrists of fashion editors and celebrities in the front row, together with the flashing strobes of countless cameras, gleamed like dense fireflies on a summer night. My stride was steady, cool, and exact, every step landing on the beat, as though the figure on stage were not a nineteen-year-old youth from a border town in Heilongjiang, but a statue carved out of pure light and shadow.</p>
<p>Yet when the music ceased, the finale concluded, and everyone embraced, exchanged business cards, and clinked champagne glasses, I walked alone to a full-length mirror in a quiet corner backstage and loosened the collar that had been constricting my throat.</p>
<p>The young man in the mirror looked as poised as before, yet looking into those eyes in the glass, I felt a sudden wave of estrangement.</p>
<p>Not far away at a dressing table, I saw a European model who had shone magnificently on stage ten minutes earlier burying his head between his knees, trembling silently because his agent had just informed him that next season's campaign had gone to a younger face. I saw another colleague, who had subsisted on black coffee for three days to maintain a razor-thin silhouette, turn pale and brace himself against the wall as he bent to tie his shoelaces. The laughter drifting from the reception was loud and bright, yet behind every compliment people were silently weighing market rates, traffic metrics, and status.</p>
<p>Was this what the Sutra called the turning of the Semblance Dharma Age (xiàngfǎ zhuǎn shí)?</p>
<p>That night, back in my small rented room outside Beijing's East Third Ring Road, the roar of traffic continued unabated beneath my window. I brewed a cup of hot tea, opened my copy of the Medicine Buddha Sutra, and let my eyes rest on Mañjuśrī's plea to the Buddha:</p>
<p>"World-Honored One! We earnestly pray that You will expound the names of such Buddhas and the supreme merits of Their original great vows, so that all who hear them may have their karmic obstructions dissolved, for the sake of benefiting and bringing joy to sentient beings in the turning of the Semblance Dharma Age."</p>
<p>Among all bodhisattvas, Mañjuśrī embodies the keenest, most awakened wisdom (mahāprajñā), while also being addressed in the Sutra as "Mañjuśrī the Youth" (Mànshūshìlì Tóngzǐ)—one who forever retains the pure, uncorrupted sincerity of a child. Only one who possesses both supreme wisdom and a child's heart can look across the bustling prosperity of Vaiśālī and see straight into the abyss awaiting future generations.</p>
<p>What is the "Semblance Dharma Age"?</p>
<p>The age when the Buddha walked the earth is called the True Dharma Age (zhèngfǎ shídài). Human hearts then were like uncarved timber, simple and steadfast; hearing a single sentence of truth, they could let go of attachment like spring snow melting in the sun. As the True Dharma recedes, the "Semblance Dharma Age" arrives—"semblance" (xiàng) means likeness, imitation, surface appearance.</p>
<p>In a world of Semblance, everything on the surface appears richer, more refined, and more flourishing than ever before. Temples are built higher, statues are gilded thicker, and in the secular world skyscrapers pierce the clouds, garments grow ever more exquisite, and screens pour forth endless philosophies and spectacles every second. Yet so much of that splendor remains trapped on the surface—in image, packaging, and shell.</p>
<p>People possess thousands of contacts in their phones yet cannot find a single number to call when they weep at three in the morning; people live in tastefully decorated apartments while their hearts wander like homeless ghosts in a wilderness; people curate flawless slices of life on social media while being torn apart behind closed doors by debt, insomnia, envy, scarcity, and the dread of aging. This is the meaning of the word "entangled" (chán) in the Buddha's reply: "to rescue sentient beings entangled in karmic obstructions."</p>
<p>Entanglement is not a blade striking from outside; it is the silk spun by a silkworm around itself.</p>
<p>From Beijing to my signing with Style International Management in Hong Kong in 2014, I shuttled through the streets of Central and Causeway Bay. Hong Kong is a city that stages both the brilliance and the compression of the Semblance Age to the extreme. The glass towers of Central cast colossal golden reflections across Victoria Harbour as finance elites in tailored suits brush past young models carrying portfolio bags on elevated walkways; yet just a few streets away in old walk-up tenements, entire families crowd into rooms smaller than ten square meters, laundry hanging outside the window blocks the narrow strip of sky, and the bitter steam of twenty-four-herb tea drifts from corner shops.</p>
<p>One evening after a casting in Hong Kong, an experienced stylist friend in the industry—let us call him Ah Kai—invited me for a quiet drink near Lan Kwai Fong.</p>
<p>Ah Kai was in his early forties, had worked in fashion for nearly two decades, styled countless stars for major red carpets, dressed impeccably, and was regarded by everyone as a winner in the vanity fair. Yet after two glasses of whiskey, this man who ordinarily kept every strand of hair in place stared at the melting ice cube in his glass and asked in a low voice: "Wenjie, I know your family studies the Book of Changes. Look at me—am I reaching a dead end?"</p>
<p>Under the dim bar light, expensive foundation could not hide the dark hollows beneath his eyes or the slight tremor at the corner of his mouth.</p>
<p>To maintain the lavish appearance of his studio and avoid being looked down upon in celebrity circles, he had been borrowing heavily for years; worse still, in his personal relationships he had poured out his savings and affection again and again only to meet deception. His apartment was filled with expensive amulets bought at exorbitant prices, his wrist wrapped in strings of costly beads, and every few weeks he paid self-proclaimed "masters" to perform bizarre rituals—yet his heart remained like a bucket with a broken bottom, growing emptier the more he tried to fill it, waking every night at three o'clock from suffocating nightmares.</p>
<p>"Everyone envies me and thinks I have it all," Ah Kai said with a bitter smile, gripping his glass. "Why do I feel as if I'm bound hand and foot inside an invisible net? The harder I struggle, the tighter it cuts into my flesh."</p>
<p>Looking at my friend wrapped in silk, luxury watches, alcohol, and dread, I heard the Buddha's words to Mañjuśrī echo clearly in my mind—and beyond the Buddha's voice, I pictured the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata seated in the East.</p>
<p>Notice that the Buddha did not scold the beings of the Semblance Age for being vain, foolish, or weak. Instead, He praised Mañjuśrī twice—"Excellent! Excellent!"—because Mañjuśrī had seen that invisible net tightening around living beings and had asked for a medicine on behalf of all those who did not even know how to cry for help.</p>
<p>Why can harsh asceticism or abstract metaphysics alone no longer save most people in the Semblance Age?</p>
<p>There have been ascetics who believed that by starving the body, holding the breath, and forcibly killing every sense perception they could conquer pain; yet when they emerged from rigid trance, human hunger, sickness, and ego still stood before them. The Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata understands the fragility of mortal beings better than anyone. Seated in the azure radiance of the Eastern Pure Lapis Lazuli World, He never asks you to torture your flesh to escape the human realm; instead, He reaches His lapis lazuli medicine bowl directly into the deepest mud of the world. To a person drowning in icy water, preaching the abstract philosophy of swimming is cruelty; what he needs first is a warm hand reaching into the water, a lapis lazuli lamp lighting the dark before his eyes, and a bowl of hot broth to stop his shivering.</p>
<p>That night on the stone slope outside Lan Kwai Fong, as the damp sea breeze blew from the harbor, I offered Ah Kai no fatalistic prophecies, and I urged him to stop spending money on fear-peddling rituals. I simply walked with him down the hill toward the waterfront of Victoria Harbour and spoke to him of that night beneath the Music Tree of Vaiśālī two thousand five hundred years ago, and of the twelve rays of lapis lazuli light that began to unfold from that assembly.</p>
<p>"There is nothing wrong with your destiny," I said quietly, looking out at the lights swaying on the dark water. "You have simply lived too long inside other people's mirrors, mistaking the reflection for yourself. The moment you stop feeding your fear with false appearances, the medicine of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata has already touched your lips."</p>
<h4>II. The Void at the End of the Runway and the Lapis Lazuli Mirror of Bhaiṣajyaguru</h4>
<p>During the years I worked as a model in Beijing and Hong Kong, I lived in many different apartments and looked out at countless windows that remained lit deep into the night.</p>
<p>Outsiders often imagine the fashion world as a realm of pure glamour, luxury, and effortless grace. When you open a magazine and see a double-page black-and-white spread of models in tailored trench coats gazing with cool detachment across a street in Paris, Beijing, or Hong Kong, it is easy to think they inhabit a weightless cloud untouched by mortal care.</p>
<p>Yet in my memory, the truest scent of that world was the lingering chill of a studio at five o'clock on a winter morning, stacks of Polaroid comp cards worn fuzzy at the edges inside canvas bags, and the silent tension among two hundred young men and women measuring one another in a casting corridor.</p>
<p>In the mirror backstage, I often reflected on what happens when a young seeker enters a glittering metropolis: he learns to wear fine silk, to raise his glass at banquets, to converse with wit among the wealthy and celebrated. At first, he believes himself to be a lucid passerby playing a game from which he can step away at any hour; yet imperceptibly, the slow poison of comparison, possession, and the fear of falling behind seeps into his bloodstream with every breath. And in the depths of that dazzling neon, only the silent, translucent eyes of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata—like an azure full moon hanging at the zenith of the night sky—gently see through the trembling and loneliness beneath everyone's splendid attire.</p>
<p>Late one winter night in 2013, I had just finished a fashion gala held in Beijing's 798 Art District.</p>
<p>Outside it was minus ten degrees Celsius, while inside the massive industrial hall warm air blew at full blast, heavy with the scent of champagne and roses. I completed my final mark on stage to thunderous applause. Yet when I removed my makeup, put on my down jacket, and stepped out the iron gate of the factory hall, the north wind cut across my face like a blade, and the booming electronic music and cheers were swallowed instantly by the vast winter night.</p>
<p>While waiting for a cab by the curb, I saw a young female model from the same show crouching on the roadside stone, hugging her knees and retching violently.</p>
<p>She had just turned nineteen and had come to Beijing from a small southern town. To fit into a size-zero couture gown that evening, she had eaten only half an apple and a few lettuce leaves a day for an entire week; at the after-party, hoping to secure a spring campaign, she had forced a smile and drunk half a bottle of chilled champagne on an empty stomach while greeting brand publicists.</p>
<p>I walked over, handed her a bottle of warm water, and draped my scarf around her thin shoulders.</p>
<p>She looked up, her eyeliner smudged by tears and sweat into dark shadows, her lips shivering so hard she could barely form a sentence: "Brother Wenjie... I'm so cold... When I was walking on stage just now, it was so bright down there, why did I feel as if I were stepping on cotton, one misstep away from falling into a bottomless abyss?"</p>
<p>Her words—"It was so bright down there, yet I felt one misstep away from falling into a bottomless abyss"—left me silent in the cold wind for a long time.</p>
<p>Why, in the middle of a blindingly lit vanity fair, does a person feel perched on the edge of an abyss?</p>
<p>Because that light does not arise from within one's own heart; it is cast by a spotlight held in someone else's hand. While the beam rests on you, you feel like a monarch admired by ten thousand eyes; yet the switch belongs to another, and at the flick of a finger, darkness can swallow you whole in the next second. Placing one's worth, dignity, joy, and sorrow entirely upon external gazes, market price tags, and a fleeting youthful shell—that is what the Sutra calls "sentient beings entangled in karmic obstructions."</p>
<p>And how different is the light of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata! The light of the Medicine Buddha is not an external spotlight that dazzles your eyes for an hour and leaves you in freezing darkness when the show ends. His light is the radiance of pure lapis lazuli—cool, steady, permeating from the marrow of the bones outward, illuminating the darkest chambers of the heart without ever scorching or abandoning a single living soul.</p>
<h4>III. Mañjuśrī's Right Knee on the Ground: Admitting One's Vulnerability Is the Beginning of Healing</h4>
<p>On nights like that, when you return to the opening scene of the Medicine Buddha Sutra and behold Mañjuśrī Bodhisattva rising to make his plea, his humble yet majestic posture strikes the heart with extraordinary force:</p>
<p>"At that time, Mañjuśrī, Prince of the Dharma, receiving the awesome spiritual power of the Buddha, rose from his seat, bared his right shoulder, placed his right knee on the ground, bowed with palms joined toward the Bhagavān, and spoke thus..."</p>
<p>Who is Mañjuśrī? He is the teacher of seven Buddhas, the embodiment of supreme wisdom. In that assembly at Vaiśālī, no one lacked wisdom less or carried fewer obstructions than he. Yet it was precisely Mañjuśrī who rose from his seat, bared his right shoulder, knelt with his right knee pressed against the earth of Vaiśālī fragrant with soil and grass, bowed low with palms joined, and begged the Buddha for a healing Dharma on behalf of all future beings lost in the fog of the Semblance Age.</p>
<p>Why did supreme wisdom itself kneel with right knee on the ground?</p>
<p>Because before suffering and truth, pride is the hardest wall.</p>
<p>In our Semblance Age, people's gravest illness is often not ignorance, but pretending to know, pretending to be invulnerable, pretending not to need help. Before others we hold our spines rigid, wear impeccable masks, and hide our inner panic behind sharp irony, packed schedules, or numb indifference. We are ashamed to admit that we are hurting, ashamed to admit that we are afraid, ashamed to bow our heads in the quiet of the night.</p>
<p>Mañjuśrī's kneeling beneath the Music Tree is a demonstration for every proud and fragile modern soul: true wisdom is never aloof observation from a pedestal; it is the courage to bend down, acknowledge human suffering and limitation, and ask the Buddha to light a lamp for the millions who cannot find their way in the dark.</p>
<h4>IV. The Youth and the Elder: Why Is Mañjuśrī Forever a Youth?</h4>
<p>During the years I flew back and forth between Central in Hong Kong and Sanlitun in Beijing, I often gazed out the cabin window at the sea of clouds on late-night flights.</p>
<p>Once, while rereading the second paragraph of the Medicine Buddha Sutra above the clouds, my eyes paused on the six Chinese characters with which the World-Honored One addressed Mañjuśrī: "Mañjuśrī the Youth" (Mànshūshìlì Tóngzǐ).</p>
<p>A question rose in my mind: Mañjuśrī is the ancient teacher of immeasurable Buddhas; in seniority, wisdom, and acquaintance with the vicissitudes of existence, no sage in the triple world is older. Why, then, does the Buddha address him in the Medicine Buddha Sutra as a Youth—a boy of sixteen?</p>
<p>As the plane descended through the cloud layer, the myriad lights of the city below gleamed like crushed diamonds scattered across black velvet.</p>
<p>I thought of two kinds of people I had met on runways and film sets. One kind, though barely twenty years old, had already learned every worldly calculation, cynicism, indifference, and flattery; their eyes held no spark, their words were watertight yet hollow, like an old cynic steeped in brine for eighty years. The other kind, even after walking through decades of storms and seeing every dark card at the bottom of the vanity fair, retained in their eyes the curiosity, transparency, and unquenchable warmth of a youth.</p>
<p>Just so is the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata seated in the Eastern Pure Lapis Lazuli World: though He has cultivated across countless eons and witnessed the birth and death of beings across ten directions, when you look up at His countenance, the compassion and purity between His brows remain as fresh as the first drop of morning dew sliding off a lotus leaf, unstained by a single speck of worldly cynicism.</p>
<p>The title "Mañjuśrī the Youth" reveals one of the most moving qualities of the Medicine Buddha's teaching: to have seen a thousand sails pass and yet keep an uncorrupted heart. Only one with the deepest wisdom refuses to grow cynical after seeing human frailty; precisely because he sees the illusions and pains of the Semblance Age, he rises like a sincere youth, kneels on the earth, and asks the Buddha to call forth that ageless lapis lazuli light.</p>
<h4>V. Cultivating a Heart That "Listens Attentively and Reflects Well" Amid the Clamor</h4>
<p>At the end of Mañjuśrī's request in Chapter 2, the Buddha speaks a brief injunction that casual readers easily glide over:</p>
<p>"Listen attentively now and reflect well; I shall speak of it for you." (Rǔ jīn dìtīng, jí shàn sīwéi, dāng wèi rǔ shuō.)</p>
<p>After walking through the noisy vanity fairs of Beijing and Hong Kong, whenever I read the words listen attentively (dìtīng) and reflect well (jí shàn sīwéi) in the stillness of night, I recognize them as the very first primer for healing the spiritual maladies of our age.</p>
<p>What does it mean to listen attentively?</p>
<p>It means to listen with truth, undivided focus, and freedom from preconception. Consider how noisy our era is: from the second we open our eyes and reach for a smartphone in the morning, hundreds of short videos, trending gossip, anxiety-peddling headlines, and work notifications chatter endlessly at our ears like a flock of restless sparrows. We receive oceans of data every day, yet our ears have lost the capacity to listen attentively—we fail to hear the compassion behind a quiet warning, the unspoken grievance behind a partner's silence, or the cry for rest from our own exhausted organs.</p>
<p>Before the Dharma seat of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, the greatest art a seeker can learn is to emulate the quiet listening of the Medicine Buddha Himself:</p>
<p>Behold how the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli World-Honored One sits upon His lotus pedestal: His long earlobes hang in tranquil symmetry, His expression still and gentle, as though with a chest as vast as the lapis lazuli sky He is listening to every faint sigh in the mortal world, every groan upon a sickbed, every midnight confession of regret—without impatience, without disdain, without harsh judgment, simply receiving and dissolving all suffering inside His azure radiance.</p>
<p>And to listen attentively the Buddha joins reflect well (jí shàn sīwéi—literally, "reflect in the most wholesome way"). Ordinary minds think unceasingly all day long, yet much of that thinking is unwholesome rumination—replaying an offhand remark ten times to suspect malice, inventing ten catastrophic scenarios before a minor hurdle, or using scriptural rules to judge others rather than examine oneself. Reflecting well means guiding every truth one hears, like spring rain soaking into dry soil, toward compassion, self-reflection, and a luminous way forward.</p>
<h2 class="volume" id="part2">Part II: The Neon Vanity Fair and the Twelve Rays of Lapis Lazuli Light</h2>
<p class="subtitle">(The Main Section I · The First Six Great Vows of the Medicine Tathāgata)</p>
<h3 id="ch3">Chapter 3: The Temple of the Body and Universal Radiance (The First and Second Great Vows: Perfect Marks and a Body Like Lapis Lazuli)</h3>
<h4>Scriptural Text</h4>
<p>The Buddha told Mañjuśrī: "To the east of this world, past Buddha-lands as numerous as the sands of ten Ganges Rivers, there is a world named Pure Lapis Lazuli. In that land there is a Buddha named Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, Arhat, Samyaksaṃbuddha, Perfect in Knowledge and Conduct, Sugata, Knower of the World, Unsurpassed Worthy, Tamer of Passions, Teacher of Devas and Humans, Buddha, Bhagavān. Mañjuśrī! When that World-Honored Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata was formerly practicing the Bodhisattva path, He made twelve great vows so that all sentient beings might obtain whatever they seek.</p>
<p>The First Great Vow: 'I vow that in a future life, when I attain Anuttara-samyak-saṃbodhi, My own body shall blaze with radiant light, illuminating immeasurable, countless, boundless worlds, adorning My body with the thirty-two marks of a great person and the eighty secondary excellences; and I shall cause all sentient beings to become wholly like Me, without any difference.'</p>
<p>The Second Great Vow: 'I vow that in a future life, when I attain Bodhi, My body shall be like lapis lazuli, transparently clear within and without, spotless and free of blemish; My light shall be vast, My merits towering, My body well-abiding in peace, adorned with a blazing net of light surpassing the sun and moon; and beings dwelling in murky darkness shall all be awakened and illuminated, proceeding freely wherever they wish to accomplish their endeavors.'"</p>
<h4>I. The Mercury Mirror and the Blue Lapis Lazuli of Victoria Harbour</h4>
<p>Whenever I close my eyes and contemplate the sacred form of the Eastern Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, a breathtaking vision rises before my mind: in the Eastern Pure Lapis Lazuli World beyond ten Ganges-sands of Buddha-lands, the Medicine Tathāgata does not sit withered inside a gloomy cave. His Dharma body is majestic and upright, endowed with the thirty-two marks of a great person and eighty secondary excellences, His entire form as transparently clear within and without as the purest deep-blue lapis lazuli gem, wreathed in a golden net of flame more resplendent and gentle than sun and moon combined. With this supremely whole and spotless body of light, He tells every traveler trudging through mortal dust: the body is not a despised enemy to be whipped and hated, but a sacred vessel that can be clarified into a temple of lapis lazuli.</p>
<p>Many ascetic traditions speak of the human body—this frame of bone, flesh, skin, and hair—with deep revulsion, calling it a "stinking skin-bag" or a "sack of pus and blood" to be discarded as quickly as possible.</p>
<p>Yet when you open the Medicine Buddha Sutra and read the First and Second Great Vows made by the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata when He walked the Bodhisattva path, you discover that this King of Physicians in the East neither curses nor humiliates the human form. Instead, in the most radiant language in all of Buddhist literature, He makes two vows concerning Form (shēn) and Light (guāng).</p>
<p>During the years I worked in the modeling and film industries, I experienced more acutely than most how deeply human beings wrestle with their physical appearance.</p>
<p>It is an industry that enshrines the physical shell as an idol. In casting rooms and beneath the shadowless lamps of photography studios, facial symmetry, nasal bridge height, jawline angle, and waist-to-leg ratios are measured with unforgiving rulers and lenses. In the autumn of 2014, while shooting the November cover of a campus magazine, softboxes and reflectors surrounded me as the photographer called through the viewfinder: "Turn slightly, chin down, eyes cooler—hold it!"</p>
<p>In the intervals between the clicking shutters, I often wondered: Why have human beings across all ages been so captivated by a comely face and an upright form?</p>
<p>Superficial observers call it vanity. Yet if you look deeper, the longing for beauty and wholeness is in truth a homesickness in the human soul for completeness and light.</p>
<p>The Medicine Buddha understood that homesickness. Therefore, in His First Great Vow, He declares: Not only shall My own body blaze with light and be adorned with the thirty-two marks and eighty excellences, "I shall cause all sentient beings to become wholly like Me, without any difference"—I vow that every living being who feels inferior, timid, or ashamed because of appearance, physique, or vitality shall ultimately share the same dignified, radiant, unblemished wholeness as a Buddha.</p>
<p>That year in Hong Kong, I knew a young female model whom I shall call Ah Lin.</p>
<p>Ah Lin possessed an extraordinary face and had been the favorite of several international beauty campaigns. Then, in a traffic accident, a shard of windshield glass cut her left cheek. Although she underwent meticulous reconstructive surgery, under harsh studio lights or macro lenses a faint, pale-pink line remained near her left cheekbone.</p>
<p>To anyone else, that fine line was barely noticeable—if anything, it added a touch of lived depth to her cool features. Yet in Ah Lin's own eyes, that tiny scar was a bottomless chasm splitting her life in two. She began to fear bright light, feared anyone standing on her left side, and habitually let her long hair hang down to cover half her face. She turned down every casting and locked herself inside her Kowloon Tong apartment with heavy blackout curtains drawn, staring into a ten-times magnifying mirror for hours and weeping over that faint line.</p>
<p>One late afternoon, a few friends met for tea in Tsim Sha Tsui, and Ah Lin arrived wearing oversized sunglasses and a baseball cap. Throughout the gathering she kept her head lowered, stirring her ice water with a straw in silence. Afterward, I walked her to the Star Ferry pier.</p>
<p>Victoria Harbour at dusk was entering its gentlest hour. As the sun sank behind Lantau Island, the sky shifted from burnished gold to a deep, translucent indigo-blue; the water rippled with light as the green-and-white Star Ferry glided toward the pier.</p>
<p>Standing by the gangway, the sea breeze lifted the long hair covering Ah Lin's left cheek. Like a startled bird, she reached up to press her hair back down.</p>
<p>"Ah Lin," I paused and looked at her gently, "take off your sunglasses and look at the sea."</p>
<p>She hesitated, her hand frozen mid-air, and slowly removed her sunglasses.</p>
<p>"Look at the ripples on the water," I said, pointing to the undulating harbor below. "A speedboat just cut across the bay and left a deep white wake across the surface. If this harbor were a dead glass mirror backed with mercury, that scratch would stay on it forever and the mirror would be ruined. But it isn't a mercury mirror—it is the living, translucent sea. So long as the water doesn't freeze itself into ice, the sunlight shines straight into its depths, and the whole harbor glows like living lapis lazuli. Who looking at the ocean cares whether one wavelet is an inch higher or one ripple curves slightly to the left?"</p>
<p>Watching the broken gold and deep blue shimmering across the water, Ah Lin felt her eyes grow moist.</p>
<p>"What the fashion world calls flawlessness on a runway or a retouched cover is often the image inside a mercury mirror," I said quietly over the sound of the waves and the ferry horn. "A glass mirror reflects skin only because its back is coated with a thick, opaque layer of dark lead. The perfection people chase in the vanity fair is often built the same way—dazzling on the surface, yet coated behind with dark lead made of anxiety, pride, comparison, and the terror of aging. The moment a tiny scratch appears on the glass, their whole world shatters.</p>
<p>"Yet the dignity spoken of in the Medicine Buddha Sutra is not a mercury mirror; it is lapis lazuli (liúlí). In the First Great Vow, the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata vows that all beings shall share His radiant marks; and immediately in the Second Great Vow, He reveals where such radiance comes from: 'My body shall be like lapis lazuli, transparently clear within and without, spotless and free of blemish.' What does transparently clear within and without mean? It means there is no opaque lead hidden behind your face—no self-hatred, no scheming, no shame locking out the light. When a person's heart is clean, open, and compassionate, refusing to lock herself in a dark room over a scar, a gray hair, or a stranger's remark, the azure light of the Medicine Buddha shines straight out from her bones and her eyes."</p>
<p>During the fifteen-minute Star Ferry crossing of Victoria Harbour that evening, for the first time since her accident Ah Lin did not lift her hand to hide her left cheek. She tucked her hair behind her ear, let the sea breeze brush across that faint mark, and smiled into the last glow of sunset. At that moment, she was more moving than in any airbrushed campaign she had ever shot.</p>
<h4>II. The Cage of Size Ten and a Rainy Night in Causeway Bay</h4>
<p>If the First Great Vow grants every ordinary person the foundational dignity of knowing our nature is no different from the Buddha's, the Second Great Vow—"My body shall be like lapis lazuli, transparently clear within and without... My body well-abiding in peace"—teaches us how to step out of the prison of worldly judgment and self-punishment.</p>
<p>In 2014, while working with Style International Management in Hong Kong, I lived in an apartment on Hong Kong Island and rode the tram or MTR every day with my black portfolio bag from casting to casting.</p>
<p>In the fashion industry at that time, I witnessed an invisible pressure that could be called "the tribunal of the tape measure and the lens."</p>
<p>Once, in the backstage dressing room of a group editorial shoot, I met a seventeen-year-old male model from Eastern Europe whom I shall call Igor, along with a young visiting actress whom I shall call Ah Wei.</p>
<p>On screen, Ah Wei was known for her bright, effortless charm. Yet beneath the unforgiving ring-light of the dressing room, I saw her push away a bowl of warm oatmeal in distress. Her collarbones already jutted out sharply and her wrists looked fragile as twigs, yet she stared at the monitor playback and pleaded with the stylist to pull more hair over her cheeks and darken the contour shading because she feared her face looked swollen on camera.</p>
<p>Beside her, Igor—a seventeen-year-old youth with sculpted features—was trying on an ultra-narrow sample leather jacket. Because his shoulders had broadened by two centimeters during a teenage growth spurt, the zipper caught at his chest. A wardrobe assistant muttered offhandedly, "You've gotten broader than last month; if the sample doesn't zip, we'll have to swap someone else in."</p>
<p>Igor said nothing. He took off the jacket and slipped out through the fire door into the concrete stairwell at the end of the corridor. When I walked past the stairwell a few minutes later, through the half-open fire door I saw the 189-centimeter teenager sitting on the steps, striking his own collarbone and shoulders with his fist while tears dropped onto his sneakers, cursing his own growing bones in his native tongue.</p>
<p>That sight pierced my heart.</p>
<p>I pushed open the fire door, sat down beside the boy on the concrete step, placed a hand on his broad shoulder, and said to him gently in English: "Hey, brother, there is nothing wrong with your bones. The only thing wrong is that the leather jacket is too small."</p>
<p>He lifted his tear-stained face and looked at me in surprise.</p>
<p>"When a young pine tree grows tall and strong," I said, pointing through the stairwell window toward the blue sky and mountain ridge above Hong Kong, "it doesn't blame its branches for outgrowing last year's flowerpot. The pot is simply too small—go grow in the open earth."</p>
<p>When I left the studio that evening, a torrential rainstorm was pouring over Causeway Bay. Red and green neon reflections dissolved across the wet asphalt like spilled oil paint. Walking beneath a black umbrella through the rain, I turned the words of the Medicine Buddha's First and Second Great Vows over and over in my mind:</p>
<p>"My body shall be like lapis lazuli, transparently clear within and without, spotless and free of blemish; My light shall be vast, My merits towering, My body well-abiding in peace, adorned with a blazing net of light surpassing the sun and moon; and beings dwelling in murky darkness shall all be awakened and illuminated, proceeding freely wherever they wish to accomplish their endeavors."</p>
<p>Suddenly I understood why, right after saying "My body shall be like lapis lazuli, transparently clear within and without," the Medicine Buddha adds the four Chinese characters "shēn shàn ānzhù"—"My body well-abiding in peace"!</p>
<p>A body well-abiding in peace!</p>
<p>Those words are the supreme prescription for every modern anxiety over appearance, physique, age, and status.</p>
<p>In this world, countless people possess enviable beauty, wealth, or fame, yet their bodies and minds have never "abided in peace" for a single day. They inhabit their own bodies as though living inside a condemned building that might collapse at any moment, or wearing a stolen robe that could be stripped away tomorrow. When young, they fear not being striking enough; when striking, they fear growing old; when thin, they fear lacking presence; when strong, they fear not fitting into a sample jacket. They have never truly accepted their own living frame, never once said to themselves: "I dwell quietly, steadily, and gratefully inside this body, at peace with its seasons."</p>
<p>When the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata shines His azure light upon you, He teaches you to stop treating your body as a commodity for buying approval, and instead to cherish it as a lapis lazuli vessel carrying awareness and kindness. Then tall is dignified, and short is dignified; the clear vigor of youth is dignified, the seasoned steadiness of middle age is dignified, and the silver at the temples of old age is likewise one of the eighty secondary excellences carved by time.</p>
<h4>III. Lapis Lazuli and the Blazing Flame-Net: When Your Heart Hides No Filth, the Night Makes Way for You</h4>
<p>In the Second Great Vow, there is another wondrous pairing of images: the union of Lapis Lazuli (liúlí) and the Blazing Net of Flame (yànwǎng)—"My body shall be like lapis lazuli, transparently clear within and without... My body well-abiding in peace, adorned with a blazing net of light surpassing the sun and moon."</p>
<p>Many readers pause here in wonder: Lapis lazuli is cool, translucent, and deep blue as the ocean; a blazing flame-net is warm, vibrant, and golden as fire. How can supreme coolness (lapis lazuli) and supreme warmth (the flame-net) appear simultaneously upon the same Dharma body of the Medicine Buddha, and even "surpass the sun and moon"?</p>
<p>As I studied the Sutra and walked through the years, I came to see the profound truth of this union.</p>
<p>The sun appears only by day, and its glare can be so fierce that no eye dares look directly at it; the moon appears only by night, and its light is cold, unable to warm a freezing traveler or reach into shaded corners beneath the eaves. In the world, some who cultivate themselves become like a "cold moon"—pure and unstained, yet icy and detached from human suffering, so that anyone approaching them feels a chill; others act like a "scorching sun"—full of passionate zeal, yet because their inner mind is not transparent and pure, ego and attachment mingle with their enthusiasm, eventually burning both themselves and those around them.</p>
<p>Only the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata has lapis lazuli as His essence—utterly translucent, selfless, and unstained, harboring no calculation, resentment, or grasping (transparently clear within and without, spotless and free of blemish); and precisely because His inner essence is as clear as pure crystal and deep-sea lapis, when the fire of His great compassionate vows shines through that translucent body, it refracts into a warm, magnificent net of golden flame (yànwǎng zhuāngyán)!</p>
<p>It is a radiance that combines the cool serenity of lapis lazuli wisdom with the warm fire of active compassion. It never blinds the eyes, yet it penetrates the hidden chambers of despair that neither sun nor moon can reach (surpassing the sun and moon; beings dwelling in murky darkness are all awakened and illuminated).</p>
<p>Years later, when I began writing under the pen name "Yancan" (Yán Càn, literally "Blazing Radiance") and in 2026 founded my cultural brand of the same name, readers sometimes asked me: "Why is your pen name 'Yancan'—a name of fire and brightness—when you write so devotedly of the cool lapis lazuli light of the Medicine Buddha Sutra? Do fire and lapis lazuli not conflict?"</p>
<p>Whenever I hear that question, I smile and point to the Second Great Vow of the Medicine Buddha.</p>
<p>"They do not conflict at all," I reply. "'Yancan' comes straight from 'adorned with a blazing net of light' (yànwǎng zhuāngyán) in the Second Great Vow, while its foundation is the 'pure lapis lazuli' (jìng liúlí) of my natal day on the thirtieth of the ninth lunar month. To walk well in this mortal world, your inner heart must be like lapis lazuli—clean, transparent, harboring no hidden malice or grudge; yet when you meet living beings and relieve their suffering, you cannot be a block of cold ice—you must be a warm flame and a bright lamp. Lapis lazuli purity within, blazing compassionate light without: that is the posture the Medicine Buddha teaches us for walking through the human world."</p>
<h4>IV. The Unpainted Face After Midnight and the Inextinguishable Light</h4>
<p>One winter night during my year in Hong Kong, we wrapped a fourteen-hour magazine shoot at two o'clock in the morning.</p>
<p>One by one the high-output studio lamps were switched off, and the set that had blazed like noon moments earlier reverted to bare wooden scaffolding in the gloom. Sitting before the dressing mirror, I used cotton pads soaked in cleansing oil to wipe away layers of foundation, contour, and hairspray. After washing my face with warm water, I looked up under the plain incandescent bulb of the washroom at a face that was tired, yet real enough to show every pore and fine line.</p>
<p>An unexpected wave of relief washed over me.</p>
<p>For fourteen hours under the lights, the figure in the lens had looked chiseled as marble, yet it was a tense image holding its breath; only now, washed clean of paint and dressed in a loose cotton shirt, had I returned inside my own living body.</p>
<p>From the original scriptural meaning, the First Great Vow describes the fruit-ground majesty of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata upon attaining Buddhahood—illuminating boundless worlds with thirty-two marks and eighty excellences, and vowing that all sentient beings shall attain the exact same Buddhahood (causing all sentient beings to become wholly like Me, without any difference); the Second Great Vow reveals the translucent, illuminating nature of that Dharma body.</p>
<p>When we bring these two vows into our present-day cultivation of mind, they answer the question that troubles so many modern souls: How should we regard our outward appearance and our body-mind in this world?</p>
<p>People easily fall into one of two extremes regarding form: either clinging to form—tying all security to youth, cosmetics, luxury labels, and praise, terrified of a single wrinkle; or neglecting form—treating the body with slovenly self-abandonment under the excuse that physical form is impermanent.</p>
<p>The Medicine Buddha teaches neither anxious clinging nor harsh neglect, but cultivating a dignified presence through a lapis lazuli heart. Why does lapis lazuli glow? Because it neither blocks the light nor hoards it. A lump of mud or iron stops the light dead and casts a dark shadow behind it; a piece of pure lapis lazuli lets light pass straight through, so that even its reflection on the ground is a pool of soft azure brightness. When we cease coating our hearts with envy, calculation, and vanity, and instead maintain clean daily habits, upright bearing, and clear eyes, our presence carries an unshakeable dignity that time cannot dim.</p>
<h4>V. "Proceeding Freely to Accomplish Their Endeavors": The Otherworldly Lapis Heart and Worldly Action</h4>
<p>At the close of the Second Great Vow stand two phrases that often surprise first-time readers of the Medicine Buddha Sutra:</p>
<p>"Beings dwelling in murky darkness shall all be awakened and illuminated, proceeding freely wherever they wish to accomplish their endeavors." (Yōumíng zhòngshēng, xī méng kāixiǎo, suíyì suǒ qù, zuò zhū shìyè.)</p>
<p>Many who hold stereotypes about Buddhism assume that spiritual cultivation means abandoning worldly aspirations, quitting one's profession, and retreating into passive withdrawal.</p>
<p>Yet look at what the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata vows: with His vast lapis lazuli radiance surpassing sun and moon, He illuminates beings trapped in murky confusion (beings dwelling in murky darkness shall all be awakened and illuminated); and once the darkness in their hearts has been dispelled and the fog before their eyes cleared, what does the Medicine Buddha empower them to do?</p>
<p>"Proceeding freely wherever they wish to accomplish their endeavors!"</p>
<p>Following your wholesome aspiration and calling, go forth gladly and uprightly into the human world to build your work, fulfill your responsibilities, and exercise your gifts!</p>
<p>Seekers of truth have often swung between two extremes: either "otherworldly coldness" that shuns all human work as defilement, or "worldly grasping" that loses its soul among ledgers, fame, and rivalry. The Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata reveals the middle way that unifies both: He is at once the Supreme Physician seated upon the Pure Lapis Lazuli throne, transparently clear within and without, and the compassionate guide holding a lamp at every mortal crossing to support living beings as they "accomplish their endeavors."</p>
<p>When a person's heart is still locked in "murky darkness" (yōumíng), his work is driven by greed, fear, and rivalry—every day is spent in anxious calculation. When his heart has been illuminated by the Medicine Buddha's lapis lazuli light (xī méng kāixiǎo), understanding interconnectedness and compassion, the work he does in the world ceases to be a desperate attempt to fill an inner void and becomes a joyful offering. If you are a physician, your clinic is your lapis lazuli practice hall; if you are an actor, your lens is a mirror reflecting human compassion; if you are an artisan, a teacher, an entrepreneur, or a parent, every honest task done with a clean heart is "accomplishing your endeavors" beneath the azure radiance of Bhaiṣajyaguru.</p>
<h3 id="ch4">Chapter 4: Inexhaustible Provisions and the Cedar Fragrance of the Underground Shelter (The Third and Fourth Great Vows: Freedom from Scarcity and Establishment in the Great Vehicle)</h3>
<h4>Scriptural Text</h4>
<p>"The Third Great Vow: 'I vow that in a future life, when I attain Bodhi, by means of immeasurable, boundless wisdom and skillful means, I shall cause all sentient beings to obtain an inexhaustible supply of the things they need for life, never allowing any living being to suffer from scarcity or lack.'</p>
<p>The Fourth Great Vow: 'I vow that in a future life, when I attain Bodhi, if there are sentient beings who walk upon deviant paths, I shall cause them all to be securely established within the path of Bodhi; and if there are those who practice the Śrāvaka or Pratyekabuddha vehicles, I shall establish them all within the Great Vehicle (Mahāyāna).'"</p>
<h4>I. Twenty Concrete Steps Beneath the Earth in Underground Fragrance</h4>
<p>From late 2014 into 2015, I stepped away from glittering fashion runways and entered a world that could not have been more different.</p>
<p>It was the film set of Underground Fragrance (Dìxià Xiāng), directed by the young filmmaker Peng Fei, in which I played the male lead, a young man named Yong Le.</p>
<p>To inhabit Yong Le, I took off tailored runway suits and put on a faded jacket dusted with sawdust and plaster, descending every morning with the crew down twenty steep concrete steps through an iron door into Beijing's subterranean air-raid shelters and basement tenements for fourteen hours at a stretch.</p>
<p>If you have never spent a winter inside an underground tenement in the north, it is hard to imagine that bone-deep dampness and compression. Once you walk down those twenty steps, daylight vanishes completely. Dim energy-saving bulbs hang every few meters from low ceilings crisscrossed by thick heating pipes and tangled wires; the air carries a permanent mixture of cooking oil smoke, cheap detergent, damp cement, and mildew. Inside tiny cubicles partitioned by thin plasterboard live the young men and women who polish the city's shop windows above ground, dance in nightclubs, deliver takeout meals, and haul old furniture.</p>
<p>In the film, Yong Le drives an old truck through demolished hutongs and high-rise neighborhoods collecting discarded furniture, carrying broken wardrobes and scarred wooden tables back to his underground storeroom to plane, glue, sand, and varnish them for resale to equally strapped tenants. Yong Le has a simple, distant dream: he wants to save enough money to move out of the sunless basement and buy a room above ground where sunlight touches the floor.</p>
<p>During those weeks of filming, I often lingered in the underground warehouse after wrap, sitting quietly among stacked camphor-wood chests and old elm tables, running my fingers over wood grain weathered by decades of family life.</p>
<p>I watched the real residents who lived in those subterranean corridors: a young couple from rural Hebei who rose before dawn every day to sell savory crepes at the morning market and returned at night to scrub greasy iron buckets under an icy tap in the communal washroom—the wife's frost-reddened fingers split with small cuts, yet smiling like a child when she counted thirty extra yuan earned that day; and an elderly carpenter in his fifties who could not afford specialist eye surgery, peering through reading glasses taped together at the bridge as he planed wood beneath a bare bulb.</p>
<p>Sitting inside that furniture-stacked basement, gazing in my mind's eye toward the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata—who holds His warm medicine bowl in the Eastern Pure Lapis Lazuli World—I understood for the very first time the blood-warm compassion of the Third and Fourth Great Vows.</p>
<p>Read the Third Great Vow closely:</p>
<p>"I vow that in a future life, when I attain Bodhi, by means of immeasurable, boundless wisdom and skillful means, I shall cause all sentient beings to obtain an inexhaustible supply of the things they need for life, never allowing any living being to suffer from scarcity or lack."</p>
<p>In the entire canon of world scriptures, there is scarcely a sentence with more human warmth or less pretension.</p>
<p>Throughout history, countless moralists perched comfortably in ivory towers, well-fed and warmly clothed themselves, have loved to lecture the poor from on high: "Do not care about money; poverty is a blessing for spiritual practice; material goods are fleeting clouds." Such talk sounds lofty, yet it is icy to the core—because those speakers have never scrubbed a frozen iron bucket under a winter tap, never lain awake at midnight wondering how to pay next month's basement rent, never felt the shattering of human dignity when a parent falls ill or a child cries from hunger and one's pocket holds only a few coins.</p>
<p>The Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata never speaks such heartless words.</p>
<p>The Medicine Buddha knows that human beings in the desire realm are made of flesh and blood, and that food, clothing, shelter, and medicine are the foundation upon which life stands. "Never allowing any living being to suffer from scarcity or lack" (mò lìng zhòngshēng, yǒu suǒ fáshǎo)—within those words lies the Buddha's deepest empathy for mortal struggle. When a person is shivering in terror over tomorrow's meal or a roof to keep out the rain, his heart contracts into a tight knot; in extreme scarcity and survival panic, resentment, humiliation, and desperate wrongdoing easily take root.</p>
<p>Yet look carefully at the first half of the Third Great Vow—by what means does the Medicine Buddha enable sentient beings to obtain an "inexhaustible supply of the things they need for life"?</p>
<p>The Sutra states explicitly: "By means of immeasurable, boundless wisdom and skillful means" (yǐ wúliàng wúbiān zhìhuì fāngbiàn)!</p>
<p>It is not by raining gold bars from the sky to reward idleness or feed greed, but by awakening immeasurable wisdom and skillful means!</p>
<p>Scarcity has two faces: one is the actual shortage of material necessities; the other is the constriction of wisdom and vision. When a heart is locked in fear and narrowness, it either misses avenues of honest livelihood or, even after accumulating millions, remains haunted by the terror of "not enough." The Medicine Buddha's Third Great Vow both blesses honest human labor with abundant provision and opens our cramped vision through wisdom and skillful means—just as Yong Le, in the darkest hour of Underground Fragrance, hears the pulse of resilience beneath the earth and smells the enduring fragrance of aged wood in the dark.</p>
<p>In September 2015, when our Underground Fragrance crew walked onto the red carpet of the "Venice Days" (Giornate degli Autori) section at the 72nd Venice International Film Festival, where the film won the FEDEORA Award for Best Film from the Federation of Film Critics of Europe and the Mediterranean, and shortly thereafter won the Gold Hugo in the New Directors Competition at the 51st Chicago International Film Festival, I stood on an ancient stone bridge in Venice beneath the brilliant Adriatic sun, breathing the salt breeze and remembering that damp underground room in Beijing a few months earlier.</p>
<p>From a windowless basement twenty steps underground to the sunlit waters of the Lido di Venezia—what bridges those two worlds is precisely the progression from the Third Great Vow to the Fourth Great Vow:</p>
<p>"If there are sentient beings who walk upon deviant paths, I shall cause them all to be securely established within the path of Bodhi; and if there are those who practice the Śrāvaka or Pratyekabuddha vehicles, I shall establish them all within the Great Vehicle (Mahāyāna)."</p>
<p>Why does the Fourth Great Vow follow immediately upon the Third Great Vow's promise of material sufficiency?</p>
<p>Because when a person has not yet solved basic survival (the Third Vow), it is hard to speak to him of vast spiritual horizons; yet the moment a person finally climbs out of the basement, puts on fine clothes, and acquires wealth and recognition, he immediately faces a second trap. Either his desires swell and drive him onto "deviant paths" (xiédào) of harming others for profit, or he locks his door to guard his private comfort and quietude, becoming an exquisite egoist—or, in spiritual terms, a self-enclosed practitioner who cares only for his own peace while remaining indifferent to the suffering world (Śrāvaka and Pratyekabuddha vehicles).</p>
<p>When a person who has acquired abundant money, gardens, and status lives solely for his own pleasure and winning, his soul withers like a tree severed from its roots, decaying day by day amid luxury until he hears a quiet weeping inside his own chest. At that threshold, the warm, spring-water gaze of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata shines through the eaves—the ambrosia in His medicine bowl not only cures the hunger of the poor, but waters the parched soul of the prosperous, calling him out of his narrow room into the Great Vehicle (Mahāyāna).</p>
<p>Later, after I founded Laiya Cultural Media in Harbin, co-founded Bawan Culture in Shenzhen, and in 2026 established the "Yancan" cultural brand while devoting myself to writing books, friends sometimes asked me: "You played the lead in a Venice award-winning film and opened major international runway shows—why do you now prefer sitting quietly at a desk writing books on classical scriptures and helping ordinary people untangle their worries?"</p>
<p>Whenever I hear that question, I remember the damp scent of camphor wood in that Beijing basement and the Third and Fourth Great Vows standing side by side in the Medicine Buddha Sutra.</p>
<p>"Because I have spent time in that basement," I answer gently. "I know how cold it is when there is no light, and I know that when a person finally steps up onto the sunlit ground, the best thing he can do is not bolt the iron door behind him, but hand a lighted lamp down the concrete steps and say to those still below: Do not be afraid. Even in the dark, old wood keeps its fragrance, and up the steps lies a clear sky."</p>
<h4>II. The Secret at the Bottom of the Old Camphor Chest and the Truth of "Inexhaustible Provisions"</h4>
<p>Let us return for a moment to that underground furniture storeroom during the filming of Underground Fragrance in 2015.</p>
<p>For the set, our art department had gathered hundreds of pieces of genuine old furniture from demolished Beijing alleyways and secondhand markets—chests of drawers with flaking lacquer, eight-immortals tables missing a corner wedge, dressing mirrors mottled with black oxidation spots, and a heavy old camphor-wood trunk.</p>
<p>One afternoon before the cameras rolled, I was sitting alone among the props to find the rhythm of my character. I lifted the lid of that heavy camphor-wood trunk, and a rich, slightly pungent aroma of aged camphor rose into the damp air.</p>
<p>Beneath a layer of yellowed, crumbling newspaper lining the bottom of the trunk, my fingers touched a small packet wrapped in faded red cloth, evidently left behind in a crevice by the trunk's former owner decades ago. I unfolded the red cloth carefully. Inside lay no gold or jewels—only an old grain-ration coupon, a small jade peace-buckle, and a slip of paper written neatly in fountain pen: "May our whole family be safe and sound, with padded coats in winter and hot rice in the pot."</p>
<p>Holding that thin slip of paper beneath the dim basement bulb, I sat still for a long time.</p>
<p>"With padded coats in winter and hot rice in the pot."</p>
<p>How intimately those eight words echo the vow made two thousand five hundred years ago by the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata: "I shall cause all sentient beings to obtain an inexhaustible supply of the things they need for life, never allowing any living being to suffer from scarcity or lack."</p>
<p>Just as a traveler steps out of the mountain forest to learn bookkeeping, trade, and the real weight of grain and salt in the marketplace, after walking through the ups and downs of running enterprises myself and looking up at the compassionate countenance of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, I gained an understanding of the Third Great Vow a hundred times deeper than any armchair reading could provide.</p>
<p>In business, I have seen two opposite kinds of people seeking wealth.</p>
<p>The first kind treats wealth as loot to be seized through speculation, shortchanging partners, delaying workers' wages, or burning thick incense sticks to bribe deities. Such people violate the prerequisite of the Third Great Vow: "by means of immeasurable, boundless wisdom and skillful means."</p>
<p>What are wisdom and skillful means?</p>
<p>Wisdom (zhìhuì) is seeing clearly the laws of cause, effect, and timing (what the Book of Changes calls "knowing advance and retreat, survival and ruin, without losing one's uprightness"), recognizing that all enduring prosperity is the natural return of the value and relief you bring to the world. Skillful means (fāngbiàn) is conducting one's work with empathy, integrity, and mutual benefit, so that every customer, colleague, and partner touched by your endeavor finds peace and fair reward.</p>
<p>When a person possesses genuine wisdom and skillful means, he will never be cornered into helplessness. Even if placed in a basement stacked with broken furniture, he will plane warm fragrance out of old timber and craft vessels that comfort human homes. That is the true meaning of "inexhaustible provisions"—not a static string of digits in a bank account, but an unfailing spring of creative wisdom and merit within the heart.</p>
<h4>III. Stepping Out of the "Self-Preserving Island" into the "Great Vehicle of All Beings"</h4>
<p>Over the years, friends reading the Fourth Great Vow have often asked me:</p>
<p>"Wenjie, the Fourth Great Vow says: 'If there are sentient beings who walk upon deviant paths, I shall cause them all to be securely established within the path of Bodhi; and if there are those who practice the Śrāvaka or Pratyekabuddha vehicles, I shall establish them all within the Great Vehicle.' Guiding people away from deviant paths makes immediate sense; but aren't Śrāvakas and Pratyekabuddhas upright practitioners who keep themselves pure, restrain their desires, and harm no one? Why does the Medicine Buddha also guide them out of their solitary vehicle into the Great Vehicle?"</p>
<p>Whenever someone asks this, I remember an encounter during a film shoot on the western Sichuan plateau.</p>
<p>During a break in filming at the foot of a three-thousand-meter snow peak, someone mentioned that halfway up a forested slope lived a recluse who had retreated from a major city years earlier to practice quiet contemplation and study classical texts. Having a free afternoon, I walked up the mountain trail to pay a visit.</p>
<p>His timber cabin was indeed refined: bamboo fencing, mountain spring water channeled through the courtyard, a spotless room with a zither and sutra scrolls on the desk. Over tea, he spoke eloquently of emptiness and non-attachment; yet the moment the conversation touched upon the world below the mountain, his brows furrowed with undisguised disdain: "People down in the world are too foolish, muddy, and greedy. Speaking an extra sentence to them stains my pure cultivation. So long as I guard this courtyard and perfect my own tranquil mind, what do the troubles of the world have to do with me?"</p>
<p>Just then, a herdsman came running up the trail carrying his ten-year-old son on his back; the boy had been bitten on the ankle by a venomous pit viper while gathering firewood, his face already turning pale and bluish, and the father knocked urgently at the cabin gate hoping for first-aid herbs or help getting down the mountain.</p>
<p>To my astonishment, the recluse who had been discoursing on "emptiness" and "purity" darkened his face and waved through the doorway for them to hurry on to the town clinic by themselves, anxious lest the commotion disturb his quiet courtyard.</p>
<p>I did not say another word to the recluse. I stood up immediately, stepped outside, helped the father bind the boy's leg above the bite with a cord to slow the venom's spread, and took turns with the father carrying the child on our backs down the steep mountain trail to the highway, where our film crew's off-road vehicle rushed him to the county hospital for antivenom serum and saved his life.</p>
<p>That night, returning to our lodging with yellow mud caked on my trouser legs, I washed away the sweat, looked out at the cold, brilliant stars above the plateau, and understood the Medicine Buddha's Fourth Great Vow to its depths.</p>
<p>Why does the sacred image of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata move the human heart to tears far more than any aloof hermit? Because the Medicine Buddha is like a vast, boundless river that never refuses a single passenger. Whether noble king, wealthy merchant, mud-stained beggar, or weeping child, the Medicine Tathāgata lifts them all with the same translucent, warm lapis lazuli light, filling His inexhaustible medicine bowl with ambrosia to ferry them from the shore of suffering to the shore of peace.</p>
<p>If a person's cultivation serves only to turn himself into a spotless, cold-blooded island—reading ten thousand scrolls while covering his ears when a neighbor's child cries for help—such "purity" is colder than mortal mud. The Medicine Buddha's Fourth Great Vow gently shatters that shell of solitary self-preservation, reminding us that true lapis lazuli never fears touching mortal soil, and true awakening reaches the ocean only inside the Great Vehicle (Mahāyāna) that shares the joys and pains of all living beings.</p>
<h4>IV. The Waves of the Lido in Venice and the Echo of Old Wood</h4>
<p>In early September 2015 on the Lido di Venezia, the air carried the warm salt scent of the Adriatic Sea mingled with the roasted aroma of espresso.</p>
<p>When the 72nd Venice International Film Festival's "Venice Days" (Giornate degli Autori) section announced that Underground Fragrance had won the FEDEORA Award for Best Film, sustained applause filled the hall. Dressed in a black evening suit beneath the spotlights, surrounded by our embracing director and cast, I looked out at filmmakers and journalists from around the world.</p>
<p>The celebratory reception lasted late into the night.</p>
<p>Around one o'clock in the morning, I slipped quietly away from the noisy terrace, took off my dress shoes, and walked barefoot onto the quiet sand of the Lido beach.</p>
<p>The Adriatic at night looked like an immense bolt of dark-blue silk. Across the lagoon, the lights of Venice shimmered softly as waves rolled in one after another, washing coolly over my insteps before receding with the fine sand.</p>
<p>Standing in the shallow water, I looked down at my bare feet.</p>
<p>Less than a year earlier, those same feet had stood twenty steps below Beijing's streets on damp concrete, wearing dust-speckled rubber shoes inside the sunless furniture warehouse of Underground Fragrance. Next door in that basement had lived the delivery rider saving every coin for a home, the dancer pressing a hot-water bottle to her bruised knees after night shifts, and the old carpenter planing timber through taped glasses.</p>
<p>"Yong Le," I murmured my character's name quietly toward the Adriatic waves.</p>
<p>At that moment I felt no intoxication or pride at standing at an international film festival—only an unprecedented clarity and humility. Looking up at the night sky over the Adriatic, deep blue as polished lapis lazuli, I seemed to behold the all-seeing eyes of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata gazing quietly down upon the sea. Beneath the Buddha's lapis lazuli light, human gold, red carpets, and applause are merely brief foam on the surface of the water; what truly buoys the vessel of life, what allows a film to touch audiences ten thousand miles away, is the unextinguished kindness of ordinary people amid scarcity, and the human compassion sheltered by the Medicine Buddha's Third Great Vow—"with padded coats in winter and hot rice in the pot."</p>
<h4>V. "Wisdom and Skillful Means" and "Establishment in the Great Vehicle": Witnessing an Unfailing Spring in Giving</h4>
<p>When we contemplate the Third Great Vow ("by means of immeasurable, boundless wisdom and skillful means, causing all sentient beings to obtain an inexhaustible supply of the things they need for life") together with the Fourth Great Vow ("establishing them all within the Great Vehicle"), a law of abundance spanning both worldly and spiritual life reveals itself.</p>
<p>During my early years transitioning from actor and model to founder of cultural enterprises between Harbin and Shenzhen, I faced the practical trials familiar to every entrepreneur: delayed project payments, monthly payroll and office rent for a team of twenty young colleagues, and tough contract negotiations. At times, looking at tight cash-flow sheets late at night, I too tasted the anxiety of scarcity.</p>
<p>One weekend during that season, I visited an elder lay practitioner in Shenzhen who had built a manufacturing enterprise from nothing over thirty years while quietly funding rural schools.</p>
<p>Over tea I asked him: "Senior, you have weathered several major economic storms over three decades. How have you managed not only to survive every downturn, but to find your path growing wider the colder the industry winter becomes?"</p>
<p>The elder poured me a cup of hot tea and pointed to a calligraphy scroll hanging on his wall, which distilled the essence of the Third and Fourth Great Vows into eight characters: "Wisdom Generates the Inexhaustible; the Heart Abides in the Great Vehicle."</p>
<p>"Wenjie," he said gently, "when I was young, I too thought that avoiding scarcity meant clutching my pocket tightly and crowding out competitors. Only after understanding the Third and Fourth Great Vows of the Medicine Buddha Sutra did I realize why people's provisions dry up: because they stay trapped in the 'Small Vehicle of self-preservation' or the 'deviant path of harming others.' Think of a water well. If you fear villagers will draw all your water and you seal the mouth of the well with stones, within half a year the underground spring will silt up and die for lack of flow. Conversely, if you leave the well open so every thirsty traveler can draw water, the more water is drawn out, the faster and clearer the living spring surges up from deep underground!"</p>
<p>That parable of the sealed well and the living spring illuminated the two vows for me in a flash.</p>
<p>Where lies the underground spring of the Third Great Vow's "inexhaustible provisions"? Right inside the Fourth Great Vow's "establishing them all within the Great Vehicle"!</p>
<p>When your heart expands from a stagnant puddle of self-interest into a Great Vehicle river that seeks to solve real problems for partners, bring genuine consolation to readers, and secure a dignified livelihood for colleagues, the living waters of heaven and earth naturally converge toward your channel.</p>
<h3 id="ch5">Chapter 5: The Mended Precept Vessel and the Compassion of Complete Faculties (The Fifth and Sixth Great Vows: Pure Conduct and Wholeness of Body and Mind)</h3>
<h4>Scriptural Text</h4>
<p>"The Fifth Great Vow: 'I vow that in a future life, when I attain Bodhi, if there are immeasurable, boundless sentient beings who cultivate pure conduct within My Dharma, I shall cause them all to obtain unblemished precepts and be endowed with the threefold pure precepts; and even if they should commit a transgression or violation, upon hearing My name they shall regain their purity and not fall into evil destinies.'</p>
<p>The Sixth Great Vow: 'I vow that in a future life, when I attain Bodhi, if there are sentient beings whose bodies are inferior, whose faculties are incomplete, who are ugly, dull, or foolish, blind, deaf, or mute, crippled, hunchbacked, afflicted with leprosy, madness, or all manner of painful illnesses—upon hearing My name, they shall all obtain upright appearance and keen wisdom, with all their faculties complete and free from every sickness and affliction.'"</p>
<h4>I. The Scent of Alcohol on a Rainy Night and the River That Washes Mud-Stained Robes</h4>
<p>In this human life, the hardest person to forgive is often not an enemy who wronged us, but the version of ourselves who once stumbled, broke a vow, and ruined something precious.</p>
<p>On the long road of life, few scenes are more heart-wrenching than that of a person who once cherished noble ideals, yet after slipping into the mire of vanity, gambling, or a moment's folly, flees alone to a quiet riverbank at dawn. Looking down into the water at a face swollen, exhausted, and etched with remorse, he feels an unbearable shame. He believes he is permanently soiled, like a white porcelain vessel shattered in mud, no longer worthy to lift his eyes toward heaven or the Buddhas.</p>
<p>Yet just as he closes his eyes in despair, from the eastern horizon a deep, cool ray of pure lapis lazuli light spreads gently across the surface of the river. The Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata sits within that azure radiance; the medicine bowl in His left palm tilts ever so slightly, and a single crystal drop of ambrosia falls into the turbid water, cleansing the entire river to its pebble bed in an instant. A voice both gentle and majestic resonates in the depths of the traveler's soul, reminding him that even stumbling and tears of remorse, once illuminated by awakened awareness, can become a bend in the river flowing toward the sea.</p>
<p>If you have ever felt that tremor of being caught and held by the Buddha's light at the edge of an abyss, your heart will leap when you read the sixteen Chinese characters at the center of the Medicine Buddha's Fifth Great Vow:</p>
<p>"And even if they should commit a transgression or violation, upon hearing My name they shall regain their purity and not fall into evil destinies." (Shè yǒu huǐfàn, wén wǒ míng yǐ, huán dé qīngjìng, bù duò èqù.)</p>
<p>"Even if they should commit a transgression or violation"—this means: suppose you once made a solemn promise to yourself or to the Dharma, yet failed to keep it; suppose you knew clearly what was right, yet driven by desire, weakness, fear, or impulse, you did something that violated your conscience, hurt those who trusted you, and stained your own life.</p>
<p>In the courts of worldly opinion, people love nothing more than declaring a single misstep an eternal condemnation. Once a person bears a stain, society rushes to affix an indelible label, and in the dead of night the person himself whips his own soul: "I have broken my precepts; I have ruined my life; how could someone as soiled as I still kneel before a Buddha or deserve peace?"</p>
<p>From my early days on the set of Kunlun Returns to filming Deep in My Heart and Picking Stars with Willow Leaves around 2018 and 2019 in Hengdian and Xiangshan, I spent many months living inside film crews. A film crew is a condensed laboratory of human nature: hundreds of people gathered for months under high pressure, late nights, financial temptation, and emotional loneliness, where human fragility and fractures surface with stark clarity.</p>
<p>One winter while shooting a period drama in the south, there was an actor in his mid-thirties on our crew whom I shall call Old Chen.</p>
<p>Old Chen had graduated from a respected drama academy, acted in several acclaimed serious dramas early in his career, and was by nature generous and warm, with a devoted wife and a young daughter in primary school back home. Yet a couple of years earlier, during an industry slowdown, he had foolishly followed an acquaintance into high-leverage speculative trading. Within six months he not only lost all of his family's savings, but fell more than two million yuan into high-interest debt. Desperate to recover his losses and hide his embarrassment in the industry, he began telling lies at dinner tables, borrowed money from every friend in his address book without repaying on time, lost his temper on set in a brawl with a production manager, and finally drove his heartbroken wife to file for divorce and take their daughter away.</p>
<p>Halfway through our shoot, on a freezing rainy night, Old Chen knocked on my hotel room door reeking of alcohol.</p>
<p>Drenched by the winter rain, hair plastered across his forehead, he slid down against the door panel onto the carpet and wept aloud like a lost child: "Wenjie, you've studied the Book of Changes since childhood—tell me, is my life completely over? I drove away my wife and daughter, I lied away every friend's trust, I feel sick just looking at myself in the mirror... Yesterday I went to a temple to burn incense, and kneeling on the cushion I didn't even dare lift my head to look at the Buddha statue—I felt the Buddhas and Bodhisattvas would find me too dirty to look at!"</p>
<p>The hotel room was quiet save for the patter of Jiangnan winter rain against the awning outside.</p>
<p>I did not reach for the bronze coins on the desk. I walked into the bathroom, wrung out a hot towel and handed it to Old Chen, poured a glass of warm water and set it beside him, and sat down cross-legged on the carpet across from him.</p>
<p>"Old Chen," I said, looking into his bloodshot eyes, "do you know why I poured you this glass of plain warm water tonight instead of casting a hexagram? Because a hexagram is cast for an unknown road, whereas your road lies right beneath your feet—you yourself have blocked it with the words 'I am unworthy.'"</p>
<p>I picked up the worn copy of the Medicine Buddha Sutra from my nightstand, opened to the Fifth Great Vow, and pointed to the line: "And even if they should commit a transgression or violation, upon hearing My name they shall regain their purity and not fall into evil destinies."</p>
<p>"Look at this line, Old Chen," I said gently. "If the Buddha's Dharma and the way of heaven truly accepted only spotless saints who had never made a mistake or gathered a speck of dust, the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata would never have needed to make this Fifth Great Vow. He could simply have said, 'I save only those whose conduct is flawless.' Yet the Medicine Buddha deliberately added the words 'even if they should commit a transgression.' Why? Because as the Supreme Physician, He knows human nature to the bone. He knows how easy it is to be a good person when the wind is favorable, and how easily the human heart snaps like a reed in a gale when cornered by fear, greed, or desperation.</p>
<p>"If a shirt falls into a mud puddle," I asked him, "do you throw the shirt—and yourself with it—into a furnace to burn just because it got mud on it? No. You pick it up, carry it to a clear flowing river, wash the mud out inch by inch, and hang it in the sun until it is a clean garment again. The Fifth Great Vow of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata is that river which allows you to walk in and wash your robe whenever you truly wake up. When He says 'upon hearing My name they shall regain their purity,' He is not giving you a fig leaf to keep deceiving people; He is telling you that the very moment you genuinely admit your fault, stop falling further, confess honestly to those you wronged, repay your debts coin by coin through honest work, and stand upright again, in the lapis lazuli eyes of the Medicine Buddha you are already clean."</p>
<p>Old Chen sat in my room talking until dawn broke. When he left, the rain had stopped, and morning light pierced the clouds across the wet flagstones. In the years that followed, Old Chen quit drinking, never touched speculation again, took every acting role regardless of whether it had three scenes or thirty with utmost diligence, and every time a paycheck arrived, divided it immediately to repay the friends he owed and send child support to his former wife. Four years later, he had cleared every cent of debt; the restlessness and self-disgust had been washed from his brow, replaced by a weathered calm, and his wife, seeing his transformation, eventually took his hand again.</p>
<p>If the Fifth Great Vow heals the moral despair caused by transgression and guilt, the Sixth Great Vow immediately following it heals every physical and psychological brokenness in human life:</p>
<p>"If there are sentient beings whose bodies are inferior, whose faculties are incomplete, who are ugly, dull, or foolish, blind, deaf, or mute, crippled, hunchbacked, afflicted with leprosy, madness, or all manner of painful illnesses—upon hearing My name, they shall all obtain upright appearance and keen wisdom, with all their faculties complete and free from every sickness and affliction."</p>
<p>In our era, not everyone suffers from physical blindness, deafness, muteness, or paralysis; yet if you look up toward the compassionate eyes of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata—eyes that perceive the hidden ailments of the five aggregates—and look through His lapis lazuli radiance at the people around us, you will see how widespread spiritual "incompleteness of faculties" truly is:</p>
<p>Some have twenty-twenty eyesight yet see only others' flaws and the rise and fall of stock charts, remaining blind to the gray hair at their parents' temples and the blooming magnolias of spring—this is blindness of the heart.</p>
<p>Some have acute hearing yet cannot bear a single word of honest counsel or hear the sigh behind a partner's silence, listening only to flattery and rumor—this is deafness of the heart.</p>
<p>Some speak fluently at banquets and online for hours yet cannot utter a single gentle apology or word of truth to those they love most—this is muteness of the heart.</p>
<p>Some have four sound limbs yet bend their spines before wealth and power, or remain paralyzed by past trauma unable to take a single step forward—this is crippling of the heart.</p>
<p>And the Sixth Great Vow of the Medicine Buddha is a clear lapis lazuli mirror that never turns away a single wounded being. So long as we call upon that azure light, acknowledge our ailment, seek proper care, and let go of stubborn pride, His healing power begins to flow through our faculties.</p>
<h4>II. The Crack Before the Dressing Mirror: Why Do We Punish Ourselves Forever for Past Regrets?</h4>
<p>After spending years in the performing arts, I developed the habit of sitting quietly for an hour on the eve of a crew wrap to reflect on the people I had met along the way.</p>
<p>In 2019, while filming Picking Stars with Willow Leaves, a young actress recently graduated from an arts college joined our crew to play a supporting lady-in-waiting. She had delicate features, yet her temperament was painfully taut: whenever she stumbled over a line or missed a blocking mark by half a step—even before the director said a word—she would flush crimson, bow repeatedly in apology, and spend the rest of the day hiding behind the prop crates, waiting until everyone else had finished picking up their meal boxes before taking the coldest one left at the bottom.</p>
<p>One night while waiting for a lighting setup, I saw her sitting alone on a stone step staring blankly at her script, her fingers pinching the corner of the page into creases. I walked over and handed her a warm drink.</p>
<p>"Don't be nervous," I said with a smile. "You held that last scene very steadily."</p>
<p>As she took the warm cup, a teardrop fell straight onto the lid. Through her halting words, I learned that she had been carrying an invisible iron shackle on her back for six full years.</p>
<p>Six years earlier, during her second year of high school, her family had been poor and her mother chronically ill. That winter, her school organized a provincial art-exam training camp requiring a three-thousand-yuan fee for tuition and lodging. Unwilling to see her daughter give up her dream, her mother walked through a snowstorm to borrow the money from relatives in town, slipped on an icy road on the way back, suffered a cerebral hemorrhage, and passed away at the hospital.</p>
<p>From that day on, the girl regarded herself as a "sinner who had killed her mother."</p>
<p>Although she later passed her college entrance exams and entered film crews, the judge inside her mind never pardoned her for a single second. Every time she tasted a delicious meal, bought a new coat, or smiled before the camera, an icy voice inside her chest demanded: "You cost your mother her life—how dare you smile? How dare you enjoy good days?" And so she punished herself relentlessly, tolerating mistreatment in relationships and berating herself mercilessly over the smallest error at work, as though only by living in a private hell could she pay back that three-thousand-yuan debt.</p>
<p>Far across the set, the gaffer called out to shift the lights, and a searchlight beam cut across the night sky, casting long shadows over the tiled roofs.</p>
<p>After a moment's silence, I asked her softly: "If your mother were standing beneath that lamp right now looking at you, do you think she walked through that snowstorm to borrow three thousand yuan so that you would spend your life hiding behind prop boxes eating cold rice and stabbing your own heart every day—or so that she could see you standing in the sunlight, healthy, upright, and acting every scene with joy?"</p>
<p>She covered her mouth with both hands as her shoulders shook.</p>
<p>"In the Fifth Great Vow of the Medicine Buddha Sutra," I said, looking up at the thin mist drifting across the night sky, "there is the line: 'Even if they should commit a transgression or violation, upon hearing My name they shall regain their purity and not fall into evil destinies.' Many assume 'evil destinies' (èqù) refer only to hells, hungry ghosts, and beasts after death. Yet whenever you nail yourself forever to a cross of shame over an unintended tragedy or a past regret—forbidding yourself to be happy, forbidding yourself to be loved, roasting your own soul over the fire of self-hatred day after day—you have already thrown yourself into an evil destiny here and now.</p>
<p>"The Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata made the Fifth Great Vow to pull us out of the prison of self-punishment. If even one who has genuinely broken a grave precept can regain purity through sincere repentance upon hearing the Medicine Buddha's name, how much more a daughter who simply loved learning and loved her mother? Living the life your mother gave you with warmth, brightness, and dignity is the only true repayment you can offer her. Starting tomorrow, when the hot meal boxes arrive, walk up and take the first one."</p>
<p>From that night on, the girl who hid behind the prop crates disappeared. She remained humble and earnest, yet when the camera turned toward her, her eyes held a clear, fearless light that dared to meet the sun.</p>
<h4>III. From "Blind, Deaf, and Mute" to "Upright and Keen-Witted": Cultivating a Whole Heart in an Imperfect World</h4>
<p>Whenever I gaze upon the countenance of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, watching those eyes that see every mortal sorrow while remaining infinitely serene, I read a silent teaching in His gentle smile: when you cease fighting the imperfections of life with harsh resentment, and instead learn to embrace and heal this scarred world of birth, aging, sickness, and death with translucent lapis lazuli compassion, life begins moving toward wholeness in that very instant.</p>
<p>During the 2015 filming of Underground Fragrance, in order to understand Yong Le's inner state when his eyesight is injured and plunged into darkness, I spent several days living inside a small room with a blindfold over my eyes.</p>
<p>When sight was first taken away, wave after wave of disorientation swept over me—not knowing where the corner of the table stood or where the water glass rested, afraid that a single step would bruise my shins. Yet once I quieted down in the dark, stopped waving my hands in frustration, and breathed evenly while listening and touching with care, something remarkable happened: I heard the flutter of sparrows outside the window that I had never noticed before, heard the subtle rise in pitch as hot water filled a porcelain cup, and smelled the distinct layers of resin and earth released by old timber as the humidity shifted.</p>
<p>In that darkness I realized that "all faculties complete, upright and keen-witted" (zhūgēn wánjù, duānyán xiáhuì) in the Sixth Great Vow means far more than intact physical organs.</p>
<p>When you truly hear the name of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata—hearing that call to cherish life, awaken to the present moment, and behold oneself and all beings with compassion—your dulled faculties awaken as though bathed in cool ambrosia:</p>
<p>Your eyes rediscover the morning light and the dewdrop (no longer spiritually "blind");</p>
<p>Your ears hear once more the wind in the trees and the joys and sorrows of others (no longer spiritually "deaf");</p>
<p>Your tongue learns to speak words that are warm, truthful, and encouraging (no longer spiritually "mute");</p>
<p>Your spine stands straight between heaven and earth, neither bowing in flattery before vanity nor collapsing before hardship (no longer spiritually "crippled or bent");</p>
<p>Your mind settles from the frenzy of greed, anger, and delusion into a lake after rain, clear to the bottom (no longer spiritually "maddened").</p>
<h4>IV. Pouring Lapis Lazuli into the Cracks: When the Frozen River of Self-Blame Meets the Spring Breeze</h4>
<p>Whenever I reflect on the Fifth Vow's "even if they should commit a transgression, upon hearing My name they shall regain their purity" and the Sixth Vow's "upon hearing My name, they shall all obtain upright appearance and keen wisdom, with all their faculties complete," I remember the spring ice-break (kāijiāng) on the Ussuri River in my northern homeland.</p>
<p>During the long winter, the ice on the Ussuri freezes over a meter thick, hard as iron plate, covered with gray-black dust, dead branches, and cart tracks. Standing by the bank in January, you might think the river had died forever and that those dirty cracks were frozen permanently into its bones.</p>
<p>Yet when the south wind of the second and third lunar months blows across the Wanda Mountains and the spring sun warms the ice day after day, a low rumble like distant thunder rises from beneath the surface—the river water sealed all winter has awakened in the depths. Soon the entire frozen expanse splits into ten thousand fissures; massive ice floes drift downstream, colliding and melting in the sunlight, and within a few days the Ussuri transforms once again into a vast, surging river of azure spring water. Every trace of winter dust and cart tracks vanishes without a shadow in the living current.</p>
<p>Is not the repentance and self-healing of the human heart a springtime breaking of the ice?</p>
<p>In the traditional East Asian art of kintsugi (golden joinery), when a cherished ceramic bowl is cracked, the artisan does not toss it onto the rubbish heap; instead, he binds the fracture with natural lacquer and dusts the seam with warm gold powder. Once mended, the line where the bowl broke is no longer an ugly defect, but a golden river gleaming in the lamplight.</p>
<p>The Fifth and Sixth Great Vows of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata are the kintsugi performed upon our lives with pure lapis lazuli light. They do not ask you to pretend you never cracked, nor do they allow you to lie on the ground in self-abandonment because you once fell. The Medicine Buddha lifts you gently, washes away the grit with pure conduct and wisdom, and causes the very place where you were once wounded to grow more resilient, luminous, and empathetic toward the suffering of all beings.</p>
<h4>V. "Endowed with the Threefold Pure Precepts": Precepts Are Not Ropes, but the City Walls Guarding Lapis Lazuli</h4>
<p>In the Fifth Great Vow, there is a phrase that modern readers enamored of unconstrained freedom often misunderstand: "I shall cause them all to obtain unblemished precepts and be endowed with the threefold pure precepts" (dé bùquē jiè, jù sānjù jiè).</p>
<p>Many people hear the word "precepts" (śīla) and immediately think of restriction, repression, and chains.</p>
<p>Yet after walking through the vanity fair for over a decade and watching gifted peers fall from the clouds into ruin within a few short years because they lacked boundaries and self-restraint, I came to understand why the Medicine Buddha places "unblemished precepts and the threefold pure precepts" (the Fifth Vow) directly before "upright appearance, keen wisdom, complete faculties, and freedom from illness" (the Sixth Vow).</p>
<p>In the Book of Changes, there is the hexagram Jié (Water over Lake: Moderation / Restraint). Its Commentary on the Image states: "Above the lake is water: Moderation. The noble person fashions measures and examines virtuous conduct." A lake on the earth can hold clear water and nourish fish and lotus blossoms only because its banks provide a boundary; if you tear down the embankments around a lake, the water spills in all directions and turns into a dry mudflat within a day.</p>
<p>Precepts are never ropes meant to strangle life; they are the lakeshore embankments guarding your vital energy and lapis lazuli mind, the guardrails along a mountain highway keeping your carriage from plunging over the cliff!</p>
<p>Look at how complete and warm the Bodhisattva's Threefold Pure Precepts (sānjù jìngjiè) are:</p>
<p><em>The First is the Precept of Restraining Behavior (shè lǜyí jiè)</em>: stopping all harm—refusing to do what injures others, violates law and conscience, or drains one's own vital spirit. This builds the flood dike of life.</p>
<p><em>The Second is the Precept of Cultivating Wholesome Dharmas (shè shànfǎ jiè)</em>: practicing all good—studying diligently, refining character, and maintaining cleanliness and mindfulness every day. This pours fresh spring water continuously into the lake.</p>
<p><em>The Third is the Precept of Benefiting Sentient Beings (shè zhòngshēng jiè)</em>: serving living beings—using compassion and skill to comfort, understand, and uplift those around you. This opens the irrigation channels so the full lake waters parched fields.</p>
<p>When there is pure restraint and sincere renewal in the cause (the Fifth Vow), there naturally blooms physical and spiritual wholeness in the fruit (the Sixth Vow). And ahead on my own journey lay the misty peaks of Mount Lu in Jiangxi, where the Seventh and Eighth Great Vows would unite all twelve rays of lapis lazuli light inside a quiet temple hall.</p>
<h2 class="volume" id="part3">Part III: The Mist of Mount Lu and Prescriptions for Hidden Mortal Afflictions</h2>
<p class="subtitle">(The Main Section II · Vows Seven to Twelve, the Fourfold Admonition, and the Non-Duality of East and West)</p>
<h3 id="ch6">Chapter 6: The Gaze at Donglin Temple on Mount Lu and the Alms Bowl at the End of the Road (The Seventh and Eighth Great Vows: Peace of Body and Mind and Transcending Oppression)</h3>
<h4>Scriptural Text</h4>
<p>"The Seventh Great Vow: 'I vow that in a future life, when I attain Bodhi, if there are sentient beings who are pressed hard by manifold illnesses, without rescue and without refuge, without physician and without medicine, without relatives and without home, destitute and weighed down by suffering—as soon as My name passes through their ears, all their illnesses shall be dispelled, their bodies and minds shall abide in peace and joy, their families and provisions shall all become abundant, and they shall ultimately realize unsurpassed Bodhi.'</p>
<p>The Eighth Great Vow: 'I vow that in a future life, when I attain Bodhi, if there are women who are oppressed and tormented by the hundredfold afflictions of female existence, who feel deep weariness and wish to relinquish the female form—upon hearing My name, they shall all be transformed from female to male, endowed with the marks of a great person, and ultimately realize unsurpassed Bodhi.'"</p>
<h4>I. The Mountain Mist of Donglin Temple on Mount Lu and the Soul-Absorbing Statue of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli World-Honored One</h4>
<p>In every human life there is a mountain that must at some point be climbed alone, and a reunion written at the hour of one's birth that can be kept only after walking ten thousand miles.</p>
<p>In the stories of ancient seekers, that place was sometimes an age-old river murmuring day and night, or a Bodhi tree rustling in the breeze; in my life, the place where I truly came face to face with the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata was Donglin Temple at the western foot of Mount Lu (Lúshān Dōnglín Sì) in Jiangxi Province.</p>
<p>In the months leading up to my journey to Mount Lu, I had entered a subtle, weary pause in my life.</p>
<p>To outward appearances, my twenties had unfolded smoothly: a boy from the banks of the Ussuri River who opened major international runway shows in Beijing at nineteen, starred at twenty-two in Underground Fragrance—which won the FEDEORA Award for Best Film in the "Venice Days" section of the Venice International Film Festival and the Gold Hugo at the Chicago International Film Festival—and went on to work across film crews while founding cultural enterprises. I had seen the vanity fair at close range and often drew upon my family's heritage in the Book of Changes to help colleagues and friends untangle their dilemmas.</p>
<p>Yet like a counselor standing year after year on a riverbank comforting distressed travelers, after listening to too many sighs, witnessing too much grasping and anxiety in the world of fame, and enduring too many sleepless nights on film sets, my own heart had grown heavy and parched as a sponge soaked in cold water.</p>
<p>On several nights I woke in hotel rooms in unfamiliar cities, listening to the wind outside the window with a dull ache in my chest. Taking out the three bronze coins left by my grandfather to quiet my mind and reflect, I read an indication pointing toward "high mountains and ancient woods in the southwest, where the original heart awaits."</p>
<p>And so I bought a ticket to Jiujiang in Jiangxi and set out alone for Donglin Temple on Mount Lu.</p>
<p>Early that morning, dense mist rose at the foot of Mount Lu.</p>
<p>It was not the dry, biting frost-fog of a northern winter, but the milky mountain vapor unique to the hills and waters of Jiangnan, breathing with the moisture of thousand-year-old trees and mosses. As the car wound up the mountain road, camphor trees, green bamboo, and ancient pines appeared and vanished in the mist like a Song Dynasty ink scroll freshly washed by rain. When the venerable mountain gate and dark-tiled, ochre-walled halls of Donglin Temple emerged from the clouds, the clear, resonant tolling of the morning bell echoed through the valley.</p>
<p>I crossed the stone bridge over Tiger Brook (Hǔxī Qiáo) in front of the mountain gate—the celebrated site of the "Three Laughs at Tiger Brook." Although historical scholarship notes that the Daoist master Lu Xiujing was born in 406 CE while Master Huiyuan passed into stillness in 416 CE when Lu was still a boy of ten—making the tale of Huiyuan, the poet Tao Yuanming, and Lu Xiujing crossing the brook together in shared laughter a poetic legend woven by later generations to celebrate the harmony of Confucianism, Buddhism, and Daoism—the stream rushing over green stones beneath the bridge carried a genuine mountain clarity that seemed to wash ten years of worldly dust from the soles of my shoes.</p>
<p>Instead of raising a phone to snap photographs like a casual tourist, I slowed my pace alongside a few early-rising monastics and lay practitioners, walking quietly across wet flagstone courtyards from hall to hall into the depths of the monastery.</p>
<p>As I passed through a covered corridor and stepped across the threshold of the quiet, solemn hall enshrining the Medicine Buddha, my footsteps stopped as though drawn by a magnet deep within the earth.</p>
<p>Inside the hall there was no clamor of voices—only the soft glow of ever-burning oil lamps mingling with daylight from the high windows through curling ribbons of aloeswood incense. And upon the lotus pedestal in the center of the hall sat a statue of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli World-Honored One (Yàoshī Liúlí Shìzūn).</p>
<p>I lifted my head, and my eyes met the gaze of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli World-Honored One in mid-air.</p>
<p>In that instant, time seemed to cease flowing altogether.</p>
<p>Many times since that morning I have tried to describe what happened in words, only to find every ordinary adjective pale before that presence. It was not merely the sculptural beauty of sacred art; it was a vast, tranquil, soul-penetrating absorptive power and blessing (shèshòulì yǔ jiāchílì). The Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli World-Honored One sat with compassionate eyes gently half-lowered, His countenance majestic yet infinitely tender, as though a deep-sea radiance of pure lapis lazuli glowed from within His very form. One hand formed a mudra of fearless bestowal while the other held the medicine bowl brimming with ambrosial nectar. What sent a tremor through my entire frame was the look in those half-veiled eyes—a gaze devoid of the slightest divine intimidation, looking instead like an elder waiting with a lantern beneath a snow-swept porch for a child who had wandered for thirty years, or like my own truest nature greeting me across a vaster dimension of time.</p>
<p>Bathed in that gaze, I felt as though a stream of warm, crystal-clear spring water poured down from the crown of my head, soothing every taut nerve and meridian throughout my body.</p>
<p>In a single flash I remembered the first northern snow of my birth on the thirtieth day of the ninth lunar month in 1993—realizing that from my very first cry on earth, the calendar of my life had been inscribed upon the natal day of this King of Physicians of the Eastern Pure Lapis Lazuli World. I remembered the stir in my boyhood heart by the Ussuri River when I first saw "Natal Day of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata" printed in cinnabar ink beneath the thirtieth of the ninth lunar month; I remembered the blinding spotlights on the runways of Beijing and Hong Kong; I remembered the scent of old camphor wood twenty steps underground in Underground Fragrance; I remembered all the years I had tried to comfort others with the bronze coins of the Book of Changes while searching for an ultimate light that could heal the root of human sorrow.</p>
<p>"So You have been waiting for me here all along," I thought, my eyes filling with tears as I stood before the kneeling cushion in the hall. "All the roads I walked, all the lives I inhabited before the camera, all the cold and warmth I tasted in the mortal world were meant to bring me today to Donglin Temple on Mount Lu, to recognize my path inside Your gaze."</p>
<p>Slowly I bent my knees, bowed down upon the cool, clean cushion, and rested my forehead against the backs of my hands for a long time without rising.</p>
<p>In that prostration, the mountain mist of Mount Lu drifted through the open lattice, brushing across my back with the cool scent of pine needles and incense. I felt every ounce of restlessness, bravado, and exhaustion I had carried through the first three decades of my life received steadily into the medicine bowl held by the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli World-Honored One. From that morning onward, my faith in the Medicine Buddha and the Medicine Buddha Sutra grew from a childhood seed of affinity into a deeply rooted tree.</p>
<h4>II. The Medicine Bowl for Those Without Refuge: "As Soon as My Name Passes Through Their Ears" in the Seventh Great Vow</h4>
<p>During the days I stayed near Donglin Temple on Mount Lu, I went every morning at dawn to sit quietly before that statue of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli World-Honored One and reread the Medicine Buddha Sutra word by word.</p>
<p>Reading the Seventh Great Vow beneath the World-Honored One's gaze, every character on the page seemed alive with warmth:</p>
<p>"If there are sentient beings who are pressed hard by manifold illnesses, without rescue and without refuge, without physician and without medicine, without relatives and without home, destitute and weighed down by suffering—as soon as My name passes through their ears, all their illnesses shall be dispelled, their bodies and minds shall abide in peace and joy, their families and provisions shall all become abundant, and they shall ultimately realize unsurpassed Bodhi."</p>
<p>Look at the five four-character phrases the Sutra places one after another: "pressed hard by manifold illnesses, without rescue and without refuge, without physician and without medicine, without relatives and without home, destitute and weighed down by suffering" (zhòngbìng bīqiè, wújiù wúguī, wúyī wúyào, wúqīn wújiā, pínqióng duōkǔ).</p>
<p>That twenty-character sequence in the Chinese text describes the lowest valley of human hardship.</p>
<p>If a person falls ill yet has savings at home, skilled doctors, effective medicine, and parents or spouse beside the bed offering warm water, illness is painful but not yet hopeless. True desolation arrives when a person is pinned down by sickness or life's blows (pressed hard by manifold illnesses), looks around, and finds neither a protector to call upon nor a harbor to retreat to (without rescue and without refuge), lacking timely medical care (without physician and without medicine), without even a roof or a relative before whom he can weep in safety (without relatives and without home, destitute and weighed down by suffering).</p>
<p>Along a covered walkway behind the halls on Mount Lu, I met a lay volunteer in his seventies who was sweeping fallen leaves (to respect his quiet life, I withhold his name).</p>
<p>His hair was snow-white, his washed cotton robe neat, his movements brisk, and his face wore a smile as calm as a mountain brook. While resting on a stone bench over tea, he told me how he had first come to Mount Lu twenty years earlier.</p>
<p>In his early fifties, a chain of disasters had struck his life: a fire damaged the building-materials warehouse he ran in the south, family members passed away amid shock and illness, he sold his only home to pay off creditors, and chronic grief and exhaustion left him with severe gastric ulcers and deep depression until he was reduced to skin and bone.</p>
<p>One winter day, wandering near Jiujiang in cold rain, hungry, penniless, and utterly despondent, he followed a group of pilgrims up the mountain simply to shelter from the freezing rain and stepped inside a hall at Donglin Temple.</p>
<p>Evening recitation was underway inside, and a deep, resonant chant rose like a warm tide through the hall: "Namo Medicine Master Buddha Who Dispels Calamities and Lengthens Life... Namo Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata..."</p>
<p>Cold and shivering in a corner by a pillar, the moment the sacred name "Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata" entered his ears (as soon as My name passes through their ears), he looked up and beheld the Medicine World-Honored One upon the altar, compassionate eyes lowered, holding the medicine bowl in His hand.</p>
<p>"Young man," the elder said to me, holding his earthenware teacup with clear, bright eyes, "the moment I heard that Buddha-name and saw the Medicine Buddha looking down at me, the dead ash in my chest—that feeling that the whole universe had thrown me away—cracked open. I sat behind that pillar and wept for a full hour, crying out years of pent-up bitterness, and my cramping stomach loosened for the first time in days. After evening service, the guest-prefect monk and lay volunteers noticed how pale I was; they brought me to the dining hall for two bowls of hot radish-noodle soup, and the very next morning pooled funds to send me to a regular hospital in Jiujiang for a full medical examination and prescribed ulcer medication."</p>
<p>With proper clinical treatment from hospital physicians and the regular, peaceful schedule of monastery life, the elder's gastric illness healed completely; afterward he worked with his carpentry and masonry skills to clear his remaining obligations, and in his later years returned often as a volunteer. Now, in his heart, fellow practitioners were his family and the pine wind and mountain moon his abundant provisions—a living embodiment of moving from "without rescue and without refuge" to "peace of body and mind, with family and provisions all abundant."</p>
<p>Here we must emphasize a vital point for every reader: when the Seventh Great Vow says "as soon as My name passes through their ears, all their illnesses shall be dispelled," it never teaches a sick person to refuse doctors and rely on chanting alone in place of medical treatment (indeed, in Chapter 10, the very first of the "Nine Untimely Deaths" condemned by the Sutra is "having an illness yet lacking physician and medicine, or taking the wrong medicine"). The true power of the Medicine Buddha's name passing through one's ears is that at the darkest hour when a person is close to mental collapse from pain and isolation, the compassionate name of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata shatters the inner despair of feeling abandoned by the world, rekindles the will to live, steadies the spirit, and connects the sufferer with compassionate people and proper medical care.</p>
<h4>III. What Does "Transformed from Female to Male, Endowed with the Marks of a Great Person" Mean? Historical Context and the Egalitarian Awakening Beyond Dependence</h4>
<p>In reading groups and conversations, female readers who turn to the Eighth Great Vow of the Medicine Buddha Sutra often ask me a sincere and important question:</p>
<p>"Wenjie, the Eighth Great Vow says: 'If there are women who are oppressed and tormented by the hundredfold afflictions of female existence, who feel deep weariness and wish to relinquish the female form—upon hearing My name, they shall all be transformed from female to male, endowed with the marks of a great person, and ultimately realize unsurpassed Bodhi.' Doesn't Mahāyāna Buddhism teach that all sentient beings are equal in Buddha-nature? Why does this vow speak of 'relinquishing the female form and transforming from female to male'? How should we read this today without falling into gender prejudice?"</p>
<p>To understand the Eighth Great Vow truthfully, we must first examine the historical reality of ancient India two thousand five hundred years ago (the original scriptural context), then look at the ultimate truth of Mahāyāna Buddhism, and finally draw out its modern contemplative application for all human beings.</p>
<p>First, in its historical and scriptural context, the Buddha never spoke in a vacuum divorced from human society; He addressed the concrete suffering before His eyes. Under the rigid caste system and patriarchal laws of ancient India two and a half millennia ago, women at the lower rungs of society faced crushing institutional oppression and physical peril from birth: denied independent legal personhood or inheritance rights, married off in childhood at the command of fathers and brothers, walking to the brink of death with every childbirth in an era devoid of modern obstetrics, and facing lifelong servitude or forced widowhood if a husband died.</p>
<p>Therefore, when the Sutra speaks of "oppressed and tormented by the hundredfold afflictions of female existence" (wèi nǚ bǎi'è zhī suǒ bīnǎo), the Buddha is not disparaging the female biological sex; rather, with unflinching empathy, He is recording and grieving over the hundredfold injustices, oppressions, and involuntary hardships inflicted upon women in that ancient patriarchal era! Because countless suffering women in ancient India cried out in the night, "May I never again have to endure such helpless oppression," the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata responded directly to their cry with the Eighth Great Vow, promising liberation from every shackle of gender oppression.</p>
<p>Second, from the ultimate standpoint of Mahāyāna Dharma, Buddha-nature has neither male nor female form. In the First Great Vow of the Medicine Buddha Sutra, the Medicine Buddha opens by declaring: "I shall cause all sentient beings to become wholly like Me, without any difference." And in the Vimalakīrti Nirdeśa Sūtra (Chapter on Viewing Sentient Beings), when the elder Śāriputra asks the goddess why she does not transform out of her female body, the goddess uses her spiritual power to exchange forms with Śāriputra and says with a smile: "All dharmas are just like this—without fixed male or female mark. Why then do you ask why I do not transform my female form?"</p>
<p>Thus, in ultimate Mahāyāna reality, maleness does not inherently signify strength, nor does femaleness signify weakness; both are provisional appearances arising from conditions.</p>
<p>Third, in a modern society committed to gender equality, how should we receive the blessing of the Eighth Great Vow in our inner contemplation?</p>
<p>In classical Chinese and Buddhist usage, the term "Great Person / Heroic Spirit" (dàzhàngfu, Sanskrit mahāpuruṣa) frequently denotes an autonomous, fearless spiritual stature transcending biological sex—Mencius defined a dàzhàngfu as one whom "wealth and rank cannot corrupt, poverty and obscurity cannot budge, and tyrannical force cannot bend," and Chan masters historically praised awakened nuns and laywomen as "heroic spirits among women" (nǚ zhōng dàzhàngfu).</p>
<p>In modern life, I have met many people—both women and men—trapped in emotional dependence and psychological manipulation (PUA). I remember a talented young graphic designer in Guangzhou whom I shall call Xiao He. Kindhearted and gifted in her work, she spent four years in an abusive relationship where her partner used cold-shoulder tactics and verbal belittling to strip away her self-worth, telling her repeatedly, "With a personality like yours, no one besides me would ever tolerate you." Suffering from chronic insomnia and anxiety, she barely had the strength to draw, yet feared walking away.</p>
<p>When Xiao He read the Medicine Buddha Sutra and asked me how to contemplate the Eighth Great Vow, I said to her:</p>
<p>"The 'hundredfold afflictions' suffered by women in ancient India were external patriarchal chains forced upon them; today, whether a person is female or male, if someone ties all of their self-worth, joy, and security to another person's approval, living in constant fear, appeasement, and self-belittlement, they are enduring an inner version of 'being oppressed by a hundred afflictions.'</p>
<p>"The message of the Medicine Buddha's Eighth Great Vow for us today is never to belittle womanhood; it is a call for <em>every soul—female or male—to awaken from helpless dependence and oppression, and to stand upright in spiritual sovereignty, dignity, and fearlessness (endowed with the marks of a great person)</em>!"</p>
<p>When Xiao He realized that genuine security is never begged from an abuser, sought professional psychological counseling, ended that toxic entanglement, and picked up her drawing tablet again, she needed no change of gender to accomplish the transformation from an "oppressed captive" into a "sovereign spirit" bathed in the Medicine Buddha's lapis lazuli light.</p>
<h4>IV. Remembering Donglin Temple on Mount Lu: The Myrobalan Fruit and Ambrosia in the Medicine Buddha's Hands</h4>
<p>In the years after I descended from Donglin Temple on Mount Lu, whenever friends asked me, "Wenjie, you studied the Book of Changes from childhood and visited so many monasteries across north and south—why was it specifically that statue of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli World-Honored One at Donglin Temple on Mount Lu that awakened and anchored your faith in the Medicine Buddha?"</p>
<p>Whenever I hear that question, I close my eyes and return to that mist-veiled morning inside the hall of Donglin Temple.</p>
<p>After rising from my prostrations before the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli World-Honored One that morning, I did not leave immediately; I stood quietly to the front side of the hall and gazed up at the statue for nearly an hour.</p>
<p>Outside the high lattice windows, the mountain rain of Mount Lu fell in soft sheets, washing the ancient pines a deep emerald green; inside the warm lamplight of the hall, I looked closely at the sacred attributes associated with the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli World-Honored One—the medicine bowl brimming with ambrosia in His left palm, and the small myrobalan fruit (hēzǐ, Sanskrit harītakī, revered in classical medicine and Buddhist iconography as the "King of Medicines") held in His right hand.</p>
<p>If you have ever tasted the medicinal fruit harītakī, you know its remarkable flavor: when it first touches the tongue it is bitter, astringent, and sour; yet as you hold it and swallow, a deep, cool, lingering sweetness (huígān) rises from the throat, harmonizing warmth and chill across the internal organs.</p>
<p>Standing inside the profound absorptive power and blessing of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli World-Honored One at Donglin Temple, I suddenly understood the mystery of that myrobalan fruit and that lapis lazuli medicine bowl:</p>
<p>Which of the hardships a human being encounters in this life—whether trudging through northern blizzards in youth, tasting vanity and loneliness beneath runway lights and film sets, or wrestling with illness, scarcity, heartbreak, and loss—does not taste bitter, astringent, and sour when it first enters the mouth?</p>
<p>An ordinary person tasting bitterness immediately curses, spits it out, or sinks into resentment. The Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli World-Honored One, however, gathers all five bitter and sour flavors of mortal life into His translucent lapis lazuli medicine bowl, and through boundless compassion and awareness transforms them into a bowl of sweet ambrosia capable of soothing the sorrows of the world.</p>
<p>What we call the "absorptive power" (shèshòulì) of a sacred image is never an external force pressing a person down; rather, when you lift your head at Donglin Temple on Mount Lu and meet the eyes of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli World-Honored One, you behold within His deep azure pupils the original clarity and courage of your own life. Through a gaze that has crossed millennia, He says silently: Since you share the bond of the thirtieth day of the ninth lunar month, none of the bitterness you have tasted was in vain; place it inside the medicine bowl of your heart, let it steep into empathy for the suffering of others, and write words that bring peace to weary travelers.</p>
<h4>V. When the Clouds Part to Reveal the True Mountain: Faith Is Not a Hideout from the World, but Luggage for Returning to It</h4>
<p>On the final morning of my stay at Donglin Temple on Mount Lu, the mountain rain that had fallen for several days finally ceased.</p>
<p>Rising before dawn, I went once more to the hall enshrining the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli World-Honored One to bow in farewell. When I pushed open the hall door and looked out, the heavy white mist that had filled the valley for days was dissolving rapidly beneath the rising sun; the craggy green peaks of Mount Lu stood sharp against a sky washed as clear as blue crystal, while water drops falling from the temple eaves into shallow hollows in the green flagstones sparkled like droplets of liquid gold.</p>
<p>A young lay practitioner visiting the temple beside me sighed and said: "Wenjie, the mountain forest here is so peaceful, and the Medicine Buddha statue has such comforting power—I wish I could stay on this mountain forever. The mere thought of going down tomorrow into the city to face complicated people and work makes my heart shrink."</p>
<p>Listening to him, I turned and looked back into the depths of the hall at the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli World-Honored One. Consider the image and the vows of the Medicine Buddha: after realizing supreme Bodhi and manifesting a body of pure lapis lazuli light, did He hide Himself inside a solitary cave where no cry could reach Him? Quite the opposite—He turned His eyes toward the bitterest, darkest, noisiest crossings of the ten directions, listening to the groans of the sick, caring for the homeless wanderer, sheltering the oppressed, and holding His medicine bowl right in the middle of mortal tears.</p>
<p>"If a faith works only atop a quiet mountain where no human noise is heard, and shatters the moment one steps back into family, workplace, and daily life down below," I said gently to the young practitioner, "then it is still a greenhouse flower, not true lapis lazuli."</p>
<p>True lapis lazuli is tempered in fire—it gleams amid the morning bell and evening drum of an ancient mountain monastery, and it remains equally clear and unbreakable inside city subways, hospital corridors, office buildings, and marketplaces. At Donglin Temple on Mount Lu, the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli World-Honored One bestowed His absorptive power and blessing upon me not to keep me hidden on the mountain, but to fill my pack with the Seventh and Eighth Great Vows so that I could walk back down into the human world carrying a steady lamp.</p>
<h3 id="ch7">Chapter 7: The Nets of the Film Set and the Nectar of the Mortal World (The Ninth to Twelfth Great Vows and the Bodhisattvas of Sunlight and Moonlight)</h3>
<h4>Scriptural Text</h4>
<p>"The Ninth Great Vow: 'I vow that in a future life, when I attain Bodhi, I shall cause all sentient beings to escape from the snares and nets of Māra and be liberated from all entanglements of heterodox paths; if they have fallen into dense thickets of various evil views, I shall guide and gather them into right view, gradually causing them to cultivate the practices of bodhisattvas and swiftly realize unsurpassed, right and equal Bodhi.'</p>
<p>The Tenth Great Vow: 'I vow that in a future life, when I attain Bodhi, if there are sentient beings who are subjected to royal laws, bound with ropes and scourged with whips, fettered and locked in dark prisons, or facing execution, together with countless other disasters and humiliations, tormented by grief and anxiety, suffering in body and mind—if they hear My name, by virtue of My merit and awesome spiritual power, they shall all be liberated from every sorrow and affliction.'</p>
<p>The Eleventh Great Vow: 'I vow that in a future life, when I attain Bodhi, if there are sentient beings who are tormented by hunger and thirst and create various evil karma in their struggle to obtain food—upon hearing My name and mindfulness-retaining it with focused heart, I shall first satisfy their bodies with exquisite food and drink, and afterward establish them in ultimate peace and joy through the flavor of the Dharma.'</p>
<p>The Twelfth Great Vow: 'I vow that in a future life, when I attain Bodhi, if there are sentient beings who are destitute and without clothing, tormented day and night by mosquitoes, gadflies, cold, and heat—if they hear My name and retain it with focused heart, according to their wishes they shall immediately obtain all manner of exquisite garments, as well as all precious adornments, flower garlands, fragrant ointments, drum music, and varied arts, fulfilling whatever their hearts desire.'</p>
<p>Mañjuśrī! These are the twelve subtle and supreme vows made by that World-Honored Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, Arhat, Samyaksaṃbuddha when He was practicing the Bodhisattva path. ... Within His land there are two bodhisattva-mahāsattvas: the first is named Sunlight Pervading Everywhere (Sūryaprabha), and the second is named Moonlight Pervading Everywhere (Candraprabha). They stand at the head of that immeasurable, countless assembly of bodhisattvas, next in succession to fill the Buddha-seat, and are fully able to uphold the treasury of the True Dharma of that World-Honored Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata."</p>
<h4>I. Invisible "Snares of Māra" and "Dense Thickets of Evil Views" (The Ninth Great Vow)</h4>
<p>When a traveler dwells for years in a sprawling metropolis and looks around through the clear wisdom-eyes of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, he notices a peculiar hidden ailment afflicting many city dwellers: they are pulled back and forth by countless invisible threads—gains and losses on balance sheets, rankings in the vanity fair, envious or disdainful glances from acquaintances. Those threads weave an airtight net inside which people run, fight, laugh wildly, and weep, mistaking the net for the entire universe. While high above in the Pure Lapis Lazuli sky, the Medicine Tathāgata extends His golden arm adorned with a blazing net of light, waiting to draw out every soul that awakens to its suffocation.</p>
<p>The Ninth Great Vow of the Medicine Buddha Sutra gives this trap a precise name: the snares and nets of Māra (mó juànwǎng) and dense thickets of evil views (èjiàn chóulín).</p>
<p>A snare-net (juànwǎng) is a fowler's corded trap that tightens the more a bird thrashes; a dense thicket (chóulín) is an overgrown jungle of thorns shut off from sunlight where a traveler loses all sense of north, south, east, and west.</p>
<p>During the years from 2019 to 2022 when I worked on projects such as Guanghan, China and Chasing Jade, friends inside and outside the industry who knew of my lifelong study of the Book of Changes and the Medicine Buddha Sutra often came to talk when they hit life impasses. Nothing pained me more than seeing intelligent people fall into modern "snares of Māra" and "thickets of evil views."</p>
<p>I remember an entrepreneur in her thirties in Shenzhen whom I shall call Sister Lin. Sister Lin had built a cross-border e-commerce business with admirable diligence and level-headed judgment. Later, however, after a painful relationship breakup coincided with an industry downturn, she was drawn into a closed, manipulative "pseudo-spiritual group." Its leader instilled in her an absurd thicket of distorted views: claiming that filial affection for one's aging parents and honest business management were "low-dimensional karmic entanglements," and persuading her to drain her company's working capital to buy worthless resin "energy props" and exorbitant courses under the promise of an overnight "wealth manifestation channel."</p>
<p>Within a year and a half, Sister Lin cut off contact with her worried parents, let her company slide toward default, and shut herself every day inside a living room with heavy black curtains drawn, burning acrid synthetic incense while waiting for miraculous orders to drop from the sky. When suppliers threatened lawsuits and her family reached out to me in desperation, I went to visit her.</p>
<p>She was sitting on the floor among dozens of cheap resin spheres, her gaze hollow yet feverish, repeating canned jargon.</p>
<p>I did not argue over her terminology. With her family's consent, I walked straight to the window and pulled wide the heavy blackout curtains that had been shut for months.</p>
<p>The bright midday sun of southern China flooded the room, illuminating the dust on the floor and the crude plastic seams on the bases of those "high-dimensional spheres."</p>
<p>Sister Lin raised an arm to shield her eyes from the glare.</p>
<p>"Sister Lin," I said calmly, standing by the sunlit window, "authentic Dharma and Eastern wisdom will never tell you to abandon the parents who raised you, never tell you to wreck a legitimate enterprise to buy plastic spheres, and never tell you to sit in a dark room waiting for windfalls. Whatever makes you more fearful, more divorced from common sense and law, and more psychologically controlled by a clique—no matter how sacred the vocabulary it borrows—is what the Ninth Great Vow of the Medicine Buddha Sutra calls a snare of Māra and a dense thicket of evil views!"</p>
<p>I opened my copy of the Medicine Buddha Sutra to the Ninth Great Vow and placed it on the table before her:</p>
<p>"Look at how the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata rescues those caught in thickets of distorted views: 'I shall guide and gather them into right view, gradually causing them to cultivate the practices of bodhisattvas.' What is right view (zhèngjiàn)? Right view is the sunlight pouring through this open window—respecting common sense and law, managing your business honestly, negotiating a repayment schedule with your suppliers, calling your parents to apologize, and creating real value step by step for your customers. That is the Bodhisattva path in the human world!"</p>
<p>That flood of midday sunlight, paired with the decisive clarity of the Ninth Great Vow, sliced through the fog that had enveloped her mind for eighteen months. Looking at the cheap plastic bases exposed in the sun, she buried her face in her hands and wept, subsequently working with her family and legal counsel to report the fraud, halt her losses, and step back into the daylight of honest work.</p>
<h4>II. Ropes, Whips, and Mental Prisons: Clarifying Principle Through the Book of Changes and the Steadiness of the Tenth Great Vow</h4>
<p>Immediately following the Ninth Vow's liberation from mental snares, the Tenth Great Vow turns toward some of the heaviest, most suffocating corners of mortal existence:</p>
<p>"If there are sentient beings who are subjected to royal laws, bound with ropes and scourged with whips, fettered and locked in dark prisons, or facing execution, together with countless other disasters and humiliations, tormented by grief and anxiety, suffering in body and mind—if they hear My name, by virtue of My merit and awesome spiritual power, they shall all be liberated from every sorrow and affliction."</p>
<p>In its original scriptural meaning, this vow addresses prisoners suffering legal punishment or wrongful imprisonment, as well as victims of war, disaster, and oppression who are "tormented by grief and anxiety, suffering in body and mind," promising that through the merit and spiritual power of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata their grievances may be redressed, their hearts reformed, and their afflictions relieved.</p>
<p>In modern life, when a person is suddenly struck by a commercial lawsuit, malicious framing, or public humiliation, the suffocating weight of "tormented by grief and anxiety, suffering in body and mind" can turn one's own home into an invisible prison.</p>
<p>One year, a veteran film producer friend whom I shall call Brother Fang was dragged into a two-year civil lawsuit after a business partner forged company seals, embezzled project funds, and absconded, leaving Brother Fang's accounts temporarily frozen under court preservation orders while rumors swirled through the industry. Normally a forthright, cheerful northerner, within three months his temples had turned half gray; he suffered from severe insomnia, could barely eat, and sank into deep despair.</p>
<p>When I heard of his situation, I brought a copy of the Medicine Buddha Sutra from Donglin Temple on Mount Lu to his home.</p>
<p>Here I wish to clarify a question many readers ask: <em>Having studied the family tradition of the Book of Changes (Zhouyi) since boyhood while holding fast to the Medicine Buddha Sutra's condemnation of charlatans who "falsely proclaim fortune and misfortune," how do classical Zhouyi philosophy and Medicine Buddha faith work together when a friend faces a real-world crisis?</em></p>
<p>That afternoon, as soon as Brother Fang saw me, he asked agitatedly: "Wenjie, cast a hexagram for me right away—or tell me whether I need to hire someone to hold a ritual to ward off this lawsuit!"</p>
<p>I shook my head and placed a steadying hand on his arm: "Brother Fang, lawsuits are resolved through law, evidence, and a clear head—never through superstitious rituals. Anyone who tells you that paying tens of thousands of yuan for a ritual can change a court judgment is the exact charlatan condemned in the Medicine Buddha Sutra!"</p>
<p>I took out my three bronze coins and cast a hexagram—Water over Mountain: Obstruction (Jiǎn) transforming into Water over Fire: After Completion (Jìjì)—not to pronounce fatalistic omens, but to use the Commentary on the Image ("On the mountain is water: Obstruction; the noble person reflects upon himself and cultivates virtue") and the structural principle of the "document line hidden beneath the first line waiting to emerge" to help a panicked friend calm down and review his evidence rationally:</p>
<p>"Look at the structure of Obstruction: danger lies ahead, which means you must stop lashing out in panic or arguing privately with the plaintiffs in anger; meanwhile, the document indicator lies intact at the foundation, reminding you that the original bank records and travel receipts proving you were filming on location in another province on the day the forged contract was signed have not vanished—they are sitting in your old production archives waiting to be retrieved!"</p>
<p>Next, I lit a stick of natural aloeswood incense on his desk, opened the Medicine Buddha Sutra to the Tenth Great Vow, and read it aloud with him three times.</p>
<p>"Brother Fang," I said, pointing to the words "tormented by grief and anxiety, suffering in body and mind—if they hear My name, by virtue of My merit and awesome spiritual power, they shall all be liberated from every sorrow and affliction," "the court and forensic examiners will establish the legal facts, but if you allow 'grief and anxiety' to destroy your health and mind first, who will work with your lawyers to dig out the archives? The Tenth Great Vow of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata grants you a lapis lazuli heart that refuses to collapse under sorrow; the Book of Changes gives you the calm perspective to organize human affairs amid difficulty. Settle your heart tonight, get a full night's sleep, and tomorrow morning sit down with your legal team to trace every bank voucher and travel log from three years ago!"</p>
<p>Those words broke the cycle of panic that had paralyzed him for months. Every morning he read the Tenth Great Vow to steady his breathing, and by day he worked methodically with his attorneys to retrieve archival vouchers and apply for judicial handwriting and seal forensics. Six months later, the absconded partner was apprehended, the forensic report proved the seal forgery beyond doubt, and the court lifted the asset freeze and exonerated Brother Fang.</p>
<p>Such is the proper boundary between classical Zhouyi wisdom, modern rule of law, and faith in the Medicine Buddha Sutra: <em>the Sutra anchors your spirit in luminous calm so you do not break under sorrow; the Book of Changes sharpens your self-reflection and timing in human action; and actual medical or legal problems are entrusted to qualified doctors and legal professionals.</em></p>
<h4>III. First Satisfy the Body with Exquisite Food, Then Establish It in the Flavor of the Dharma, and Bestow Garments and Music (The Eleventh and Twelfth Great Vows)</h4>
<p>If someone were to ask how the Twelve Great Vows of the Medicine Buddha Sutra conclude, conventional religious imagination might expect the final vows to grow ever more abstract and otherworldly.</p>
<p>Yet the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata does the exact opposite!</p>
<p>In the Eleventh and Twelfth Great Vows, the Medicine Buddha returns to the two simplest, warmest, and most aesthetic realities of human life—food and drink, and clothing, fragrance, music, and art!</p>
<p>Read the Eleventh Great Vow:</p>
<p>"If there are sentient beings who are tormented by hunger and thirst and create various evil karma in their struggle to obtain food—upon hearing My name and retaining it with focused heart, I shall first satisfy their bodies with exquisite food and drink, and afterward establish them in ultimate peace and joy through the flavor of the Dharma."</p>
<p>Who can read those words—"and create various evil karma in their struggle to obtain food" (wèi qiúshí gù, zào zhū èyè)—without being moved by the Buddha's compassion for the desperate?</p>
<p>Some wrongdoing in this world springs from insatiable cruelty; yet much human transgression arises from the torment of hunger and survival—like Jean Valjean in Victor Hugo's Les Misérables, who broke a bakery window to steal a loaf of bread for his sister's seven starving children (his nephews and nieces). Facing those driven to the brink by hunger and thirst, the Medicine Buddha does not lift a rod of condemnation; He vows:</p>
<p>"I shall first satisfy their bodies with exquisite food and drink, and afterward establish them in ultimate peace and joy through the flavor of the Dharma!"</p>
<p>First set a bowl of hot, nourishing food before the starving traveler so his stomach no longer burns with hunger; only after his body is warm and his dignity restored, gently offer him the ambrosia of truth (the flavor of the Dharma) so he may stand in lasting peace.</p>
<p>And in the Twelfth Great Vow, the Medicine Buddha elevates this tenderness to the realm of beauty and culture:</p>
<p>"If there are sentient beings who are destitute and without clothing, tormented day and night by mosquitoes, gadflies, cold, and heat—if they hear My name and retain it with focused heart, according to their wishes they shall immediately obtain all manner of exquisite garments, as well as all precious adornments, flower garlands, fragrant ointments, drum music, and varied arts, fulfilling whatever their hearts desire."</p>
<p>As someone who once presented the craft of garments on the runway, acted in the art of cinema (drum music and varied arts), and now reads and burns quiet incense in my study (flower garlands and fragrant ointments), I feel a deep sense of kinship every time I read the Twelfth Great Vow. When you look up at the all-pervading azure light of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, you realize that in His Pure Lapis Lazuli World, mountain breezes, clean garments, floral scents, noble music, and honest cinema—so long as they are uncorrupted by greed and delusion—are all manifestations of the Medicine Buddha's compassionate vows, all cooling medicines that comfort weary human souls.</p>
<h4>IV. Sunlight Pervading Everywhere and Moonlight Pervading Everywhere: The Sun of Li on the Left Shoulder and the Moon of Kan on the Right</h4>
<p>Immediately after proclaiming the Twelve Great Vows, the Buddha describes the Eastern Pure Lapis Lazuli World and introduces the two chief Bodhisattvas who assist the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata:</p>
<p>"Within His land there are two bodhisattva-mahāsattvas: the first is named Sunlight Pervading Everywhere (Sūryaprabha), and the second is named Moonlight Pervading Everywhere (Candraprabha). They stand at the head of that immeasurable, countless assembly of bodhisattvas, next in succession to fill the Buddha-seat, and are fully able to uphold the treasury of the True Dharma of that World-Honored Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata."</p>
<p>Flanked on the left by Sunlight Pervading Everywhere Bodhisattva and on the right by Moonlight Pervading Everywhere Bodhisattva, with the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata seated in the center—these are the Three Holy Ones of the East (Dōngfāng Sānshèng).</p>
<p>In the original scriptural meaning, Sunlight and Moonlight are the two chief Bodhisattvas of the Eastern Pure Lapis Lazuli World who uphold the Medicine Tathāgata's Dharma treasury and will succeed to Buddhahood in turn. And when we contemplate their symbolism alongside the Discussion of the Trigrams (Shuōguà Zhuàn) in the Book of Changes, why are the two chief attendants of the Medicine Buddha specifically Sunlight and Moonlight?</p>
<p>One autumn evening in Hong Kong after wrapping the final shot of a lookbook, I climbed alone to Victoria Peak. It was the fifteenth day of the lunar month at dusk, and a rare simultaneous shining of sun and moon appeared above Victoria Harbour: on the western horizon, the setting sun cast a warm crimson-gold glow across the sea, while above the eastern ridge a cool, silver full moon had already risen into the indigo sky.</p>
<p>Standing in the sea breeze atop the peak, gazing at those two luminaries—one warm, one cool—beneath a sky as deep blue as lapis lazuli, I saw the balance of the Three Holy Ones of the East within human cultivation:</p>
<p>When you cultivate the Medicine Buddha's teaching and let your fundamental mind become as deep and inclusive as pure lapis lazuli, upon your left shoulder rises Sunlight Pervading Everywhere Bodhisattva—corresponding in the Shuōguà Zhuàn of the Book of Changes to the trigram Li ("Li is fire, is the sun"), hollow and receptive within yet radiant without, embodying warm compassion, active service, and fearless illumination of the dark. Upon your right shoulder rises Moonlight Pervading Everywhere Bodhisattva—corresponding in the Shuōguà Zhuàn to the trigram Kan ("Kan is water, is the moon"), gentle without yet firm within, embodying cool prajñā stillness, solitary self-examination, and clear-headed composure amid the boiling vanity fair. When the sun of Li and the moon of Kan shine in harmony around a lapis lazuli heart, they form the hexagram Water over Fire: After Completion (Jìjì).</p>
<h4>V. From "Māra's Net and Dark Prisons" to "Garlands and Joyful Music": Wholeness of Human Flourishing in the Medicine Buddha's Eyes</h4>
<p>When we survey the Ninth, Tenth, Eleventh, and Twelfth Great Vows together with Sunlight and Moonlight Bodhisattvas as a single tapestry, a breathtaking staircase of compassionate salvation comes into view:</p>
<p>The Ninth Great Vow (Escaping Māra's Net into Right View) rescues our cognition and thought—freeing the mind from fanaticism, superstition, and psychological manipulation into clear rationality;</p>
<p>The Tenth Great Vow (Liberation from Prisons, Disasters, and Grief) rescues our circumstance and emotion—bestowing unshakeable inner steadiness amid legal, social, or emotional tempests;</p>
<p>The Eleventh Great Vow (First Exquisite Food and Drink, Then the Flavor of the Dharma) rescues our physical survival and spiritual life—ensuring bodily nourishment before imparting liberating truth;</p>
<p>The Twelfth Great Vow (Exquisite Garments, Adornments, Fragrances, and Music) fulfills our dignity and aesthetic joy—affirming that a life of cleanliness, beauty, fragrance, and noble art is blessed by the Buddha.</p>
<p>Together, they unite the warm abundance of Sunlight with the cool clarity of Moonlight around the azure throne of Bhaiṣajyaguru.</p>
<h3 id="ch8">Chapter 8: The Miser's Empty Granary and the Arrogant Scholar's Delusion (The Fourfold Admonition of the Sutra: Overcoming Avarice, Spiritual Pride, Envious Servitude, and Malicious Strife)</h3>
<h4>Scriptural Text</h4>
<p>At that time, the World-Honored One again spoke to the youth Mañjuśrī, saying: "Mañjuśrī! There are sentient beings who do not discern good from evil, who harbor only greed and stinginess, who do not know of giving or the fruits of giving, foolish and devoid of wisdom, lacking the root of faith. They accumulate great stores of wealth and treasure and guard them with anxious diligence. Seeing a beggar approach, their hearts feel no joy; and if they are left with no choice but to give, it feels like slicing flesh from their own bodies, arousing deep pain and regret. Moreover, there are countless miserly and greedy beings who amass wealth and property that they do not even bear to use for themselves—how much less could they give to their parents, wives, children, servants, laborers, or mendicants who come asking? Those beings, upon the end of this life, are reborn in the realm of hungry ghosts or among beasts; yet because in their former human life they once briefly heard the name of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, now while in evil destinies they briefly recall that Tathāgata's name, and at the very moment of recollection they vanish from that place and are reborn among humans. They regain remembrance of past lives, dread the sufferings of evil destinies, take no delight in sensual indulgence, gladly practice generous giving, praise those who give, and begrudge nothing they possess...</p>
<p>Furthermore, Mañjuśrī! If there are sentient beings who, though receiving training precepts from the Tathāgata, break moral discipline (śīla); or some who do not break moral discipline yet violate proper deportment and rules; or some who preserve moral discipline and deportment intact yet destroy right view; or some who do not destroy right view yet abandon wide learning, failing to comprehend the profound meaning of the sutras spoken by the Buddha; or some who possess wide learning yet harbor overweening pride (abhimāna)—because their hearts are clouded by overweening pride, they affirm only themselves and disparage others, slander the True Dharma, and become companions of Māra. Such foolish persons practice evil views themselves and further cause countless koṭis of sentient beings to plunge into a great, perilous pit...</p>
<p>Furthermore, Mañjuśrī! If there are sentient beings who are greedy and envious, praising themselves and disparaging others, they shall fall into the three evil destinies and endure acute suffering for immeasurable thousands of years; after enduring acute suffering, upon the end of that life they are reborn in the human world as oxen, horses, camels, or donkeys, constantly flogged with whips, tormented by hunger and thirst, forever carrying heavy loads along the road; or if they obtain human form, they are born in lowly station as servants and bondmaids driven by others, never enjoying autonomy. Yet if in a former human life they once heard the name of the World-Honored Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, by virtue of that wholesome cause they now recall it again and take refuge with wholehearted sincerity; through the Buddha's spiritual power all their sufferings are dispelled, their faculties become keen and bright, wise and widely learned, constantly seeking supreme Dharma, always meeting good spiritual friends, forever severing the snares of Māra, breaking open the shell of ignorance, drying up the river of afflictions, and liberating themselves from all birth, aging, sickness, death, grief, and torment.</p>
<p>Furthermore, Mañjuśrī! If there are sentient beings who delight in division and estrangement, engaging in mutual strife and litigation, troubling themselves and others, creating and increasing all manner of evil karma with body, speech, and mind, constantly plotting mutual harm; summoning spirits of mountains, forests, trees, and tombs; killing living creatures to take their flesh and blood in sacrifice to yakṣas and rākṣasas; writing down the names of enemies and fashioning their effigies to curse them with malicious spells; practicing hexes, gu-poison, and corpse-raising sorcery to cut short their lives and destroy their bodies—if those sentient beings hear the name of this Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, none of those malicious deeds can harm them; all involved turn about and give rise to hearts of loving-kindness, benefiting and comforting one another without intent to harm or resentment, each rejoicing and feeling content with what they have, neither invading nor humiliating one another, but mutually bringing benefit."</p>
<h4>I. Giving That Feels Like Slicing Flesh and the Hungry-Ghost Sickness of "Not Even Bearing to Use It for Oneself"</h4>
<p>After the Buddha finished proclaiming the Twelve Great Vows and the splendors of the Pure Lapis Lazuli World beneath the Music Tree of Vaiśālī, many kings, merchants, brāhmaṇas, and householders in the assembly listened with glowing faces.</p>
<p>Everyone loves to hear the Third Great Vow's "inexhaustible provisions" and the Eleventh and Twelfth Great Vows' "exquisite food, garments, garlands, and music." Yet as the Supreme Physician determined to cure the root of mortal suffering, right after offering sweet tonic the Buddha takes up four golden acupuncture needles—four consecutive passages beginning "Furthermore, Mañjuśrī!"—to pierce the four chronic tumors that block human beings from receiving true abundance: first, miserly greed; second, rule-breaking and the pride of erudition; third, envy and self-exaltation; and fourth, mutual litigation and malicious cursing.</p>
<p>Look first at the portrait of the miser painted by the Buddha two thousand five hundred years ago—it reads like an X-ray taken inside a modern metropolis:</p>
<p>"They accumulate great stores of wealth and treasure and guard them with anxious diligence. Seeing a beggar approach, their hearts feel no joy; and if they are left with no choice but to give, it feels like slicing flesh from their own bodies, arousing deep pain and regret. Moreover, there are countless miserly and greedy beings who amass wealth and property that they do not even bear to use for themselves—how much less could they give to their parents, wives, children, servants, laborers, or mendicants who come asking?"</p>
<p>During my years working in cultural enterprises in southern China, I encountered more than one person suffering from this exact condition.</p>
<p>Once, an elderly hardware-manufacturing owner in the Pearl River Delta worth tens of millions—let us call him Uncle Chen—asked a mutual acquaintance to invite me to examine the spatial layout of his residence and factory.</p>
<p>Uncle Chen was in his sixties and lived in a spacious sea-view apartment, yet the moment I stepped inside his front door I smelled a musty dampness reminiscent of that Beijing basement years ago. To save electricity, he refused to turn on the air conditioning even in the scorching dog days of summer; the expensive rosewood sofa in his living room was still wrapped in the yellowed, torn plastic film from ten years earlier, patched over and over with clear packing tape; the refrigerator was crammed with leftovers from three days prior, and the collar of his undershirt was frayed at the seam.</p>
<p>More painful still was his extreme stinginess toward his family and workers. When his wife was hospitalized and her physician recommended a self-paid targeted medication, he balked at the cost and argued loudly in the hospital ward; when a veteran machine operator who had worked in his factory for over a decade injured a finger on a punch press, Uncle Chen haggled endlessly to avoid paying a few thousand yuan in nutritional support; whenever social custom forced him to contribute to a charity or wedding, it was literally "like slicing flesh from his own body, arousing deep pain and regret," leaving him sighing and grumbling for days.</p>
<p>After walking through the factory and apartment, Uncle Chen rubbed his hands anxiously and asked: "Teacher Wenjie, is some corner of my apartment leaking wealth qi? Why have I felt so panicky these past two years, dreaming every night that thieves are carting away my money, while my son and daughter refuse to come home and treat me like a stranger? What feng-shui object should I place to fix it?"</p>
<p>I slipped my compass back into its cloth pouch, declined any fee, looked at the thrice-brewed pale tea in a yellowed paper cup on his table, and spoke to him frankly:</p>
<p>"Uncle Chen, your apartment isn't leaking a single coin, and you don't need to buy a single ornament. Classical Zhouyi philosophy teaches that 'great virtue carries all things' (hòudé zàiwù)—the auspiciousness of a home is the reflection of its inhabitants' hearts and family ethos. Your problem is not a leak in the reservoir; it is that water only enters and never flows out, until the water inside has turned stagnant."</p>
<p>He stared at me blankly: "The water has turned stagnant?"</p>
<p>"In classical Chinese, money is called quánhuò ('spring-currency') or tōnghuò ('circulating-currency') because, like spring water or blood in the body, only when it circulates to nourish where it is needed does it remain living water," I said, pointing to the yellowed plastic tape on his rosewood sofa. "In the Medicine Buddha Sutra, the Buddha described a condition called 'amassing wealth and property that they do not even bear to use for themselves—how much less could they give to their parents, wives, children, servants, or laborers?' In the original scriptural meaning, such habits lead to rebirth in the realm of hungry ghosts; and in terms of present-day mindfulness, when a person sits atop tens of millions yet cannot bear to eat a fresh meal, denies his sick wife proper medicine, shortchanges an injured veteran worker, and chills his own children's hearts—has he not already locked himself inside the hungry-ghost realm while still alive?"</p>
<p>My words left Uncle Chen flushed and silent for a long moment.</p>
<p>"If you want the energy of your home to turn auspicious, you don't need to knock down a wall or buy bronze toads," I said as I rose to leave. "Go home and peel the plastic film off your sofa, go to the hospital and sign for the medicine your wife's doctor recommended, pay that injured worker double his nutritional allowance, and give the workers who built your factory a generous year-end bonus. The day you stop treating kindness to your family and workers like slicing flesh from your body, the energy of your home will come alive on its own."</p>
<p>When the azure light of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata awakens in a miserly heart, wealth ceases to be an iron chain locking the soul in poverty and becomes a vessel of warmth and generosity.</p>
<h4>II. The Five Flaws of Practitioners: Why Do Some Grow More Ill-Tempered the More Sutras They Read?</h4>
<p>In the second "Furthermore, Mañjuśrī!" passage, the Buddha turns His penetrating gaze toward the hidden ailments of religious practitioners, scholars, and cultural figures themselves:</p>
<p><em>The first level (Breaking moral discipline / pò shīluó)</em>: claiming to follow the Buddha's teachings while secretly violating fundamental precepts;</p>
<p><em>The second level (Violating proper deportment / pò guǐzé)</em>: keeping major precepts on paper while acting with coarse, inconsiderate manners that repel others;</p>
<p><em>The third level (Destroying right view / huǐ zhèngjiàn)</em>: keeping outward rules strictly while filling the mind with distorted, superstitious views;</p>
<p><em>The fourth level (Abandoning wide learning / qì duōwén)</em>: having a well-meaning heart yet refusing to study deeply, reducing profound scriptural wisdom to blind, literal slogans;</p>
<p><em>The fifth level (Wide learning with overweening pride / duōwén zēngshàngmàn): the subtlest and most perilous flaw of all—keeping precepts, observing rules, and reading ten thousand scrolls (possessing wide learning), yet developing overweening spiritual pride (abhimāna</em>)!</p>
<p>What is overweening pride (zēngshàngmàn)?</p>
<p>Because a person has read a few more scriptures, learned a bit of metaphysical terminology, kept a vegetarian diet for a season, or sat in meditation, he turns sacred knowledge into a pedestal for looking down upon humanity! "Because their hearts are clouded by overweening pride, they affirm only themselves and disparage others, slander the True Dharma, and become companions of Māra."</p>
<p>Many self-styled seekers, when they first enter the mountains or open classical texts, look down from on high at ordinary people in the marketplace—at parents toiling day and night for their children's schooling, at ordinary men and women weeping over partings and daily struggles—dismissing them as ignorant worldlings. Only after they themselves have been bruised by the storms of mortal life, wept beside a loved one's sickbed, and looked up into the lapis lazuli eyes of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata—eyes that never despise a single ordinary mortal—do they drop their brittle arrogance and realize that a humble parent who truly knows love, pain, and kindness stands far closer to the Medicine Buddha's alms bowl than a scholar whose chest holds only cold concepts and superiority.</p>
<h4>III. "Breaking Open the Shell of Ignorance and Drying Up the River of Afflictions": From the Whip of Envy to Autonomous Freedom</h4>
<p>In the third "Furthermore, Mañjuśrī!" passage, the Buddha exposes the third chronic sickness of the human heart—envy and praising oneself while disparaging others (qiāntān jídù, zìzàn huǐtā):</p>
<p>"If there are sentient beings who are greedy and envious, praising themselves and disparaging others... they are reborn in the human world as oxen, horses, camels, or donkeys, constantly flogged with whips, tormented by hunger and thirst, forever carrying heavy loads along the road; or if they obtain human form, they are born in lowly station as servants and bondmaids driven by others, never enjoying autonomy. Yet if in a former human life they once heard the name of the World-Honored Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata... through the Buddha's spiritual power all their sufferings are dispelled... forever severing the snares of Māra, breaking open the shell of ignorance, drying up the river of afflictions."</p>
<p>In its original scriptural meaning, this passage sets forth the karmic consequence of envy and slander across rebirths. And when we hold this mirror up to the modern vanity fair and workplace in present-day contemplation, is not "constantly flogged with whips, tormented by hunger and thirst, forever carrying heavy loads along the road... driven by others, never enjoying autonomy" the exact psychological condition of anyone enslaved right now by envy and relentless status comparison?</p>
<p>Ask yourself honestly: when your heart is consumed by envy and the compulsion to outshine others (praising oneself and disparaging others), when you cannot bear to see a colleague succeed and carry a thousand-pound load of vanity and anxiety on your back every day—flogged forward by the whip of comparison without daring to pause for a breath of water—how does your inner state differ from a beast of burden driven along the road without a moment's autonomy?</p>
<p>What turns a free human being into a driven bondservant? It is envy, self-centered narrowness, and what the Sutra calls the "shell of ignorance" (wúmíng què—where què means a hard eggshell enclosing the unhatched bird)!</p>
<p>And the sacred name of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata is the gentle yet firm tap from outside the shell: "Forever severing the snares of Māra, breaking open the shell of ignorance, drying up the river of afflictions!"</p>
<p>For a fledgling bird to soar into the blue sky, its first task is not to resent other birds for flying high, but to peck open from within that hard shell of envy, self-centeredness, and insecurity. The moment the shell cracks open and the azure radiance of the Medicine Buddha pours in, you discover that when you can rejoice sincerely in the good fortune of others, no force on earth can enslave your spirit.</p>
<h4>IV. Turning Hostility into Loving-Kindness: When Litigation and Curses Extinguish in Lapis Lazuli Light</h4>
<p>In the fourth "Furthermore, Mañjuśrī!" passage, the Buddha addresses the most destructive sickness in human relationships—estrangement, litigation, and malicious cursing (hàoxǐ guāilí, gèngxiāng dòusòng... shū yuànrén míng, zuò qí xíngxiàng, yǐ è zhòushù ér zhòuzǔ zhī):</p>
<p>In ancient times, when people hated a rival, in addition to court battles they resorted to dark sorcery—writing an enemy's name on an effigy and pronouncing curses. In our day, people may no longer sacrifice to forest spirits, yet mutual litigation, paid online smear campaigns, and even hiring charlatans to cast hexes against former business partners or spouses still play out in boardrooms and broken homes.</p>
<p>One year in Guangzhou, two partners who had co-founded a garment supply-chain business—let us call them Old Zhao and Old Zhou—fell into a bitter deadlock over equity and accounts. Hearing a rumor that his partner had consulted a folk sorcerer to "suppress his luck," Old Zhao grew so terrified and enraged that he prepared to spend a large sum hiring another self-styled "master" to strike back. Before doing so, he came to ask my advice on the Book of Changes.</p>
<p>After listening to him, I asked a single question: "Old Zhao, if someone splashes muddy water on your coat and, to take revenge, you jump into a ditch to grab two handfuls of mire to hurl back at him, who ends up covered in stench first?"</p>
<p>Old Zhao froze.</p>
<p>I opened the Medicine Buddha Sutra and read aloud to him the concluding lines of this passage:</p>
<p>"If those sentient beings hear the name of this Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, none of those malicious deeds can harm them; all involved turn about and give rise to hearts of loving-kindness, benefiting and comforting one another without intent to harm or resentment, each rejoicing and feeling content with what they have, neither invading nor humiliating one another, but mutually bringing benefit."</p>
<p>"Look how clearly the Buddha states the law of energy," I told him. "In this world, any malicious curse, rumor, or scheme can disturb your mind only if your own heart is already filled with suspicion, hatred, and greed—opening the door from inside to welcome dark hostility! When you take refuge in the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, keeping your mind like a seamless sphere of pure lapis lazuli, refusing to stoop to dark retaliation and instead wishing that both families may settle accounts fairly and live in peace, any malicious curse striking your upright lapis lazuli light vanishes like a snowflake falling onto a warm hearth (none of those malicious deeds can harm them)!"</p>
<p>Old Zhao took those words to heart, canceled the absurd "counter-ritual," and invited his former partner Old Zhou out for a pot of Chaozhou gongfu tea with a certified public accountant. Laying every ledger openly on the table, Old Zhao voluntarily yielded three percentage points of disputed margin. Seeing the calm openness in Old Zhao's eyes, Old Zhou—who had arrived with bloodshot eyes ready for war—felt his taut shoulders drop, his eyes reddening as he admitted: "Old Zhao, to tell the truth, I haven't slept a sound night in six months either."</p>
<p>That afternoon, a feud that had threatened to ruin two enterprises dissolved over a pot of clear tea in the warm light of the Medicine Buddha's teaching: "each rejoicing and feeling content with what they have, neither invading nor humiliating one another, but mutually bringing benefit."</p>
<h4>V. Four Ancient Mirrors Illuminating the Heart: From Greed, Pride, Envy, and Strife to the Four Immeasurables</h4>
<p>Stepping back to view these four consecutive "Furthermore, Mañjuśrī!" admonitions in Chapter 8, we see that they correspond with surgical precision to the four deepest pitfalls of mortal life—and are healed directly by the Four Immeasurable Minds (Loving-Kindness, Compassion, Empathetic Joy, and Equanimity) taught throughout the Medicine Buddha Sutra:</p>
<p><em>The First Mirror reflects Miserly Greed (wéihuái tānlìn)—cured by Equanimity and Generous Giving (Shě)</em>, letting wealth circulate like living spring water to nourish family and society;</p>
<p><em>The Second Mirror reflects Precept-Breaking and the Pride of Erudition (duōwén zēngshàngmàn)—cured by Humble Right View and Sincere Practice</em>, where deeper learning yields gentler humility before the Medicine Buddha's alms bowl;</p>
<p><em>The Third Mirror reflects Envy and Self-Exaltation (qiāntān jídù, zìzàn huǐtā)—cured by Empathetic Joy (Xǐ), pecking open the hard eggshell of ego (wúmíng què</em>) by rejoicing wholeheartedly in others' goodness;</p>
<p><em>The Fourth Mirror reflects Division, Litigation, and Malicious Cursing (gèngxiāng dòusòng)—cured by Loving-Kindness and Compassion (Cí yǔ Bēi)</em>, disarming hostility through content integrity and mutual benefit.</p>
<h3 id="ch9">Chapter 9: The Returning Boat of the Eight Great Bodhisattvas and the Daily Lapis Lazuli Altar (Guidance to the Pure Land, Non-Duality of East and West, and Reverence for the Dharma Jewel)</h3>
<h4>Scriptural Text</h4>
<p>"Furthermore, Mañjuśrī! If among the fourfold assembly—bhikṣus, bhikṣuṇīs, upāsakas, upāsikās—and other men and women of pure faith, there are those who uphold the eightfold fast precepts, whether for a year or for three months upholding the training rules, and by virtue of this wholesome root vow to be reborn in the Western Land of Ultimate Bliss before Amitāyus Buddha to hear the True Dharma, yet whose resolve is not yet firmly settled: if they hear the name of the World-Honored Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, at the approach of death eight great bodhisattvas—namely Mañjuśrī Bodhisattva, Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva, Mahāsthāmaprāpta Bodhisattva, Akṣayamati Bodhisattva, Ratnacandana-puṣpa Bodhisattva, Bhaiṣajyarāja Bodhisattva, Bhaiṣajyasamudgata Bodhisattva, and Maitreya Bodhisattva—shall come flying through the sky to show them the way, and they shall immediately be born by transformation amidst multicolored jeweled lotus blossoms in that land...</p>
<p>Furthermore, Mañjuśrī! If there are men and women of pure faith who hear all the names of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, Arhat, Samyaksaṃbuddha, and upon hearing them recite and uphold them—chewing the tooth-wood at dawn, bathing and rinsing until clean, offering various fragrant flowers, burned incense, anointing incense, and varied musical arts before His image; and regarding this classic Sutra, whether copying it themselves or instructing others to copy it, wholeheartedly upholding it and listening to its meaning...</p>
<p>At that time, the youth Mañjuśrī said to the Buddha: 'World-Honored One! I vow that in the turning of the Semblance Dharma Age, by means of manifold skillful means I shall cause men and women of pure faith to hear the name of the World-Honored Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, even awakening their ears with the Buddha's name in their sleep. World-Honored One! If anyone upholds, reads, and recites this Sutra, or expounds and reveals it to others; whether copying it themselves or instructing others to copy it; revering and honoring it with various flower scents, anointing incense, powdered incense, burned incense, garlands, necklaces, banners, canopies, and music; making a bag of five-colored silk to hold it; sweeping and sprinkling a clean place, setting up a high seat, and placing it securely thereon—at that time the Four Heavenly Kings with their retinues and countless hundreds of thousands of other devas shall all approach that place to make offerings and stand guard.' ...</p>
<p>Ānanda replied: 'Virtuous World-Honored One! I harbor no doubt concerning the sutras spoken by the Tathāgata. Why is this so? Because all bodily, verbal, and mental deeds of the Tathāgatas are wholly pure. World-Honored One! These orbs of sun and moon may be made to fall, the King of Mount Sumeru may be shaken from its base, yet the words of the Buddhas never vary!'"</p>
<h4>I. The Evening Ferry from Shekou Pier: Sunrise in the East and Sunset in the West Belong to One Ocean</h4>
<p>In the years after descending from Donglin Temple on Mount Lu, I shifted the center of my daily life to Lingnan.</p>
<p>Living between Shenzhen and Guangzhou, and traveling frequently to Hong Kong for cultural and publishing work, my favorite crossing has always been the late-afternoon high-speed ferry from Shekou Pier in Shenzhen to Central or the Macau Ferry Terminal in Hong Kong.</p>
<p>The voyage takes roughly fifty minutes.</p>
<p>As the ferry clears Shenzhen Bay and cuts across the confluence of the Pearl River estuary and the Lingdingyang sea, the sky enters the most luminous hour of the day. Ahead to the west, the setting sun sinks like a colossal lotus of burnished gold behind the silhouette of Lantau Island, dyeing the western clouds and waves a serene, homecoming crimson-gold; yet when you turn to look back toward the eastern horizon behind the stern, the rising twilight sky reveals a deep, translucent, sapphire expanse—the exact hue of the Eastern Pure Lapis Lazuli World.</p>
<p>Once, standing by the cabin window, a friend gazed at the contrasting colors of East and West and asked me:</p>
<p>"Wenjie, I have always wondered about something. Your faith in the Medicine Buddha was awakened before that statue of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli World-Honored One at Donglin Temple on Mount Lu. Yet everyone knows Donglin Temple is the ancestral court of the Pure Land tradition founded by Master Huiyuan in the Eastern Jin Dynasty, dedicated for sixteen centuries to Amitābha Buddha of the Western Land of Ultimate Bliss; whereas because you share the birthday of the thirtieth of the ninth lunar month with the Medicine Buddha, you devote yourself to the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata of the Eastern Pure Lapis Lazuli World. One is in the East, one is in the West; how do the two relate?"</p>
<p>Watching the white foam and golden-blue reflections cloven by the ferry's wake, I smiled.</p>
<p>"Look at this sea beneath us," I said, pointing toward bow and stern. "Where the sun rises is called the East; where the sun sets is called the West. Yet whether it is the morning sun rising in the East or the evening sun setting in the West, do they not illuminate the very same ocean?"</p>
<p>In the Medicine Buddha Sutra, the Buddha answers this age-old question of East and West with breathtaking breadth and freedom from sectarian boundary:</p>
<p>On the one hand, the Buddha declares that the Eastern Pure Lapis Lazuli World—"with lapis lazuli as its ground, golden cords marking its roads, and city walls, palaces, pavilions, windows, and lattices all formed of the seven precious gems—is in its meritorious adornments wholly equal to, and no different from, the Western Land of Ultimate Bliss." On the other hand, the Buddha specifically adds: if practitioners uphold the eightfold fast precepts and vow to be reborn in the Western Land of Ultimate Bliss before Amitāyus (Amitābha) Buddha, yet their resolve is not yet firmly settled, so long as they hear and uphold the sacred name of the Eastern Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, at the hour of death the Medicine Buddha displays not a trace of territorial possessiveness—instead, Eight Great Bodhisattvas fly through the sky to guide their path and escort them directly into the seven-jeweled lotus blossoms of the Western Pure Land!</p>
<p>Narrow-minded sectarians love to erect fences: "If you enter my gate, you must not read another scroll; if you pray for healing in the East, you compromise rebirth in the West." Yet in the eyes of the Tathāgatas, the Eastern Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata and the Western Amitābha Tathāgata are the complete day and night, the setting sail and the returning to port, of a single life:</p>
<p>The East is the direction of sunrise—representing vitality, dawn, present-life cultivation, and healing. So long as you still breathe in this human world and face daily work, physical illness, and human relationships, you need the Twelve Great Vows of the Eastern Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata so your body and mind remain clear, nourished, upright, and compassionate.</p>
<p>The West is the direction of sunset—representing autumn harvest, evening rest, and ultimate homecoming. When you walk the eastern daylight journey with lapis lazuli clarity and fulfill your mortal responsibilities, then at the hour when the sun sinks behind the western peaks and the boat nears harbor, the Eight Great Bodhisattvas naturally light up like eight lighthouses across the sea to guide your vessel safely into port.</p>
<h4>II. The Compass of the Eight Great Bodhisattvas: Scriptural Guidance at Life's Close and Contemplative Bearings at Every Dark Crossing</h4>
<p>In the original scriptural meaning of the Medicine Buddha Sutra, the Eight Great Bodhisattvas—Mañjuśrī, Avalokiteśvara, Mahāsthāmaprāpta, Akṣayamati, Ratnacandana-puṣpa, Bhaiṣajyarāja, Bhaiṣajyasamudgata, and Maitreya—appear at the close of a practitioner's life (at the approach of death) to guide consciousness across the threshold into the Pure Land, or ensure fortunate rebirth free from evil destinies.</p>
<p>And from a modern contemplative perspective, an awakening seeker often passes through multiple spiritual "deaths and rebirths" within a single lifetime—first outgrowing the naïve shell of youth to enter the wide world; second outgrowing aloof pride to enter the crucible of mortal responsibility; and third, beneath the compassionate gaze of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, outgrowing exhaustion and grasping to become a lamp-bearer at the mortal crossing.</p>
<p>Whenever we pass through a dark crossing of transition in life and call upon the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata and the Eight Great Bodhisattvas, their names and qualities awaken eight dimensions of inner strength:</p>
<p>First, Mañjuśrī Bodhisattva: awakening prajñā clarity amid emotional storms so we see reality without self-deception;</p>
<p>Second, Avalokiteśvara (Guanyin) Bodhisattva: awakening tender compassion toward ourselves and others, ending self-flagellation;</p>
<p>Third, Mahāsthāmaprāpta (Dedashi) Bodhisattva: awakening steadfast strength and decisive action to take the first step of rebuilding after tears dry;</p>
<p>Fourth, Akṣayamati (Wujinyi) Bodhisattva: awakening inexhaustible aspiration and hope that perceives green shoots beneath frozen winter soil;</p>
<p>Fifth, Ratnacandana-puṣpa (Baotanhua) Bodhisattva: awakening the fragrant moral dignity of sandalwood blossoms even in adversity;</p>
<p>Sixth and Seventh, Bhaiṣajyarāja (Medicine King) and Bhaiṣajyasamudgata (Medicine Supreme) Bodhisattvas: awakening comprehensive care for physical and spiritual wounds—reminding us to follow proper medical care for the body while healing the heart with wholesome mindfulness;</p>
<p>Eighth, Maitreya Bodhisattva: awakening vast forgiveness, magnanimity, and future joy to turn a new page of life.</p>
<h4>III. "Chewing Tooth-Wood at Dawn" and "Holding the Sutra in a Five-Colored Silk Bag": Reverence for the Dharma Jewel and Clean Daily Living</h4>
<p>Friends who finish reading the grand vows in the first half of the Medicine Buddha Sutra often ask me: "Wenjie, in ordinary daily life, how should a layperson respectfully uphold the Medicine Buddha Sutra?"</p>
<p>The answer lies right inside the two passages cited in Chapter 9—first, the Buddha's instruction to Mañjuśrī ("chewing the tooth-wood at dawn, bathing and rinsing until clean, offering various fragrant flowers, burned incense, anointing incense, and varied musical arts before His image; and regarding this classic Sutra, whether copying it themselves or instructing others to copy it, wholeheartedly upholding it and listening to its meaning"); and second, Mañjuśrī the Youth's vow to protect the scripture ("making a bag of five-colored silk to hold it; sweeping and sprinkling a clean place, setting up a high seat, and placing it securely thereon").</p>
<p>Notice where daily practice in the Medicine Buddha tradition begins:</p>
<p>It begins with the very first act upon waking each morning—"chewing the tooth-wood at dawn, bathing and rinsing until clean" (chén jiáo chǐmù, zǎoshù qīngjìng)! In ancient India, chewing a clean willow or neem twig (danta-kāṣṭha) was the daily practice of brushing the teeth and purifying the mouth, followed by washing face and hands. Why does a supreme Mahāyāna scripture write "brush your teeth and wash your face cleanly every morning" directly into its practice guidelines? Because the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata is the Supreme Physician, and He knows that physical hygiene and orderly daily habits are the bedrock of health and mental clarity.</p>
<p>Next, examine Mañjuśrī's phrase: "making a bag of five-colored silk to hold it; sweeping and sprinkling a clean place, setting up a high seat, and placing it securely thereon" (yǐ wǔsè cǎi, zuò náng shèng zhī; sǎosǎ jìngchù, fūshè gāozuò, ér yòng ānchù).</p>
<p>Here we must state the exact original scriptural meaning clearly: in "making a bag of five-colored silk to hold it," the pronoun "it" (zhī) refers specifically to <em>the scroll or volume of this Medicine Buddha Sutra (the Dharma Jewel)! In ancient times, after practitioners copied or received a precious scroll of the Medicine Buddha Sutra, to express supreme reverence for the Dharma Jewel and protect the scripture from dust and dampness, they sewed a protective scripture bag of five-colored silk brocade (shèngjīng cǎináng), placed the Sutra volume reverently inside the five-colored bag, swept the room spotless (sweeping and sprinkling a clean place), and enshrined the scripture upon an elevated, clean desk or altar (setting up a high seat, and placing it securely thereon</em>).</p>
<p>While this tradition of honoring sacred texts in five-colored brocade bags later resonated in East Asian folk culture with the wearing of protective scripture pouches and herbal sachets, in the Medicine Buddha Sutra itself it first teaches us reverence for the Dharma scroll as though facing the Buddha Himself, paired with spotless cleanliness of one's living space.</p>
<p>When you wake each morning, open the curtains, brush your teeth and wash your face cleanly, put on fresh clothes, sweep your study desk free of dust, place the Medicine Buddha Sutra respectfully on a clean high shelf, light a stick of natural incense, and read and reflect upon its meaning with a heart free of grime and anger, your ordinary study room becomes a radiant lapis lazuli sanctuary guarded by the Four Heavenly Kings.</p>
<h4>IV. "Even Awakening Their Ears with the Buddha's Name in Their Sleep" and Ānanda's Vow: Dissolving Nightmares and Unshakeable Trust</h4>
<p>Two further gems shine in this section of the Sutra: first, Mañjuśrī's tender promise—"even awakening their ears with the Buddha's name in their sleep" (nǎizhì shuìzhōng yì yǐ Fómíng juéwù qí ěr), followed by the Buddha's assurance that wholehearted mindfulness of the Medicine Buddha causes bad dreams and ominous signs to vanish and brings comfort and safety to women enduring the intense pain of childbirth; and second, the Venerable Ānanda's ringing declaration of faith:</p>
<p>"World-Honored One! These orbs of sun and moon may be made to fall, the King of Mount Sumeru may be shaken from its base, yet the words of the Buddhas never vary!"</p>
<p>As we emphasized in the Reader's Advisory at the front of this book, maternal care and childbirth must always rely first and foremost upon modern clinical obstetrics in accredited hospitals. At the same time, while modern medicine safeguards physical health, anyone who has experienced the vulnerability of midnight insomnia, recurring nightmares, or the anxious vigil outside a delivery room understands why a gentle recitation of "Namo Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata" brings such deep psychological steadiness. Sleep and childbirth are thresholds where human consciousness is most exposed; the name of the Medicine Buddha acts like a warm azure night-lamp shining into the depths of the subconscious, calming terror and tension while Ānanda's oath stands like Mount Sumeru beneath the heart.</p>
<h4>V. "Giving Rise to an Unstained Mind and a Non-Harming Mind": Building a Wall-Less Lapis Lazuli Temple in the Human World</h4>
<p>Right after Mañjuśrī's vow to enshrine the Sutra in a five-colored silk bag, the Buddha delivers what may be called the central mindfulness pith-instruction of the entire Medicine Buddha Sutra:</p>
<p>"For seven days and seven nights, uphold the eightfold fast precepts, eat pure food, bathe until fragrant and clean, wear clean garments, and give rise to an unstained mind and a non-harming mind; toward all sentient beings, give rise to thoughts of benefit and peace, and an equal heart of loving-kindness, compassion, empathetic joy, and equanimity... contemplating the merits of that Tathāgata's original vows, reading and reciting this Sutra, reflecting upon its meaning, and expounding and revealing it."</p>
<p>Readers of my earlier novel The Wall-Less Temple (Wú Qiáng zhī Miào) often ask me: "What is a temple without walls?"</p>
<p>Whenever asked, I point to these lines in the Medicine Buddha Sutra: "Give rise to an unstained mind and a non-harming mind; toward all sentient beings, give rise to thoughts of benefit and peace, and an equal heart of loving-kindness, compassion, empathetic joy, and equanimity."</p>
<p>A visible monastery has red walls, dark tiles, and mountain gates—like Donglin Temple on Mount Lu, a sacred ancestral sanctuary where weary travelers can pause and look up into the absorptive gaze of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli World-Honored One. Yet when we walk back down the mountain gate into bustling cities, offices, and family kitchens, how do we carry that blessing with us? By building a wall-less lapis lazuli temple inside the chest—whose foundation is an unstained, non-harming mind (wú gòuzhuó xīn, wú nùhài xīn), and whose four pillars are loving-kindness, compassion, empathetic joy, and equanimity toward all beings equally.</p>

<h3 id="ch10">Chapter 10: Forty-Nine Life-Continuity Lamps and the Warning Bell of the Nine Untimely Deaths (Tranamukta Bodhisattva, Ānanda's Questions, Seeing Through Charlatans, and Releasing Inner Captives)</h3>
<h4>Scriptural Text</h4>
<p>At that time, among the assembly there was a bodhisattva-mahāsattva named Tranamukta (Savior and Liberator), who rose from his seat, bared his right shoulder, placed his right knee on the ground, bowed with palms joined, and said to the Buddha: "Virtuous World-Honored One! <h4>I. "Seeing Darkness in Every Direction" Beside the Sickbed and the Lamps That Focus Compassionate Resolve</h4>
<p>Entering the Dissemination Section in the latter half of the Medicine Buddha Sutra, the focus of the assembly shifts to a great bodhisattva of urgent, practical compassion—Tranamukta Bodhisattva (Jiùtuō Púsà, Savior and Liberator).</p>
<p>Look at Tranamukta Bodhisattva's description of a critically ill patient—it is rendered with clinical realism:</p>
<p>"Wasted and emaciated by prolonged sickness, unable to eat or drink, with parched throat and lips, seeing darkness in every direction as signs of death appear before them; while parents, relatives, friends, and acquaintances weep around them—while that person's body lies in its original place, they see the messengers of Yama leading their consciousness before King Yama of the Dharma. Moreover, every sentient being has an inborn spirit who records in full whatever they do, whether transgression or merit..."</p>
<p>In the original scriptural meaning, when a person lies critically ill on the threshold of death, while the body remains on the bed, consciousness confronts the karmic ledger recorded by the "inborn spirit" (jùshēng shén); if relatives take refuge in the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, observe the fast precepts, recite this Sutra forty-nine times, kindle forty-nine lamps, hang five-colored banners, and release forty-nine lives, consciousness may return as from a dream with newfound moral clarity.</p>
<p>An event that took place in Guangzhou in the winter of 2023 gave me a profound appreciation for how modern clinical medicine and the spiritual anchor of Tranamukta's forty-nine lamps work together in a crisis.</p>
<p>A senior veteran in the film industry whom I had known for years—let us call him Elder Lin—was rushed in his early sixties to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) of a top tertiary hospital in Guangzhou with acute severe pancreatitis complicated by multi-organ failure, falling into a deep coma. The ICU physicians and nurses worked around the clock with mechanical ventilation and continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) to sustain his vital signs while informing the family of the critical prognosis. Outside the ICU doors, his family was weeping in sheer panic. Knowing my devotion to the Medicine Buddha Sutra, Elder Lin's wife called me late at night, her voice hoarse: "Wenjie, the doctors are doing everything possible inside, and we are crumbling in tears out here in the corridor—what else can we do for him?"</p>
<p>I told her immediately over the phone: "Sister-in-law, first and foremost, place one hundred percent trust in the ICU attending physicians and cooperate with every clinical measure—that is the irreplaceable foundation for saving Elder Lin's physical life. Second, while waiting outside the ICU during these seven days, stop weeping in panic and steady your minds according to Tranamukta Bodhisattva's instruction in the Medicine Buddha Sutra: let the family keep a clean vegetarian fast for seven days to calm your hearts; vow on Elder Lin's behalf to contribute to medical relief funds for indigent patients and protect living creatures; and in the quiet shrine room of my study here in Guangzhou, before the statue of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli World-Honored One, I will kindle forty-nine butter lamps while we recite the Medicine Buddha Sutra forty-nine times with undivided heart!"</p>
<p>Throughout those seven days and nights, inside the ICU ward modern critical-care instruments ran day and night under the vigilant eyes of the medical team; outside the ward and in my quiet Guangzhou study, forty-nine warm lamps burned steadily as his wife and children ceased their frantic sobbing and sat quietly on the corridor bench reciting the Medicine Buddha Sutra and the name of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata.</p>
<p>At dawn on the sixth day, under the intensive medical team's continuous treatment, Elder Lin's inflammatory markers began to recede; his fingers stirred, his eyes slowly opened, and several days later he was successfully weaned off the ventilator and transferred to a general ward.</p>
<p>A month later, when I visited Elder Lin in the general ward as he sat propped up sipping rice broth, he held my hand and said with deep emotion:</p>
<p>"Wenjie, coming back from that threshold, my very first gratitude goes to the ICU doctors and nurses who fought for my life day and night. Yet something remarkable happened inside my mind while I was unconscious: at first I felt as if I had fallen into a dark, freezing cavern (seeing darkness in every direction), hearing chaotic weeping in the distance that made me feel more and more panicky and exhausted; then, a few days later, the chaotic crying stopped, and the darkness around me grew extraordinarily quiet and warm, as though rows of bright, steady lamps were shining through the mist. My heart settled immediately, and walking toward that warm light, I opened my eyes."</p>
<p>Modern medical science is the irreplaceable cornerstone for saving critically ill patients; and Tranamukta Bodhisattva's teaching of kindling forty-nine lamps and reciting the Sutra forty-nine times transforms a family's paralyzing panic into focused, peaceful prayer. When the physician's clinical skill, the family's calm steadiness, and the azure light of the Medicine Buddha meet, body and spirit receive complete care.</p>
<h4>II. The First Untimely Death: A Razor-Sharp Dissection of "Lacking Medicine" and "Consulting Divinations in Search of Calamity"</h4>
<p>In Chapter 10, the Venerable Ānanda asks Tranamukta Bodhisattva a penetrating question: "Virtuous One! How can a life that is already nearing exhaustion be prolonged?"</p>
<p>Tranamukta replies that countless people in this world die not because their natural lifespan is exhausted, but because they fall victim to the "Nine Untimely Deaths" (jiǔ héngsǐ)!</p>
<p>And standing first among all nine—explained at greatest length and in the sternest tone—is the First Untimely Death (chūhéng), which delivers a thunderous warning against neglecting proper medical care and believing charlatans who peddle fear:</p>
<p>"If there are sentient beings whose illness is slight, yet who lack both medicine, physician, and caregiver—or even if they meet a physician, are given the wrong medicine—so that though they ought not to die, they suffer an untimely death; and furthermore, they believe in worldly demonic heterodoxies and charlatan masters who falsely proclaim fortune and misfortune, thereby giving rise to panic and agitation; because their minds are not upright within, they consult divinations in search of calamity, slaughter various living creatures, petition spirits, summon ghosts and goblins to beg for blessings, hoping to prolong their years, yet can never succeed. Through folly and delusion, believing in distorted and evil views, they bring about an untimely death... this is called the First Untimely Death."</p>
<p>Read this passage three times! No passage in the entire Buddhist canon demonstrates a more lucid, rational integration of medical common sense and spiritual clarity.</p>
<p>Notice how Tranamukta Bodhisattva divides the First Untimely Death into two interlocking halves:</p>
<p>The first half is a failure of medical care: "whose illness is slight, yet who lack both medicine, physician, and caregiver—or even if they meet a physician, are given the wrong medicine—so that though they ought not to die, they suffer an untimely death." Here the Buddha explicitly instructs humanity through Tranamukta Bodhisattva: <em>When physical illness strikes, your very first duty is to seek a qualified physician, receive accurate diagnosis and proper medicine (zhèngyào), and provide attentive nursing care!</em></p>
<p>The second half is a failure of spiritual rationality: "and furthermore, they believe in worldly demonic heterodoxies and charlatan masters who falsely proclaim fortune and misfortune, thereby giving rise to panic and agitation; because their minds are not upright within, they consult divinations in search of calamity..."</p>
<p>Look at those sixteen Chinese characters: "Falsely proclaiming fortune and misfortune, thereby giving rise to panic and agitation; because their minds are not upright within, they consult divinations in search of calamity" (wàngshuō huòfú, biàn shēng kǒngdòng, xīn bù zìzhèng, bǔwèn mìhuò)!</p>
<p>As someone raised in the classical study of the Book of Changes, I know all too well how street charlatans ("charlatan masters") profit by "falsely proclaiming fortune and misfortune to stir up panic." When a person visits a fraudster over a minor illness or career setback, the fraudster never tells them to visit a hospital or work steadily; instead, he widens his eyes and whispers terrifying fabrications about "deadly curses," "vengeful spirits," or "imminent blood calamities" unless the victim pays tens of thousands of yuan for bizarre rituals. Terrified out of their wits (giving rise to panic and agitation; their minds not upright within), victims delay proper medical treatment and bankrupt themselves chasing ghosts—turning a minor, curable ailment into a fatal tragedy!</p>
<p>Tranamukta Bodhisattva summarizes that entire behavior in four piercing characters: "consulting divinations in search of calamity" (bǔwèn mìhuò)! You think you are consulting charlatans to avoid disaster; in truth, you are actively shopping for disaster and inviting it into your home!</p>
<p>The Confucian philosopher Xunzi once wrote: "One who truly masters the Book of Changes does not obsess over divination." Authentic study of the Book of Changes cultivates ethical self-reflection, timing, and uprightness of mind (xīn zìzhèng); it never traffics in ghost-fear. Whoever masters this First Untimely Death in the Medicine Buddha Sutra holds two lifelong safeguards: first, when ill, go promptly to an accredited hospital and follow qualified medical treatment; second, when facing difficulty, keep an upright, compassionate mind and never let fear-peddling charlatans shake your spirit.</p>
<h4>III. The Second to Ninth Untimely Deaths: What Is Draining Your Vital Spirit in the Modern City?</h4>
<p>Following the First Untimely Death, Tranamukta Bodhisattva enumerates the Second through Ninth Untimely Deaths:</p>
<p>The Second Untimely Death: "untimely execution by royal law"—crossing legal boundaries through greed and hubris;</p>
<p>The Third Untimely Death: "hunting and carousing, indulging in sexual dissipation and strong drink without restraint, so that their vital essence is untimely snatched away by non-human spirits";</p>
<p>The Fourth to Ninth Untimely Deaths: untimely death by fire, drowning in water, vicious beasts, falling off a cliff, poison/curses, and starvation/thirst.</p>
<p>Look closely at the Third Untimely Death: "indulging in sexual dissipation and strong drink without restraint, so that their vital essence is untimely snatched away by non-human spirits" (dānyín shìjiǔ, fàngyì wúdù, héng wèi fēirén duó qí jīngqì).</p>
<p>In the original scriptural meaning, ancient Indian tradition held that when a person abandons all self-restraint in dissipation and intoxication, protective deities depart and malevolent spirits snatch away their vital energy (ojas / jīngqì). And from the perspective of modern health and mindfulness: in today's sleepless cities, what is it that snatches away a person's vital essence? It is the lifestyle of "unrestrained dissipation" itself—staying awake until three or four every morning glued to a glowing screen, drowning emptiness in alcohol, and burning through one's constitutional reserves as though trimming an oil lamp's wick as thick as a torch!</p>
<p>And beyond their literal reminder to respect natural hazards and safety in the physical world, the Fourth through Ninth Untimely Deaths also mirror six psychological hazards in our inner life:</p>
<p>Inner "burning by fire": letting explosive rage and hatred scorch one's liver, judgment, and relationships;</p>
<p>Inner "drowning in water": sinking into toxic obsessions or insatiable material cravings without coming up for air;</p>
<p>Inner "devouring by vicious beasts": allowing envy, suspicion, and panic to gnaw at one's heart through the night;</p>
<p>Inner "falling from a cliff": losing all humility at the peak of success and stepping into thin air;</p>
<p>Inner "harm from poison and curses": steeping one's mind daily in vitriolic speech and resentment;</p>
<p>Inner "starvation and thirst": surrounding oneself with material clutter while never nourishing the soul with a single page of wisdom or a moment of quiet contemplation.</p>
<h4>IV. "Pardoning Those Who Are Fettered and Imprisoned": When You Release Others, Heaven and Earth Release You</h4>
<p>In Chapter 10, Tranamukta Bodhisattva also offers a teaching for anointed kṣatriya kings and leaders facing national calamities (epidemics, invasions, rebellion, meteorological anomalies, drought):</p>
<p>"Those anointed kṣatriya kings should at that time give rise to a heart of compassion toward all sentient beings and pardon those who are fettered and imprisoned (shè zhū xìbì); and in accordance with the method of offering spoken earlier, make offerings to that World-Honored Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata. By virtue of this wholesome root and the power of that Tathāgata's original vows, their realm shall immediately obtain peace, wind and rain shall arrive in season, grain crops shall ripen to maturity, and all sentient beings shall be free of illness and filled with joy..."</p>
<p>Look at those four characters: "Pardon those who are fettered and imprisoned" (shè zhū xìbì)!</p>
<p>In the original scriptural context, this instructs rulers in times of crisis to show mercy by pardoning prisoners held for minor offenses or wrongful detention. And when we turn this teaching inward to contemplate the "realm" of our own body, mind, and household—where each of us is the sovereign ruler of our inner kingdom—why does our inner weather so often suffer from unseasonable storms (emotional outbursts) or prolonged droughts (exhaustion and joylessness)?</p>
<p>Because inside the prison of our chest, we have locked up too many inmates!</p>
<p>In your inner prison, you may still be locking up a former partner who betrayed you five years ago, a colleague who slighted you three years ago, an elder who failed to understand you in youth, or the version of yourself who once made a painful mistake. Every day you rattle the chains of resentment to keep them locked tight inside your chest, and the toxic air rising from that inner dungeon poisons your own sleep and vitality first.</p>
<p>Take up the amnesty decree of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata today: walk to the iron gate of your inner prison, turn the key, and proclaim a general amnesty—"pardon those who are fettered and imprisoned." The moment you let those inner captives go and allow azure light into the cell, the winds and rains of your life return to harmony.</p>
<h4>V. "Because They Have Personally Witnessed the Fruits of Karma, Even at the Cost of Their Lives They Commit No Evil": Awakening Toward Life</h4>
<p>Near the close of Tranamukta Bodhisattva's account of the patient whose consciousness returns from the brink of death, the Sutra records a fourteen-character conclusion of immense moral power:</p>
<p>"Because they have personally witnessed the fruits of karma, even at the peril of their lives they never again commit any evil deed." (Yóu zì zhèngjiàn yèguǒ bàogù, nǎizhì mìngnàn, yì bù zàozuò zhū'è zhī yè.)</p>
<p>Why does someone who has walked back from the threshold of the ICU—or someone who has truly absorbed Chapter 10 of the Medicine Buddha Sutra—refuse to commit an unscrupulous act even under extreme pressure? Because in that quiet darkness where all worldly applause falls away, he has seen with his own eyes that neither fame, nor ill-gotten money, nor luxury watches accompany consciousness across the threshold; only the clear ledger of one's own conscience (the inborn spirit) and the warmth of compassion remain.</p>
<p>After Elder Lin recovered from his critical illness in Guangzhou in late 2023, everyone around him witnessed that transformation: he left behind noisy drinking banquets, kept regular hours, treated the humblest crew assistants with unfailing kindness, and quietly donated to pediatric medical relief. When someone once tempted him with a lucrative gray-area endorsement, he simply smiled and shook his head: "I have seen the ledger in the dark; for the rest of my days, I will earn only clean money that lets a man sleep peacefully at night."</p>
<h3 id="ch11">Chapter 11: The Armor of the Twelve Yakṣa Generals and the Medicine Buddha Dhāraṇī (Twelve Divine Generals, Five-Colored Cords, and the Provenance and Meaning of the Mantra)</h3>
<h4>Scriptural Text</h4>
<p>At that time, among the assembly there were twelve yakṣa generals seated together—namely, General Kumbhīra, General Vajra, General Mihira, General Andīra, General Anila, General Śaṇḍila, General Indāla, General Pajra, General Mahoraga, General Kinnara, General Catura, and General Vikarāla. Each of these twelve yakṣa generals had seven thousand yakṣas as his retinue. Raising their voices together, they said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One! Now, having received the Buddha's awesome power and heard the name of the World-Honored Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, we no longer have any fear of evil destinies. Leading one another with a single united heart, for the rest of our lives we take refuge in the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha, vowing to shoulder the burden of all sentient beings, working for their benefit, enrichment, peace, and joy. In whatever village, town, capital city, or quiet forest this Sutra circulates, or wherever someone upholds the name of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata and makes reverent offerings, our retinues shall guard and protect that person, causing them to be liberated from all suffering and difficulty and fulfilling all their wishes. And if there are those in illness or distress who seek deliverance, they too should read and recite this Sutra, and with a five-colored cord tie our names; once their wish has been fulfilled, they may untie the knot."</p>
<p>At that time, the World-Honored One praised the yakṣa generals, saying: "Excellent! Excellent, great yakṣa generals! When you think of repaying the kindness and virtue of the World-Honored Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, you should ever benefit and bring peace and joy to all sentient beings in this way."</p>
<p><em>The Standard Liturgical Passage of the Medicine Buddha Empowerment Dhāraṇī</em><em> (Note: Absent from Master Xuanzang's original Tang translation manuscript; incorporated into standard Chinese monastic recitation editions from Śrīmitra's Eastern Jin Consecration Sutra and Master Yijing's Tang translation, matching the Appendix verbatim)</em>:</p>
<p>"Furthermore, Mañjuśrī! When that Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata attained Bodhi, by the power of His original vows He observed sentient beings encountering manifold painful illnesses—emaciation, convulsion, consumption, jaundice, fever, and the like; or struck by sorcery and gu-poison; or short-lived, or dying an untimely death. Wishing to cause all such illnesses and sufferings to be dispelled and all wishes to be fulfilled, at that time the World-Honored One entered a samādhi named 'Eliminating the Sufferings and Afflictions of All Sentient Beings.' Having entered this concentration, from His cranial uṣṇīṣa He emitted a great radiant light, and within that light proclaimed a great dhāraṇī:</p>
<p>'Namo bhagavate bhaiṣajyaguru-vaiḍūrya-prabha-rājāya tathāgatāya arhate samyaksaṃbuddhāya. Tadyathā: Oṃ bhaiṣajye bhaiṣajye bhaiṣajya-samudgate svāhā.'"</p>
<h4>I. When Fierce Warriors Put On the Armor of Compassion: Scriptural Meaning, Cultural Evolution, and Contemplative Reflection on the Twelve Yakṣa Generals</h4>
<p>As the Medicine Buddha Sutra approaches its grand finale beneath the Music Tree of Vaiśālī, twelve towering figures clad in heavy armor rise in unison before the Buddha.</p>
<p>They are the Twelve Yakṣa Generals—Kumbhīra, Vajra, Mihira, Andīra, Anila, Śaṇḍila, Indāla, Pajra, Mahoraga, Kinnara, Catura, and Vikarāla—each commanding seven thousand yakṣa followers, totaling eighty-four thousand yakṣas!</p>
<p>To maintain strict clarity between scriptural text and later tradition, let us distinguish three levels for the reader:</p>
<p>First, the original scriptural meaning: In ancient Sanskrit lore, yakṣas were swift, mighty spirits of mountains, forests, and wilderness whose fierce power often inspired dread among mortals. Yet at the assembly in Vaiśālī, bathed in the awesome virtue of Śākyamuni Buddha and the name of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, these twelve yakṣa commanders are freed from all fear of evil destinies (we no longer have any fear of evil destinies), take lifelong refuge in the Three Jewels (Buddha, Dharma, and Saṅgha) together with their eighty-four thousand followers, and vow to guard everyone who circulates the Medicine Buddha Sutra or upholds the Medicine Buddha's name across every village, city, and forest.</p>
<p>Second, the East Asian liturgical and calendrical tradition: After the Medicine Buddha Sutra spread across China and East Asia from the Sui and Tang dynasties onward, monastic ritual manuals and temple iconography paired the Twelve Yakṣa Generals with the twelve double-hours of the day (shí'èr shíchén: Zi, Chou, Yin, Mao, Chen, Si, Wu, Wei, Shen, You, Xu, Hai) and the twelve Earthly Branches / zodiac animals, symbolizing that the protection of the Medicine Buddha's teaching flows unbroken through every hour of day and night.</p>
<p>Third, our personal contemplative reflection before the seat of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli World-Honored One:</p>
<p>Notice that twelve generals, each leading seven thousand yakṣa followers, multiply to exactly eighty-four thousand—the exact classical Buddhist number for the eighty-four thousand afflictions and restless impulses (bāwàn sìqiān fánnǎo) surging inside the ordinary human mind!</p>
<p>A person who has walked through the storms of the mortal world eventually realizes a profound truth: the raw instincts, passion, drive, anger, and sensitivity inside a human life are like fierce tigers and yakṣas in a mountain forest. If one tries to kill that vital energy through rigid repression, the heart grows dry and lifeless; yet if one drops the reins in the city of desire, the runaway beast tears one's life apart. At the assembly of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, the Medicine Buddha neither annihilates the Twelve Yakṣa Generals nor leaves them untamed—instead, He bathes them in cool, boundless lapis lazuli light, freeing them from fear so that their fierce vitality is transformed into diamond armor protecting the True Dharma!</p>
<p>When your fierce drive and courage are illuminated by the Medicine Buddha's light and take refuge in the Three Jewels, reckless aggression transforms into the fearless resolve of General Vajra shielding the vulnerable; when your acute sensitivity is freed from terror by His azure radiance, anxious dread transforms into vigilant mindfulness guarding every hour of your life.</p>
<h4>II. "With a Five-Colored Cord Tie Our Names; Once the Wish Is Fulfilled, Untie the Knot"</h4>
<p>In the vow of the Twelve Yakṣa Generals appears a practice of extraordinary poetic beauty and psychological wisdom:</p>
<p>"And if there are those in illness or distress who seek deliverance, they too should read and recite this Sutra, and with a five-colored cord tie our names; once their wish has been fulfilled, they may untie the knot." (Yǐ wǔsè lǚ, jié wǒ míngzì, dé rúyuàn yǐ, ránhòu jiějié.)</p>
<p>In the original scriptural meaning, a practitioner facing illness or hardship recites the Medicine Buddha Sutra and plaits threads of five colors (blue/green, yellow, red, white, and black) into a cord, tying knots while invoking the names of the Twelve Yakṣa Generals; once the crisis has passed and the wish is fulfilled, the knots are untied (once their wish has been fulfilled, they may untie the knot).</p>
<p>In our inner contemplation, who among mortals does not carry a few tight knots in the heart—a knot of old regret, a knot of grievance, or a knot of fear over illness? Ordinarily, the more our anxious thoughts pick at an inner knot, the tighter it constricts. The ritual of tying the five-colored cord while reciting the Sutra turns formless panic into a solemn act of entrustment: "I entrust this wish to the Three Jewels and the twelve guardians, and now I settle my mind to follow my doctor's treatment and do my daily work in peace."</p>
<p>And the most liberating instruction of all lies in the final four characters: "then untie the knot" (ránhòu jiějié)!</p>
<p>Once the illness has healed and the storm has passed, remember to untie the knot. Do not cling for the rest of your life to the identity of "I am a victim" or "I am a sufferer." When spring returns, untie the five-colored cord with your own hands, smooth the silk threads flat again, and walk lightly into the next chapter of life.</p>
<h4>III. The "Oṃ" in the Lapis Lazuli River and the Textual Provenance and Meaning of the Medicine Buddha Empowerment Dhāraṇī</h4>
<p>Late at night, whenever I sit quietly before my desk and gaze up at the statue of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli World-Honored One glowing softly in the lamplight, my mind settles as though submerging into a boundless river of liquid lapis lazuli.</p>
<p>Within that river formed of the Medicine Buddha's original vows, I seem to hear ten thousand mortal voices flowing together: the cry of a newborn infant, the whispered prayer of a mother by a bedside lamp, the sigh of a patient in the dark, and the quiet smile of a traveler awakening from delusion. And the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata enters the samādhi named "Eliminating the Sufferings and Afflictions of All Sentient Beings." From the cranial uṣṇīṣa atop His head rises a great azure radiance illuminating the universe, embracing and purifying every cry, laugh, plea, and wound until, at the center of that ocean of lapis lazuli light, all sounds converge into a single sacred, primordial syllable—</p>
<p>"Oṃ!"</p>
<p>From that light flows the Medicine Buddha Empowerment Dhāraṇī (Yàoshī Guàndǐng Zhēnyán).</p>
<p>First, let us state its textual history with precision: Master Xuanzang's original Tang translation manuscript of 650 CE (Taishō No. 450) did not contain this dhāraṇī paragraph. However, the core Medicine Buddha mantra had already been transmitted in China in Śrīmitra's Eastern Jin translation of the Consecration Sutra (Fóshuō Guàndǐng Báchú Guòzuì Shēngsǐ Déduò Jīng, Fascicle 12), and Master Yijing's Tang translation of 707 CE (Sutra on the Original Vows and Merits of the Seven Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Buddhas, Taishō No. 451) recorded the complete passage of the Tathāgata entering samādhi, emitting light from His uṣṇīṣa, and proclaiming the dhāraṇī. Later Chinese patriarchs incorporated this passage into standard liturgical editions of Master Xuanzang's text so practitioners could chant both Xuanzang's luminous prose and the core dhāraṇī together:</p>
<p>Namo bhagavate bhaiṣajyaguru-vaiḍūrya-prabha-rājāya tathāgatāya arhate samyaksaṃbuddhāya. Tadyathā: Oṃ bhaiṣajye bhaiṣajye bhaiṣajya-samudgate svāhā.</p>
<p>（南谟薄伽伐帝，鞞杀社窭噜，薜琉璃，钵喇婆，喝啰阇也，怛他揭多也，阿啰喝帝，三藐三勃陀也。怛侄他：唵，鞞杀逝，鞞杀逝，鞞杀社，三没揭帝，莎诃。）</p>
<p>When we translate each Sanskrit word of this dhāraṇī into clear English, we discover a hymn of breathtaking beauty:</p>
<p>The Homage (First Half):</p>
<p>Namo: Homage and wholehearted refuge;</p>
<p>bhagavate: to the World-Honored One (Bhagavān);</p>
<p>bhaiṣajyaguru: Master of Medicine (bhaiṣajya = healing medicine, guru = master/teacher);</p>
<p>vaiḍūrya-prabha-rājāya: King of Lapis Lazuli Light (vaiḍūrya = azure lapis lazuli gem, prabha = radiant light, rāja = king);</p>
<p>tathāgatāya arhate samyaksaṃbuddhāya: the Tathāgata (Thus-Come One), Arhat (Worthy of Offerings), Samyaksaṃbuddha (Perfectly and Fully Awakened One)!</p>
<p>The Heart-Mantra (Sixteen Chinese Characters: 唵，鞞杀逝，鞞杀逝，鞞杀社，三没揭帝，莎诃):</p>
<p>Tadyathā: Namely, as follows:</p>
<p>Oṃ: The primordial sound uniting body, speech, and mind with the Buddha's lapis lazuli light;</p>
<p>bhaiṣajye, bhaiṣajye: O Medicine! O Medicine! (Heal! Heal!);</p>
<p>bhaiṣajya-samudgate: Supreme Medicine—arising, transcending, and lifting all beings to awakening (samudgate = risen up, emerged to the highest summit);</p>
<p>svāhā: May auspiciousness, healing, and perfection be firmly established!</p>
<p>In sixteen Chinese characters (six Sanskrit words in the heart-mantra), it proclaims: "In the luminous unity of Oṃ: O Medicine, O Medicine—the Supreme Medicine has risen; may all suffering be healed in auspicious perfection!"</p>
<p>In scriptural commentary, the first "bhaiṣajye" heals physical and worldly distress; the second "bhaiṣajye" heals the inner sickness of greed, anger, pride, and delusion; and "bhaiṣajya-samudgate" reveals that when met with awakened awareness and compassion, every hardship and wound we have walked through is transformed into the supreme medicine that awakens both ourselves and others.</p>
<h4>IV. One Hundred and Eight Recitations of Compassionate Mindfulness: Good Medicine Treats the Body, Pure Faith Steadies the Soul</h4>
<p>Immediately following the dhāraṇī in the standard liturgical text appears a practical instruction for caring for the sick:</p>
<p>"If you see a man or woman suffering from illness, you should with a single, undivided heart, on behalf of that sick person, regularly bathe and rinse until clean, and over either food, or medicine, or insect-free clean water, recite this dhāraṇī one hundred and eight times and give it to them to take..."</p>
<p>Look at the explicit words of the text: "either food, or medicine, or insect-free clean water" (huò shí, huò yào, huò wúchóng shuǐ)!</p>
<p>Those who have never read the Sutra sometimes imagine that Buddhism tells people to reject medicine and drink only "blessed water." Yet the Sutra states explicitly: bless the nourishing food (huò shí), the medicine (huò yào), or clean water (huò wúchóng shuǐ) one hundred and eight times and give it to the patient to take! Once again, the Medicine Buddha Sutra places proper medicine (huò yào) right at the center (and as always, spiritual recitation is a supportive consolation, never a replacement for clinical medical care).</p>
<p>When I was a boy in Hulin, Heilongjiang, I once caught a severe winter flu with high fever and vomiting, unable to keep fever medicine down. While the northern blizzard howled outside, my mother sat beside the heated brick bed with the doctor's prescribed fever medicine and a bowl of warm pear water. Instead of forcing me impatiently to swallow, she wiped my forehead with a warm towel, held the bowl against her chest to keep it warm, murmured prayers to the Medicine Buddha for her child's safety, and spooned the medicine and warm water gently into my mouth. Bathed in the calm warmth of my mother's hands and eyes, my frightened stomach settled, the medicine stayed down, and after a night of sound sleep the fever broke at dawn.</p>
<p>Medicine treats the physical lesion; the warmth in the caregiver's hands and the focused love of one hundred and eight recitations calm the patient's fear and awaken the body's resilience.</p>
<h4>V. "In Whatever Village, Town, Capital City, or Quiet Forest": Fearless Companionship Across the Twelve Double-Hours</h4>
<p>When the Twelve Yakṣa Generals pledge their protection—"In whatever village, town, capital city, or quiet forest this Sutra circulates, or wherever someone upholds the name of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata... our retinues shall guard and protect that person"—they name three spaces of human life: the bustling capital city (guóyì), the humble village or town (cūnchéng), and the solitary quiet forest (kōngxián línzhōng).</p>
<p>Whether a traveler who has been bathed in the light of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata is walking through a northern snow forest, standing beneath dazzling urban neon, or sitting alone beside a quiet river at midnight, the image of the Medicine World-Honored One holding His lapis lazuli medicine bowl never leaves his eyes. When the cool Sanskrit syllables of "Oṃ bhaiṣajye bhaiṣajye bhaiṣajya-samudgate svāhā" blend into every breath, the village is a practice hall, the metropolis is a practice hall, and the solitary room is a Pure Lapis Lazuli sanctuary where one sits face to face with Bhaiṣajyaguru.</p>
<h3 id="ch12">Chapter 12: The Ferryman's River and the Inextinguishable Lapis Lazuli (Epilogue: The Three Titles of the Sutra, the Echo of the Ninth Lunar Month, and Universal Self-Healing)</h3>
<h4>Scriptural Text</h4>
<p>At that time, Ānanda asked the Buddha: "World-Honored One! By what name should this Dharma gate be called, and how should we uphold it?"</p>
<p>The Buddha told Ānanda: "This Dharma gate is named Expounding the Merits of the Original Vows of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata; it is also named Expounding the Spirit Mantra by Which the Twelve Divine Generals Benefit Sentient Beings and Tie Their Vows; it is also named Uprooting All Karmic Obstructions. Thus should you uphold it."</p>
<p>When the Bhagavān had spoken these words, all the bodhisattva-mahāsattvas, the great śrāvakas, kings, ministers, brāhmaṇas, householders, devas, nāgas, yakṣas, gandharvas, asuras, garuḍas, kinnaras, mahoragas, human and non-human beings—the entire great assembly, hearing what the Buddha had spoken, were all filled with great joy, accepted it in faith, and put it into practice.</p>
<h4>I. Once More the Thirtieth Day of the Ninth Lunar Month: The River Flowing from the Ussuri to the Lingdingyang Sea</h4>
<p>Page by page the calendar turns, and as the autumn breeze of southern China stirs the silk-floss blossoms along Shenzhen Bay and the Pearl River in Guangzhou, the hands of time arrive once more at the thirtieth day of the ninth lunar month.</p>
<p>Today is the sacred natal day of the Eastern Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata; it is also my birthday.</p>
<p>At six o'clock in the morning, the sky still holds a cool wash of indigo-blue. Waking in my study, I leave the bright ceiling lamps off; after brushing my teeth and washing my hands in clean water (chewing tooth-wood at dawn, bathing and rinsing until clean), I stand before the statue of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli World-Honored One on my desk, kindle a clear butter lamp, and light a stick of natural aloeswood-sandalwood incense.</p>
<p>A slender ribbon of blue-white smoke rises straight up, ties a gentle cloud-knot in the early light, and dissolves quietly into the room.</p>
<p>Sitting cross-legged on the cushion, I look up at the World-Honored One's half-lowered compassionate eyes, the gentle curve of His lips, and the deep, warm lapis lazuli medicine bowl resting in His left hand. In the soft flicker of the lamp flame, waves of azure light seem to ripple outward from the Buddha's form, turning the mountains and rivers of my thirty-three years into a single translucent river flowing quietly before my eyes:</p>
<p>I see that snow-swept northern morning on the thirtieth of the ninth lunar month in 1993, frost feathers blooming on the windowpane of our old house in Hulin by the Ussuri River, the line of cinnabar characters in the old almanac reading "Natal Day of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata," and an infant opening his eyes amid the sound of wind and snow;</p>
<p>I see the winter night of my twelfth year, my grandfather's calloused fingers resting on the three Qianlong bronze coins and the old medical notebook as he says to me: "The Medicine Buddha is the One who first heats the brick bed and brews hot ginger soup for the freezing carters";</p>
<p>I see the heavy black velvet curtain parting backstage at the 2013 Dior Homme show in Beijing when I was nineteen, a 188-centimeter northern youth stepping onto a silver runway beneath blinding white beams as his heartbeat echoes through the music;</p>
<p>I see the dusk waters of Victoria Harbour in Hong Kong at twenty-one, a young fellow model removing her sunglasses on the Star Ferry as the sea breeze lifts her hair and the entire harbor glows in the sunset like living blue lapis lazuli;</p>
<p>I see the damp air-raid shelter twenty steps beneath Beijing at twenty-two, Yong Le in Underground Fragrance touching the old fountain-pen note at the bottom of a camphor chest—"with padded coats in winter and hot rice in the pot"—and a few months later, the Adriatic sun shining upon the "Venice Days" award stage at the Venice International Film Festival;</p>
<p>I see colleagues weeping in repentance over a cup of warm water on rainy film-set nights, entrepreneurs pulling open blackout curtains to return to honest work, and elderly manufacturers peeling yellowed plastic off their sofas to learn the joy of giving;</p>
<p>And clearest of all, I see that mist-shrouded morning at Donglin Temple on Mount Lu in Jiangxi—crossing Tiger Brook Bridge, stepping across the threshold of the quiet hall, and looking up into the ocean-deep, soul-absorbing gaze of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli World-Honored One.</p>
<p>All of those scenes—the northern snow, the runway lights, the basement cedar scent, the Adriatic waves, the mountain mist of Mount Lu, and the study lamp in Lingnan—converge at this moment into a single river inside the Medicine Buddha's alms bowl.</p>
<h4>II. The Three Titles of This Sutra: Merits of the Original Vows, the Twelve Divine Generals' Vow-Knot Mantra, and Uprooting All Karmic Obstructions</h4>
<p>At the close of the Medicine Buddha Sutra, the Venerable Ānanda asks the Buddha by what name this teaching should be called and how it should be upheld.</p>
<p>The Buddha gives the Sutra three names, which form a three-step staircase of human self-healing:</p>
<p><em>The First Step is Expounding the Merits of the Original Vows of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata—looking up at the starry sky to recover the original blueprint of human dignity.</em> Whenever you feel small, broken, destitute, or cornered, read the Twelve Great Vows of the Medicine Buddha. You will know that in the Eastern Pure Lapis Lazuli World, the Supreme Physician has already vowed that you are worthy of radiance and dignity (Vows 1–2), worthy of abundant provision through wisdom and a Great Vehicle heart (Vows 3–4), worthy of regaining purity after sincere repentance and healing from brokenness (Vows 5–6), worthy of medical care and fearless spiritual autonomy (Vows 7–8), worthy of escaping mental snares and dark prisons of grief (Vows 9–10), and worthy of nourishing food, Dharma truth, clean garments, fragrance, and noble art (Vows 11–12).</p>
<p><em>The Second Step is Expounding the Spirit Mantra by Which the Twelve Divine Generals Benefit Sentient Beings and Tie Their Vows—planting your feet on the earth to live every double-hour in mindful guardianship. Across the twelve double-hours of day and night, let the Medicine Buddha's azure light transform your restless inner impulses into armored guardians; know how to tie the five-colored cord of focused aspiration in times of trial and how to untie the knot in gratitude once the storm has passed; and in the resonance of "Oṃ bhaiṣajye bhaiṣajye bhaiṣajya-samudgate svāhā,"</em> allow every bitter experience to steep like myrobalan fruit into healing ambrosia.</p>
<p><em>The Third Step is Uprooting All Karmic Obstructions—the spring breeze that unties every knot into ultimate freedom.</em> When you peel the plastic film of miserliness off your life, pull open the blackout curtains of delusion, seek qualified doctors when ill, make amends honestly when wrong, reject fear-peddling charlatans who "consult divinations in search of calamity," and grant a general amnesty to the prisoners locked inside your heart, every karmic obstruction is uprooted in the living present.</p>
<h4>III. The Ferryman's Song: Listening to All Things by the Mortal Riverbank</h4>
<p>Whenever I sit beneath the lamp in my study or recall the hall of Donglin Temple on Mount Lu, gazing for a long time at the brow and half-lowered eyes of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, I feel that this King of Physicians in the East never needs loud arguments to prove His truth. When you lay down all defenses and let your heart draw close to the pure lapis lazuli light before His chest, in that instant of silence you behold an unending river of life—</p>
<p>Reflected in that translucent lapis lazuli river are the newborn child, the youth trudging through northern snowfields, the bewildered traveler beneath runway lights and film cameras, the family praying beside a hospital bed, and the awakened sage stepping out of a dark room. The joys and sorrows of ten thousand living beings mingle and dissolve inside the Medicine Buddha's alms bowl, while above the entire vast river shines the gentle, crystal-clear, unchanging smile of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli World-Honored One.</p>
<p>Perhaps as you read this page you are sitting beside a cold northern window worrying over tomorrow's bills; perhaps you are walking down a neon-lit avenue feeling bone-deep loneliness inside a crowd; perhaps you have just endured a broken relationship, a business setback, or a long night in a hospital corridor; or perhaps you are simply looking in a mirror late at night, wondering how to make peace with your scars.</p>
<p>Do not be afraid, and never believe that you are without rescue or refuge.</p>
<p>Two thousand five hundred years ago, the Music Tree outside Vaiśālī never stopped singing in the breeze; inside the hall of Donglin Temple on Mount Lu in Jiangxi, the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli World-Honored One has never turned away His compassionate gaze.</p>
<p>Quiet your heart and take a deep breath. When you stop locking yourself up in greed, stop frightening yourself with superstition, and stop whipping yourself over yesterday's mistakes; when you wash your face cleanly at dawn, sweep your room, brew a bowl of warm soup for yourself and your family, trust good doctors when sick, hold fast to right view in difficulty, and abide transparently like pure lapis lazuli within and without—you will hear the great river deep within your life utter a clear, resonant hymn:</p>
<p>Namo Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata. May you walk through every mortal storm and finally behold, within the lapis lazuli light, your own originally whole and spotless self.</p>
<h4>IV. Epilogue: To Everyone Who Looks Up at the Starry Sky on the Thirtieth Day of the Ninth Lunar Month</h4>
<p>As I write these closing lines, daylight has broken in full over Lingnan outside my window.</p>
<p>The morning sun streams through the lattice onto Master Xuanzang's translation of the Sutra on the Original Vows and Merits of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata on my desk, glinting softly across the three old bronze coins that have accompanied me for thirty-three years and the serene countenance of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli World-Honored One.</p>
<p>Two thousand five hundred years after the Buddha spoke at Vaiśālī, our world moves a thousand times faster and roars a thousand times louder than the ancient Ganges plain. High-speed trains and aircraft carry us across continents in hours, while glowing screens push the desires and anxieties of the entire planet before our eyes in a fraction of a second.</p>
<p>Yet the longing and the ache deep inside the human soul have never changed.</p>
<p>And that is why I wished to open my own thirty-three years of wandering—together with the profound absorptive power and blessing I received before the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli World-Honored One at Donglin Temple on Mount Lu—to write this book.</p>
<p>Remember: every year on the thirtieth day of the ninth lunar month, when the autumn wind sweeps away the last withered leaf and winter snow prepares to descend upon the earth, the great lamp of the Eastern Pure Lapis Lazuli World shines anew across heaven and earth. No matter how far or how wearily you have walked, you have only to lift your head: the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli World-Honored One, holding His medicine bowl and smiling with boundless compassion, is forever waiting for you where the mountain mist parts.</p>
<h4>V. Closing Words: A River Flowing Through Twelve Chapters and the Bowl of Ambrosia in Your Palm</h4>
<p>Closing the final page of the Sutra on the Original Vows and Merits of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, the scripture comes to rest on sixteen Chinese characters of supreme warmth and completion:</p>
<p>"The entire great assembly, hearing what the Buddha had spoken, were all filled with great joy, accepted it in faith, and put it into practice."</p>
<p>Why, beneath the Music Tree of Vaiśālī, after the Buddha had spoken of birth, aging, sickness, death, hunger, prison, miserliness, pride, and the Nine Untimely Deaths, did the eight thousand bhikṣus, thirty-six thousand bodhisattvas, kings, ministers, brāhmaṇas, householders, and Twelve Yakṣa Generals respond not with gloom or terror, but with great joy (jiē dà huānxǐ)?</p>
<p>Because a true physician never points out an illness to leave the patient in despair; He names the root of the illness only because His hands already hold the unfailing ambrosial medicine!</p>
<p>In this book, looking up toward the radiant Dharma body of the Eastern Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, I have laid before you my thirty-three years of travel since being born by the Ussuri River on the thirtieth day of the ninth lunar month in 1993: from northern snows and my grandfather's Zhouyi coins, through the spotlights of the Dior Homme runway in Beijing and the dusk waters of Victoria Harbour in Hong Kong, down twenty steps into the underground shelter of Underground Fragrance and across to the Lido in Venice, up into the morning mist of Donglin Temple on Mount Lu in Jiangxi before the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli World-Honored One, and finally down to the quiet study lamp in Lingnan.</p>
<p>Now the story is told, and the boat has reached the shore. Just as the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata holds forth His bowl of cool ambrosia amid the moonlight of Vaiśālī and the morning mist of Mount Lu, I place this invisible lapis lazuli medicine bowl into your palm as you finish reading this final line. When you close this book and step back out into the sunlight, wind, and crowd, remember: when ill, seek good doctors in peace; when troubled, keep an upright heart and never shop for superstitious fear; with others, practice loving-kindness, compassion, joy, and equanimity; in solitude, remain transparently clear within and without. Wherever the lapis lazuli light in your heart shines forth, half the sorrows of the mortal world have already melted away, and every step beneath your feet is the Eastern Land of Pure Lapis Lazuli.</p>

<h4>II. Complete English Translation of Master Xuanzang's Sutra on the Original Vows and Merits of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata</h4>
<p>Textual Note: Translated in full from the Tang Dynasty Chinese version by Tripitaka Master Xuanzang (Yàoshī Liúlíguāng Rúlái Běnyuàn Gōngdé Jīng, Taishō Tripiṭaka No. 450), with the bracketed Medicine Buddha Empowerment Dhāraṇī passage incorporated from the standard Chinese monastic liturgical edition (derived from Śrīmitra's Eastern Jin Consecration Sutra and Master Yijing's Tang translation, Taishō No. 451).</p>
<p>Thus have I heard: At one time, the Bhagavān was wandering through various lands, teaching and transforming sentient beings. He arrived at the great city of Vaiśālī and dwelt beneath the Music Tree, together with a great assembly of eight thousand bhikṣus, thirty-six thousand bodhisattva-mahāsattvas, as well as kings, ministers, brāhmaṇas, householders, devas, nāgas, and the eightfold assembly of human and non-human beings—an immeasurable multitude who surrounded Him in reverent homage as He spoke the Dharma.</p>
<p>At that time, Mañjuśrī, Prince of the Dharma, receiving the awesome spiritual power of the Buddha, rose from his seat, bared his right shoulder, placed his right knee on the ground, bowed with palms joined toward the Bhagavān, and spoke thus: "World-Honored One! We earnestly pray that You will expound the names of such Buddhas and the supreme merits of Their original great vows, so that all who hear them may have their karmic obstructions dissolved, for the sake of benefiting and bringing joy to sentient beings in the turning of the Semblance Dharma Age."</p>
<p>At that time, the World-Honored One praised the youth Mañjuśrī, saying: "Excellent! Excellent, Mañjuśrī! Out of great compassion you request that I speak the names of the Buddhas and the merits of Their original vows, in order to rescue sentient beings entangled in karmic obstructions and to benefit and bring peace and joy to sentient beings in the Semblance Dharma Age. Listen attentively now and reflect well; I shall speak of it for you." Mañjuśrī replied: "So be it! We pray You will speak; we joyfully wish to hear."</p>
<p>The Buddha told Mañjuśrī: "To the east of this world, past Buddha-lands as numerous as the sands of ten Ganges Rivers, there is a world named Pure Lapis Lazuli. In that land there is a Buddha named Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, Arhat, Samyaksaṃbuddha, Perfect in Knowledge and Conduct, Sugata, Knower of the World, Unsurpassed Worthy, Tamer of Passions, Teacher of Devas and Humans, Buddha, Bhagavān. Mañjuśrī! When that World-Honored Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata was formerly practicing the Bodhisattva path, He made twelve great vows so that all sentient beings might obtain whatever they seek.</p>
<p>"The First Great Vow: 'I vow that in a future life, when I attain Anuttara-samyak-saṃbodhi, My own body shall blaze with radiant light, illuminating immeasurable, countless, boundless worlds, adorning My body with the thirty-two marks of a great person and the eighty secondary excellences; and I shall cause all sentient beings to become wholly like Me, without any difference.'</p>
<p>"The Second Great Vow: 'I vow that in a future life, when I attain Bodhi, My body shall be like lapis lazuli, transparently clear within and without, spotless and free of blemish; My light shall be vast, My merits towering, My body well-abiding in peace, adorned with a blazing net of light surpassing the sun and moon; and beings dwelling in murky darkness shall all be awakened and illuminated, proceeding freely wherever they wish to accomplish their endeavors.'</p>
<p>"The Third Great Vow: 'I vow that in a future life, when I attain Bodhi, by means of immeasurable, boundless wisdom and skillful means, I shall cause all sentient beings to obtain an inexhaustible supply of the things they need for life, never allowing any living being to suffer from scarcity or lack.'</p>
<p>"The Fourth Great Vow: 'I vow that in a future life, when I attain Bodhi, if there are sentient beings who walk upon deviant paths, I shall cause them all to be securely established within the path of Bodhi; and if there are those who practice the Śrāvaka or Pratyekabuddha vehicles, I shall establish them all within the Great Vehicle (Mahāyāna).'</p>
<p>"The Fifth Great Vow: 'I vow that in a future life, when I attain Bodhi, if there are immeasurable, boundless sentient beings who cultivate pure conduct within My Dharma, I shall cause them all to obtain unblemished precepts and be endowed with the threefold pure precepts; and even if they should commit a transgression or violation, upon hearing My name they shall regain their purity and not fall into evil destinies.'</p>
<p>"The Sixth Great Vow: 'I vow that in a future life, when I attain Bodhi, if there are sentient beings whose bodies are inferior, whose faculties are incomplete, who are ugly, dull, or foolish, blind, deaf, or mute, crippled, hunchbacked, afflicted with leprosy, madness, or all manner of painful illnesses—upon hearing My name, they shall all obtain upright appearance and keen wisdom, with all their faculties complete and free from every sickness and affliction.'</p>
<p>"The Seventh Great Vow: 'I vow that in a future life, when I attain Bodhi, if there are sentient beings who are pressed hard by manifold illnesses, without rescue and without refuge, without physician and without medicine, without relatives and without home, destitute and weighed down by suffering—as soon as My name passes through their ears, all their illnesses shall be dispelled, their bodies and minds shall abide in peace and joy, their families and provisions shall all become abundant, and they shall ultimately realize unsurpassed Bodhi.'</p>
<p>"The Eighth Great Vow: 'I vow that in a future life, when I attain Bodhi, if there are women who are oppressed and tormented by the hundredfold afflictions of female existence, who feel deep weariness and wish to relinquish the female form—upon hearing My name, they shall all be transformed from female to male, endowed with the marks of a great person, and ultimately realize unsurpassed Bodhi.'</p>
<p>"The Ninth Great Vow: 'I vow that in a future life, when I attain Bodhi, I shall cause all sentient beings to escape from the snares and nets of Māra and be liberated from all entanglements of heterodox paths; if they have fallen into dense thickets of various evil views, I shall guide and gather them into right view, gradually causing them to cultivate the practices of bodhisattvas and swiftly realize unsurpassed, right and equal Bodhi.'</p>
<p>"The Tenth Great Vow: 'I vow that in a future life, when I attain Bodhi, if there are sentient beings who are subjected to royal laws, bound with ropes and scourged with whips, fettered and locked in dark prisons, or facing execution, together with countless other disasters and humiliations, tormented by grief and anxiety, suffering in body and mind—if they hear My name, by virtue of My merit and awesome spiritual power, they shall all be liberated from every sorrow and affliction.'</p>
<p>"The Eleventh Great Vow: 'I vow that in a future life, when I attain Bodhi, if there are sentient beings who are tormented by hunger and thirst and create various evil karma in their struggle to obtain food—upon hearing My name and retaining it with focused heart, I shall first satisfy their bodies with exquisite food and drink, and afterward establish them in ultimate peace and joy through the flavor of the Dharma.'</p>
<p>"The Twelfth Great Vow: 'I vow that in a future life, when I attain Bodhi, if there are sentient beings who are destitute and without clothing, tormented day and night by mosquitoes, gadflies, cold, and heat—if they hear My name and retain it with focused heart, according to their wishes they shall immediately obtain all manner of exquisite garments, as well as all precious adornments, flower garlands, fragrant ointments, drum music, and varied arts, fulfilling whatever their hearts desire.'</p>
<p>"Mañjuśrī! These are the twelve subtle and supreme vows made by that World-Honored Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, Arhat, Samyaksaṃbuddha when He was practicing the Bodhisattva path.</p>
<p>"Furthermore, Mañjuśrī! The great vows made by that World-Honored Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata when He was practicing the Bodhisattva path, as well as the meritorious adornments of His Buddha-land, could never be exhausted even if I were to speak of them for a kalpa or more than a kalpa. That Buddha-land is wholly pure, free of women's afflictions, free of evil destinies, and free of any sound of suffering. With lapis lazuli as its ground, golden cords mark its roads; its city walls, gates, palaces, pavilions, balconies, windows, and decorative nets are all formed of the seven precious gems—wholly equal to, and no different from, the meritorious adornments of the Western Land of Ultimate Bliss. Within His land there are two bodhisattva-mahāsattvas: the first is named Sunlight Pervading Everywhere, and the second is named Moonlight Pervading Everywhere. They stand at the head of that immeasurable, countless assembly of bodhisattvas, next in succession to fill the Buddha-seat, and are fully able to uphold the treasury of the True Dharma of that World-Honored Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata. Therefore, Mañjuśrī, all men and women of devout faith ought to vow to be reborn in that Buddha's world."</p>
<p>At that time, the World-Honored One again spoke to the youth Mañjuśrī, saying: "Mañjuśrī! There are sentient beings who do not discern good from evil, who harbor only greed and stinginess, who do not know of giving or the fruits of giving, foolish and devoid of wisdom, lacking the root of faith. They accumulate great stores of wealth and treasure and guard them with anxious diligence. Seeing a beggar approach, their hearts feel no joy; and if they are left with no choice but to give, it feels like slicing flesh from their own bodies, arousing deep pain and regret. Moreover, there are countless miserly and greedy beings who amass wealth and property that they do not even bear to use for themselves—how much less could they give to their parents, wives, children, servants, laborers, or mendicants who come asking? Those beings, upon the end of this life, are reborn in the realm of hungry ghosts or among beasts; yet because in their former human life they once briefly heard the name of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, now while in evil destinies they briefly recall that Tathāgata's name, and at the very moment of recollection they vanish from that place and are reborn among humans. They regain remembrance of past lives, dread the sufferings of evil destinies, take no delight in sensual indulgence, gladly practice generous giving, praise those who give, and begrudge nothing they possess, gradually becoming able to offer even their own head, eyes, hands, feet, flesh, and blood to those who come asking—how much more so external wealth and property!</p>
<p>"Furthermore, Mañjuśrī! If there are sentient beings who, though receiving training precepts from the Tathāgata, break moral discipline; or some who do not break moral discipline yet violate proper deportment and rules; or some who preserve moral discipline and deportment intact yet destroy right view; or some who do not destroy right view yet abandon wide learning, failing to comprehend the profound meaning of the sutras spoken by the Buddha; or some who possess wide learning yet harbor overweening pride—because their hearts are clouded by overweening pride, they affirm only themselves and disparage others, slander the True Dharma, and become companions of Māra. Such foolish persons practice evil views themselves and further cause countless koṭis of sentient beings to plunge into a great, perilous pit. Those sentient beings would otherwise revolve endlessly through hells, animal realms, and ghost destinies; yet if they hear the name of this Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, they abandon evil conduct, cultivate wholesome Dharmas, and do not fall into evil destinies. Even if there are those unable immediately to abandon all evil conduct and cultivate wholesome Dharmas who fall into evil destinies, by the awesome power of that Tathāgata's original vows, causing them briefly to hear His name before them, upon the end of that life they are reborn in the human realm, attaining right view and diligence, well-harmonized in aspiration, able to leave household entanglement for the homelessness of truth, upholding training rules without violation within the Tathāgata's Dharma, endowed with right view and wide learning, comprehending profound meaning, free from overweening pride, never slandering the True Dharma, never becoming companions of Māra, gradually cultivating the practices of bodhisattvas to swift perfection.</p>
<p>"Furthermore, Mañjuśrī! If there are sentient beings who are greedy and envious, praising themselves and disparaging others, they shall fall into the three evil destinies and endure acute suffering for immeasurable thousands of years; after enduring acute suffering, upon the end of that life they are reborn in the human world as oxen, horses, camels, or donkeys, constantly flogged with whips, tormented by hunger and thirst, forever carrying heavy loads along the road; or if they obtain human form, they are born in lowly station as servants and bondmaids driven by others, never enjoying autonomy. Yet if in a former human life they once heard the name of the World-Honored Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, by virtue of that wholesome cause they now recall it again and take refuge with wholehearted sincerity; through the Buddha's spiritual power all their sufferings are dispelled, their faculties become keen and bright, wise and widely learned, constantly seeking supreme Dharma, always meeting good spiritual friends, forever severing the snares of Māra, breaking open the shell of ignorance, drying up the river of afflictions, and liberating themselves from all birth, aging, sickness, death, grief, and torment.</p>
<p>"Furthermore, Mañjuśrī! If there are sentient beings who delight in division and estrangement, engaging in mutual strife and litigation, troubling themselves and others, creating and increasing all manner of evil karma with body, speech, and mind, constantly plotting mutual harm; summoning spirits of mountains, forests, trees, and tombs; killing living creatures to take their flesh and blood in sacrifice to yakṣas and rākṣasas; writing down the names of enemies and fashioning their effigies to curse them with malicious spells; practicing hexes, gu-poison, and corpse-raising sorcery to cut short their lives and destroy their bodies—if those sentient beings hear the name of this Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, none of those malicious deeds can harm them; all involved turn about and give rise to hearts of loving-kindness, benefiting and comforting one another without intent to harm or resentment, each rejoicing and feeling content with what they have, neither invading nor humiliating one another, but mutually bringing benefit.</p>
<p>"Furthermore, Mañjuśrī! If among the fourfold assembly—bhikṣus, bhikṣuṇīs, upāsakas, upāsikās—and other men and women of pure faith, there are those who uphold the eightfold fast precepts, whether for a year or for three months upholding the training rules, and by virtue of this wholesome root vow to be reborn in the Western Land of Ultimate Bliss before Amitāyus Buddha to hear the True Dharma, yet whose resolve is not yet firmly settled: if they hear the name of the World-Honored Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, at the approach of death eight great bodhisattvas—namely Mañjuśrī Bodhisattva, Avalokiteśvara Bodhisattva, Mahāsthāmaprāpta Bodhisattva, Akṣayamati Bodhisattva, Ratnacandana-puṣpa Bodhisattva, Bhaiṣajyarāja Bodhisattva, Bhaiṣajyasamudgata Bodhisattva, and Maitreya Bodhisattva—shall come flying through the sky to show them the way, and they shall immediately be born by transformation amidst multicolored jeweled lotus blossoms in that land. Or some by this cause are born in the heavens; though born in the heavens, their original wholesome root is not exhausted, and they never again fall into any evil destiny. When their heavenly lifespan ends and they return to the human world, they may become wheel-turning sage kings governing the four continents with sovereign virtue and establishing countless hundreds of thousands of sentient beings in the path of the ten wholesome deeds; or they are born into great families of kṣatriyas, brāhmaṇas, or householders, with abundant treasure and overflowing storehouses, upright appearance, harmonious kin, keen intelligence, and heroic strength; and if a woman hears the name of the World-Honored Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata and wholeheartedly upholds it, she shall never again be bound by the afflictions of female existence.</p>
<p>"[The following Medicine Buddha Empowerment Dhāraṇī passage is incorporated in standard monastic liturgical editions from the translations of Śrīmitra and Master Yijing] Furthermore, Mañjuśrī! When that Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata attained Bodhi, by the power of His original vows He observed sentient beings encountering manifold painful illnesses—emaciation, convulsion, consumption, jaundice, fever, and the like; or struck by sorcery and gu-poison; or short-lived, or dying an untimely death. Wishing to cause all such illnesses and sufferings to be dispelled and all wishes to be fulfilled, at that time the World-Honored One entered a samādhi named 'Eliminating the Sufferings and Afflictions of All Sentient Beings.' Having entered this concentration, from His cranial uṣṇīṣa He emitted a great radiant light, and within that light proclaimed a great dhāraṇī:</p>
<p>'Namo bhagavate bhaiṣajyaguru-vaiḍūrya-prabha-rājāya tathāgatāya arhate samyaksaṃbuddhāya. Tadyathā: Oṃ bhaiṣajye bhaiṣajye bhaiṣajya-samudgate svāhā.'</p>
<p>"At that time, when this mantra had been spoken within the light, the great earth quaked and emitted a great radiance, dispelling the illnesses and sufferings of all sentient beings so that they enjoyed peace and bliss. Mañjuśrī! If you see a man or woman suffering from illness, you should with a single, undivided heart, on behalf of that sick person, regularly bathe and rinse until clean, and over either food, or medicine, or insect-free clean water, recite this dhāraṇī one hundred and eight times and give it to them to take; all their painful illnesses shall be relieved. If they seek anything, reciting with wholehearted sincerity, they shall obtain freedom from illness and prolonged years, and after the end of this life be reborn in that world, attaining non-retrogression all the way to Bodhi. Therefore, Mañjuśrī, if there are men or women who cherish deep reverence and make offerings to that Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, they should constantly uphold this mantra and never let it be forgotten. [End of incorporated liturgical passage]</p>
<p>"Furthermore, Mañjuśrī! If there are men and women of pure faith who hear all the names of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, Arhat, Samyaksaṃbuddha, and upon hearing them recite and uphold them—chewing the tooth-wood at dawn, bathing and rinsing until clean, offering various fragrant flowers, burned incense, anointing incense, and varied musical arts before His image; and regarding this classic Sutra, whether copying it themselves or instructing others to copy it, wholeheartedly upholding it and listening to its meaning; and toward the Dharma master who expounds it, making offerings of all necessities of life without allowing any lack—such persons shall receive the protective mindfulness of all Buddhas, have all their wishes fulfilled, and progress all the way to Bodhi."</p>
<p>At that time, the youth Mañjuśrī said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One! I vow that in the turning of the Semblance Dharma Age, by means of manifold skillful means I shall cause men and women of pure faith to hear the name of the World-Honored Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, even awakening their ears with the Buddha's name in their sleep. World-Honored One! If anyone upholds, reads, and recites this Sutra, or expounds and reveals it to others; whether copying it themselves or instructing others to copy it; revering and honoring it with various flower scents, anointing incense, powdered incense, burned incense, garlands, necklaces, banners, canopies, and music; making a bag of five-colored silk to hold it; sweeping and sprinkling a clean place, setting up a high seat, and placing it securely thereon—at that time the Four Heavenly Kings with their retinues and countless hundreds of thousands of other devas shall all approach that place to make offerings and stand guard. World-Honored One! Wherever this precious Sutra circulates and is upheld, by virtue of the original vows and merits of that World-Honored Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata and through hearing His name, know that such a place shall be free of untimely death, nor shall anyone have their vital essence snatched away by evil spirits, and even if it has been impaired, they shall regain wholeness and abide in peace and joy of body and mind."</p>
<p>The Buddha told Mañjuśrī: "So it is! So it is, just as you have spoken. Mañjuśrī! If men and women of pure faith wish to make offerings to that World-Honored Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, they should first fashion an image of that Buddha, prepare a clean seat, and place it securely thereon; scatter various flowers, burn various incenses, and adorn the place with banners and pennants. For seven days and seven nights they should uphold the eightfold fast precepts, eat pure food, bathe until fragrant and clean, wear clean garments, and give rise to an unstained mind and a non-harming mind; toward all sentient beings they should give rise to thoughts of benefit and peace, and an equal heart of loving-kindness, compassion, empathetic joy, and equanimity, playing music and singing praises while circumambulating the Buddha image to the right. Furthermore, they should recollect the merits of that Tathāgata's original vows, read and recite this Sutra, reflect upon its meaning, and expound and reveal it. Whatever they may wish for and seek, all shall be fulfilled: seeking long life, they obtain long life; seeking abundant wealth, they obtain abundant wealth; seeking official position, they obtain official position; seeking sons and daughters, they obtain sons and daughters.</p>
<p>"Furthermore, if someone suddenly has bad dreams and sees ominous signs; or strange birds gather; or a hundred uncanny portents appear at their dwelling—if that person reverently makes offerings with fine provisions to that World-Honored Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, all bad dreams, ominous signs, and inauspiciousness shall vanish completely and cause no harm. If faced with the terror of water, fire, blades, poison, precipices, fierce elephants, lions, tigers, wolves, bears, brown bears, venomous snakes, scorpions, centipedes, millipedes, or mosquitoes and gadflies—if they wholeheartedly recollect that Buddha and make reverent offerings, they shall be liberated from all such fears. If foreign realms invade or bandits and rebels cause turmoil, those who recollect and revere that Tathāgata shall likewise be delivered.</p>
<p>"Furthermore, Mañjuśrī! If men and women of pure faith for the rest of their lives do not serve otherworldly spirits, but wholeheartedly take refuge in the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha and uphold the precepts—whether the five precepts, the ten precepts, the four hundred bodhisattva precepts, the two hundred and fifty bhikṣu precepts, or the five hundred bhikṣuṇī precepts—and if among the precepts they have received they commit a violation and fear falling into evil destinies, should they single-mindedly recollect that Buddha's name and make reverent offerings, they shall definitely never undergo rebirth in the three evil destinies. And if a woman at the hour of childbirth endures extreme pain, if she wholeheartedly calls upon and praises His name and makes reverent offerings to that Tathāgata, all pains shall be relieved, and the child born shall be complete in physical form, upright in appearance, bringing joy to all who behold them, keen and intelligent, peaceful and rarely ill, with no non-human spirit able to snatch away their vital essence."</p>
<p>At that time, the World-Honored One told Ānanda: "As I have praised the merits possessed by that World-Honored Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, these are the profound spheres of practice of the Buddhas, difficult to fathom thoroughly—do you believe them or not?"</p>
<p>Ānanda replied: "Virtuous World-Honored One! I harbor no doubt concerning the sutras spoken by the Tathāgata. Why is this so? Because all bodily, verbal, and mental deeds of the Tathāgatas are wholly pure. World-Honored One! These orbs of sun and moon may be made to fall, the King of Mount Sumeru may be shaken from its base, yet the words of the Buddhas never vary! World-Honored One, there are sentient beings whose root of faith is incomplete; hearing of the profound spheres of practice of the Buddhas, they think to themselves: 'How could merely recollecting the single name of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata yield such supreme merits and benefits?' Because of this disbelief they turn to slander, losing great benefit and joy through the long night of ignorance."</p>
<p>The Buddha told Ānanda: "For sentient beings who hear the name of the World-Honored Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata and wholeheartedly uphold it without giving rise to doubt, falling into evil destinies is an impossibility. Ānanda! This is the profound practice of the Buddhas, difficult to believe and comprehend; that you are now able to accept it, you should know, is entirely through the awesome power of the Tathāgata. Ānanda! All śrāvakas, pratyekabuddhas, and bodhisattvas who have not yet ascended the grounds are unable to believe and comprehend it as it truly is—excepting only bodhisattvas bound to a single final lifetime before Buddhahood. Ānanda! A human body is difficult to obtain; faith, reverence, and honor toward the Three Jewels are likewise difficult to obtain; and hearing the name of the World-Honored Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata is even more rare than that. Ānanda! The immeasurable bodhisattva practices, immeasurable skillful means, and immeasurable vast vows of that Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata—if I were to expound them at length for a kalpa or more than a kalpa, the kalpa would swiftly come to an end, yet the practices, vows, and skillful means of that Buddha would never be exhausted!"</p>
<p>At that time, among the assembly there was a bodhisattva-mahāsattva named Tranamukta, who rose from his seat, bared his right shoulder, placed his right knee on the ground, bowed with palms joined, and said to the Buddha: "Virtuous World-Honored One! In the turning of the Semblance Dharma Age, if there are sentient beings who are afflicted and distressed by manifold illnesses, wasted and emaciated by prolonged sickness, unable to eat or drink, with parched throat and lips, seeing darkness in every direction as signs of death appear before them; while parents, relatives, friends, and acquaintances weep around them—while that person's body lies in its original place, they see the messengers of Yama leading their consciousness before King Yama of the Dharma. Moreover, every sentient being has an inborn spirit who records in full whatever they do, whether transgression or merit, and presents the entire record to King Yama. At that time, that King questions the person and tallies their deeds, judging them according to their transgressions and merits. If at that time the sick person's relatives and friends can take refuge on their behalf in the World-Honored Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, invite monastic assemblies to read and recite this Sutra, kindle a seven-tiered lamp, and hang a five-colored life-continuity spirit banner, it may well happen that their consciousness is able to return, as though clearly witnessing everything inside a dream. Or after seven days, or twenty-one days, or thirty-five days, or forty-nine days, when their consciousness returns as though waking from a dream, they remember for themselves the retributions of wholesome and unwholesome karma; and because they have personally witnessed the fruits of karma, even at the peril of their lives they never again commit any evil deed. Therefore, men and women of pure faith should all uphold the name of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata and make reverent offerings according to their ability."</p>
<p>At that time, Ānanda asked Tranamukta Bodhisattva: "Virtuous One! How should one reverently make offerings to that World-Honored Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, and how should the life-continuity banners and lamps be fashioned?"</p>
<p>Tranamukta Bodhisattva said: "Virtuous One! If there is a sick person who wishes to be released from the suffering of illness, on their behalf one should uphold the eightfold fast precepts for seven days and seven nights, and with food, drink, and other provisions according to one's means make offerings to the monastic Saṅgha; at the six periods of day and night worship and circumambulate, making offerings to that World-Honored Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata; read and recite this Sutra forty-nine times; kindle forty-nine lamps; fashion seven images of that Tathāgata, placing seven lamps before each image, each lamp as large in radiance as a cartwheel, keeping the light unbroken for forty-nine days; fashion a five-colored banner forty-nine hand-spans in length, and release forty-nine living creatures of various kinds; then they may pass safely through danger and calamity, and no longer be seized by untimely evil spirits.</p>
<p>"Furthermore, Ānanda! When disasters arise for anointed kṣatriya kings and leaders—such as the disaster of epidemics among the populace, the disaster of foreign invasion, the disaster of internal rebellion, the disaster of anomalies among the stars and constellations, the disaster of eclipses of sun and moon, the disaster of unseasonable wind and rain, or the disaster of prolonged drought—those anointed kṣatriya kings should at that time give rise to a heart of compassion toward all sentient beings and pardon those who are fettered and imprisoned; and in accordance with the method of offering spoken earlier, make offerings to that World-Honored Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata. By virtue of this wholesome root and the power of that Tathāgata's original vows, their realm shall immediately obtain peace, wind and rain shall arrive in season, grain crops shall ripen to maturity, all sentient beings shall be free of illness and filled with joy, and within their realm no violent yakṣas or spirits shall trouble living beings; all ominous signs shall vanish, and those anointed kṣatriya kings shall gain increase in lifespan, vitality, health, and ease. Ānanda! If empresses, consorts, crown princes, princes, ministers, prime ministers, palace attendants, officials, or common people are afflicted by illness or other calamities, they too should erect five-colored spirit banners, kindle lamps to continue the light, release living creatures, scatter multicolored flowers, and burn fine incense, whereby illnesses shall be healed and all difficulties resolved."</p>
<p>At that time, Ānanda asked Tranamukta Bodhisattva: "Virtuous One! How can a life that is already nearing exhaustion be prolonged?"</p>
<p>Tranamukta Bodhisattva said: "Virtuous One! Have you not heard the Tathāgata speak of the nine untimely deaths? For this reason I urge the fashioning of life-continuity banners and lamps and the cultivation of manifold merits; through cultivating merit, one fulfills one's natural lifespan without undergoing bitter calamity."</p>
<p>Ānanda asked: "What are the nine untimely deaths?"</p>
<p>Tranamukta Bodhisattva said: "If there are sentient beings whose illness is slight, yet who lack both medicine, physician, and caregiver—or even if they meet a physician, are given the wrong medicine—so that though they ought not to die, they suffer an untimely death; and furthermore, they believe in worldly demonic heterodoxies and charlatan masters who falsely proclaim fortune and misfortune, thereby giving rise to panic and agitation; because their minds are not upright within, they consult divinations in search of calamity, slaughter various living creatures, petition spirits, summon ghosts and goblins to beg for blessings, hoping to prolong their years, yet can never succeed. Through folly and delusion, believing in distorted and evil views, they bring about an untimely death and enter hell with no fixed time of release—this is called the First Untimely Death. The Second is untimely execution by royal law. The Third is hunting and carousing, indulging in sexual dissipation and strong drink without restraint, so that their vital essence is untimely snatched away by non-human spirits. The Fourth is untimely death by burning in fire. The Fifth is untimely drowning in water. The Sixth is untimely being devoured by various vicious beasts. The Seventh is untimely falling off a mountain cliff. The Eighth is untimely harm from poison, hexes, curses, or corpse-demons. The Ninth is starvation and thirst, dying an untimely death from lack of food and drink. These are the nine kinds of untimely death briefly spoken of by the Tathāgata; besides these, there are countless other untimely calamities difficult to recount in full.</p>
<p>"Furthermore, Ānanda! That King Yama presides over the register of names in the world; if sentient beings commit unfiliality or the five grave offenses, slander the Three Jewels, violate the laws of ruler and subject, or break faith and precepts, King Yama of the Dharma examines and punishes them according to the weight of their offenses. For this reason I now urge all sentient beings to kindle lamps, fashion banners, release living beings, and cultivate merit, so as to pass beyond suffering and calamity and encounter no distress."</p>
<p>At that time, among the assembly there were twelve yakṣa generals seated together—namely, General Kumbhīra, General Vajra, General Mihira, General Andīra, General Anila, General Śaṇḍila, General Indāla, General Pajra, General Mahoraga, General Kinnara, General Catura, and General Vikarāla. Each of these twelve yakṣa generals had seven thousand yakṣas as his retinue. Raising their voices together, they said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One! Now, having received the Buddha's awesome power and heard the name of the World-Honored Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, we no longer have any fear of evil destinies. Leading one another with a single united heart, for the rest of our lives we take refuge in the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha, vowing to shoulder the burden of all sentient beings, working for their benefit, enrichment, peace, and joy. In whatever village, town, capital city, or quiet forest this Sutra circulates, or wherever someone upholds the name of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata and makes reverent offerings, our retinues shall guard and protect that person, causing them to be liberated from all suffering and difficulty and fulfilling all their wishes. And if there are those in illness or distress who seek deliverance, they too should read and recite this Sutra, and with a five-colored cord tie our names; once their wish has been fulfilled, they may untie the knot."</p>
<p>At that time, the World-Honored One praised the yakṣa generals, saying: "Excellent! Excellent, great yakṣa generals! When you think of repaying the kindness and virtue of the World-Honored Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata, you should ever benefit and bring peace and joy to all sentient beings in this way."</p>
<p>At that time, Ānanda asked the Buddha: "World-Honored One! By what name should this Dharma gate be called, and how should we uphold it?"</p>
<p>The Buddha told Ānanda: "This Dharma gate is named Expounding the Merits of the Original Vows of the Medicine Master Lapis Lazuli Light Tathāgata; it is also named Expounding the Spirit Mantra by Which the Twelve Divine Generals Benefit Sentient Beings and Tie Their Vows; it is also named Uprooting All Karmic Obstructions. Thus should you uphold it."</p>
<p>When the Bhagavān had spoken these words, all the bodhisattva-mahāsattvas, the great śrāvakas, kings, ministers, brāhmaṇas, householders, devas, nāgas, yakṣas, gandharvas, asuras, garuḍas, kinnaras, mahoragas, human and non-human beings—the entire great assembly, hearing what the Buddha had spoken, were all filled with great joy, accepted it in faith, and put it into practice.</p>
