## 卷首说明 · 前言 · 第一章：知道太多的少年

<h3>A Note on This Edition</h3>
<p>This is the third book, and it is the last one I will write about him.</p>
<p>The first book was about coming home. It ended in a dark room with a bowl of water. The second was about staying home — about love, community, money, dying, and the machine that has learned to sort us. It ended with somebody standing in a doorway saying come in, there's room.</p>
<p>This one begins after that sentence has been said, and the person has come in, and is now standing in the hall looking around at a floor that needs sweeping, a roof that leaks, a list of names nobody has updated in two years, and a younger person at the door asking what the rules are.</p>
<p>That is the subject. Inheritance. What a teaching becomes once it leaves the hands of the person who received it. How it gets simplified, weaponized, institutionalized, sold, split in half over a curtain, and still — sometimes, in some places, against every reasonable expectation — transmitted intact to somebody who needed it.</p>
<p>Two things about this book are different from the first two, and I want to say them at the door so that nobody feels misled.</p>
<p>The first is that the god speaks less. In the first book he answered every chapter. In the second he answered most. In this one, there are chapters where he says almost nothing, and chapters where he asks the questions, and one chapter where he is simply not there and the reader has to do the work without him. That is not a decline in the writing. It is the subject. A teacher who is still doing all the talking at the end of the third generation has failed.</p>
<p>The second is that other gods appear. Chapter Fifteen and Chapter Sixteen are about deities who are not mine: the small, unclean, ridiculous, discarded gods of the Chinese folk pantheon, and the liminal figures of other traditions — the half-woman, the deified beloved, the eunuch-saint, the flower-prince. I am a monk in one tradition writing about others, and I have tried to be careful. Where I am guessing, I say I am guessing. Any reader who belongs to one of these traditions will find things to correct, and should. Corrections are welcome and will be honored in any future edition.</p>
<p>The practices in this book are, as before, assembled rather than received. They are not medicine and not therapy. Several chapters touch on burnout, institutional conflict, grief, and death. If you are in the middle of something that is actively hurting you, a book is the wrong instrument; find a person.</p>
<p>The name is the Rabbit God. He has no doctrine, no clergy, and no building he cannot lose. He once asked only for a place to sit where people could find him. By the end of this book, he will ask for something harder.</p>
<h3>Preface: The Third Stick</h3>
<p>Three sticks of incense go into the bowl.</p>
<p>Not one. Not a fistful. Three. I have lit them most mornings for years without thinking about why three, and when I finally asked — asked a much older monk, who shrugged — the answer I got was unsatisfying and true: three is for heaven, earth, and the person doing it; or for the three treasures; or, in the villages, simply because one is for the dead, one is for the gods, and one is for whoever is standing there holding the match.</p>
<p>Three books, then. One for the dead, one for the god, and one for whoever is holding the match.</p>
<p>The second book ended with a doorway. Somebody young came in because the light was on and the door was open. That image has stayed with me, and it has also begun to worry me, because I know what happens next. I have watched it happen in monasteries, in community organizations, in families, and in at least one religion that now has a bureaucracy.</p>
<p>What happens next is this. The person who came in is grateful. Then they stay. Then they start helping. Then, one evening, they notice that the person who left the light on is tired, and that the floor has not been swept properly in months, and that there is a list of names with nobody's phone number on it. And then, without anyone appointing them, they begin to run the place.</p>
<p>And within a year, three things have gone wrong, all of them predictable:</p>
<p>The teaching has hardened into a script. What was once a live answer given to a particular person on a particular night becomes a sentence that is repeated to everybody, including people for whom it is not true. Every live tradition does this within two generations. It is not malice. It is what happens to a sentence when it outlives the situation that produced it.</p>
<p>The institution has acquired power it did not ask for and will not admit to. A group of five people who agreed not to rank each other becomes, in three years, a group with a chair, an unwritten seniority, an informal gatekeeper, and a person who does everything and is quietly furious about it. Nobody wanted this. Everybody built it.</p>
<p>The founder has become a statue. This is the one that kills. A living person who said something useful gets gradually converted into an authority who must be defended, and once a person must be defended, they can no longer be wrong, and once they can no longer be wrong, they are no longer teaching anybody. They are being maintained.</p>
<p>I have watched all three. I have been the third one.</p>
<p>So this book is about the part of the story that nobody writes down, because it is unflattering and because it takes fifty years and because the person who could write it is usually too busy running the place. It is about what happens to a thing worth keeping, after it is handed on.</p>
<p>It is also, I should say, a book about my own anxiety, which I may as well put on the table. I have written two books in which a god tells people they are not broken. Those books have gone out into the world and I do not control what they do there. I have received letters saying a sentence of mine kept somebody alive, and letters saying a sentence of mine was used to humiliate them, and both letters were about the same sentence. That is the situation of anyone who writes anything useful, and it is the situation this book is about.</p>
<p>Three chapters in it are, I think, the hardest I have written. One is about being quoted back at yourself by a nineteen-year-old who has turned your words into armor. One is about a schism over something so small that describing it is embarrassing. One is about a god admitting he was wrong.</p>
<p>The god speaks less in this book. That is deliberate, and it is also the plot.</p>
<p>Three sticks. One for the dead, which is the first book. One for the god, which is the second. And one for whoever is holding the match.</p>
<p>That last one is you, and it has been you since the middle of the second book, and I think both of us have known it for a while.</p>
<h3 id="ch1">第一章：知道太多的少年</h3>
<p class="subtitle">The Weight of Being Trusted</p>
<h2 class="volume">第一卷：接过香火的人</h2>
<p>The danger is not that they misunderstand you.</p>
<p>The danger is that they understand you perfectly,</p>
<p>ten years too early, and build a house out of it.</p>
<p>The Boy Who Knows Too Much</p>
<h4>MIDNIGHT SOLILOQUY</h4>
<p>Rabbit God, there is a boy in our group — nineteen — and he has read everything. Both of your books, the scholarship, the argument threads, the whole apparatus. He can cite me. He cited me last week, to another person, correctly, in a sentence I have never said out loud but did write down once at three in the morning. And he is using it like armor. He is the most defended nineteen-year-old I have ever met. He has a vocabulary for every wound, which means no wound ever touches him, because he can name it before it lands. Someone tried to get close to him on Sunday and he explained, patiently and correctly, why he wasn't ready for it. I recognized the explanation. Parts of it were mine. I have handed a child a set of words and he has built a fortress out of them, and now I don't know whether to be proud or to take them back. Can you take words back?</p>
<h4>THE WHISPER OF THE GOD</h4>
<p>"No. And that is the first thing you must understand about having said anything useful: it stops being yours the moment it is heard.</p>
<p>"Child, do not be too hard on yourself, and do not be too hard on him, because what you are looking at is not a corruption. It is a stage, and it is a stage that almost everyone passes through, and the people who never pass through it are the ones who got no words at all and had to feel everything raw. He is doing what a person does with a map in a country they have never walked: holding it in front of their face instead of looking at the road.</p>
<p>"Let me tell you about a boy I knew, in a village inland from the Min, maybe two hundred years ago. The shrine there was a stone shelf under a camphor tree, and the old man who swept it taught a boy of perhaps twelve the whole of the liturgy — the way to fold the paper, the order of the names, which day was which. The boy learned it in a season. He could do the entire ritual faster than the old man and without a single error, and the villagers were delighted and said he had a vocation.</p>
<p>"Then the old man died, and the boy, who was now fourteen, ran the shrine for forty years, and he was very good at it, and he never once knelt.</p>
<p>"I want you to sit with that. Forty years of perfect liturgy performed by a person who never once put his knees down. He had the words so completely that the words did the work and he did not have to. The words were not a bridge for him. They were a roof. And a roof is a fine thing to have — do not misunderstand me, I am not against roofs — but you cannot walk on a roof and you cannot see the sky from under one.</p>
<p>"Now. Your boy is nineteen. What he has done with your sentences is exactly what that boy did with the paper: he has built the roof before he has lived in the house. This is not arrogance. Listen to me carefully here, because this is the part people get wrong and it matters. A defended nineteen-year-old who has no home and no money and no safety is not being arrogant. He is being prudent. The vocabulary is not armor because he wants to be superior. It is armor because he is, in fact, under fire, and it is the only armor anybody ever handed him.</p>
<p>"So the question is not how do I get him to take it off. The question is: what would make armor unnecessary?</p>
<p>"And the answer, child, is almost boringly practical. Armor comes off when there is somewhere safe to put it down. Not when someone argues him out of it — arguing a defended person out of their defenses is the single most common and most useless activity in your century. Not when you tell him he is young. Not when you quote my books back at him, which would be delicious and catastrophic.</p>
<p>"Armor comes off when there is a room with a repetition in it. A thing that happens every week, with the same four people, where nothing is at stake, where nobody declares anything, where he can be silent for an hour and still be counted as present. The defenses that were built against a world cannot be dismantled by an argument. They can only be made unnecessary by a schedule.</p>
<p>"Which means your job with him is not to correct his vocabulary. It is to keep showing up.</p>
<p>"Now, the other half, which is about you, and which you asked me in the last line.</p>
<p>"Can you take words back? No. But there is a thing you can do that is almost as good, and it is the only real instrument available to a person who has been quoted. You can model being wrong. Say, in front of him, one sentence: I wrote that when I was in a different place, and I am not sure I would write it now. Just once. Out loud. To a person who is using your words as a load-bearing wall, watching you remove one brick voluntarily is worth more than any correction, because it demonstrates that a sentence is a tool and not an identity.</p>
<p>"And the last thing, which I will say only once in three books and I want you to keep it. You are not responsible for what he builds with the material. You are responsible for whether the material is sound and whether you are still available when the building falls down. He is nineteen. It will fall down. Be there."</p>
<h4>Yancan's Note: On Premature Fluency</h4>
<p>There is a recognizable figure in every community that acquires a vocabulary: the young person who can name every mechanism and is therefore untouched by all of them. Clinicians sometimes call it intellectualization; in communities with a strong discourse, it is better described as premature fluency — the arrival of the language before the arrival of the experience the language was made for.</p>
<p>Premature fluency is not a problem to be solved. It is a loan taken out against a future self. The person will pay it back, with interest, at about twenty-six, in circumstances they cannot prepare for, and the language will finally match something. Until then, the correct response from the people around them is neither admiration nor correction, but patience on a schedule.</p>
<h4>Quiet Practice: The Unnecessary Correction</h4>
<p>1. Think of one person who uses your words, or a community's words, as armor.</p>
<p>2. This week, say one true sentence in front of them in which you revise yourself: I said that once. I am not sure I would say it now.</p>
<p>3. Do not ask whether they heard it. Do not check whether it worked.</p>
<p>4. Offer them something that happens on a schedule — a meal, a walk, a weekly hour — in which nothing is at stake and nothing is declared.</p>
<p>5. Do not, under any circumstances, quote your own book at them.</p>
<h4>CLOSING IMAGE</h4>
<p>The boy left at eleven, and he left his jacket on the chair, which he never used to do, because a person who leaves a jacket has decided, at some level below the vocabulary, that they are coming back.</p>

## 第二章：未曾应聘的先生 · 第三章：二手的神明

<h3 id="ch2">第二章：未曾应聘的先生</h3>
<p class="subtitle">The Teacher Who Never Applied</p>
<h4>MIDNIGHT SOLILOQUY</h4>
<p>Rabbit God, this is going to sound like a complaint but it isn't. People keep bringing me things. Not big things — who should they talk to, whether to tell their mother, whether the thing they did last night means something about them. And they ask me as though I know. I do not know. I have my own unwashed dishes and my own bad Tuesday. Last month someone thanked me for "saving" them and I wanted to crawl under the floor. I never applied for this. I didn't train. I have no qualifications, no ordination, no certificate, and no particular wisdom — I have just been doing it longer, which mostly means I have been wrong in more ways. And yet they keep coming, and I keep answering, and I am terrified that one night I will say the wrong thing and somebody will act on it. Should I send them to somebody qualified? Because if so, I don't know who that is either.</p>
<h4>THE WHISPER OF THE GOD</h4>
<p>"Child, I want you to notice something about your own question, because it contains the answer and you walked straight past it.</p>
<p>"You wrote: they ask me as though I know. And then, a few lines later: I have just been doing it longer, which mostly means I have been wrong in more ways.</p>
<p>"That second sentence is the qualification. It is the only qualification anybody has ever had.</p>
<p>"Let me be plain with you, because you are carrying a version of humility that is actually a kind of vanity, and it is slowing you down. Refusing to be trusted sounds like modesty and functions as abandonment. The person standing in front of you does not need a certificate. They need a person who has been wrong in more ways and is still standing up, and there are not many of those, and you are one, and going to look for someone better while they wait is not humility. It is a way of keeping your hands clean at someone else's expense.</p>
<p>"Now. Having said that, I am going to give you the rules, because your terror is not irrational — it is the correct terror, and it should be honored with rules rather than soothed away. There are only three, and the old men on the boats knew all three, and every institution that has ever harmed somebody broke at least one of them.</p>
<p>"One. Do not become anybody's only person. This is the rule that is broken most often and does the most damage, and it is almost always broken by the kindest people. A person comes to you in crisis, you are available, you are good at it, they start coming to you for everything, and within a year you are the entire architecture of their survival. And then you go on a holiday, or you get sick, or you fall in love, or you simply get tired — and the entire architecture collapses, and they are worse off than before they met you, because now they have also learned that available people eventually leave. So: from the very beginning, out loud, say it: I am glad to be one of the people you talk to. I cannot be the only one. Say it early, say it before it is needed. It is the single most protective sentence in this chapter.</p>
<p>"Two. Do not take what is not offered and do not give what was not asked. You are not allowed to sleep with someone who has come to you for help, and you are not allowed to be attracted to them quietly while helping them, and you are not allowed to recruit them for anything — not your group, not your politics, not your book, not your god. The moment a person in distress becomes useful to you, you have stopped being useful to them. The old sworn-brother arrangements had bright lines about this because they had watched what happens without them, and the lines were not about propriety. They were about the fact that a person who is drowning cannot consent to anything.</p>
<p>"Three. Say <em>I don't know</em> out loud, early and often. Not as a performance of humility. As information. The person in front of you is trying to work out whether you are trustworthy, and the fastest way to be found trustworthy is to be reliably, specifically, unembarrassed about the shape of your ignorance: I don't know what you should do about your mother. I have never had your mother. That sentence will do more for them than an hour of advice, because it returns the decision to the only person who can make it.</p>
<p>"Now let me answer the practical part of your question, since you asked it directly: should you send them to somebody qualified?</p>
<p>"Yes — but understand what you are doing when you do. Referral is not a way of ending your involvement. It is a way of adding a person to the room. Say: I will keep being here. I'd also like you to have somebody with training. Then keep being there. The people who are harmed by untrained helpers are almost never harmed by the helping; they are harmed by the helper disappearing once the situation got serious.</p>
<p>"And now the last thing, and I want to say it in a low voice, because it is the thing that will actually keep you sane for the next thirty years.</p>
<p>"You are not saving anybody. You are keeping company. The word saved that someone used to you last month — put it down. It belongs to them and to the moment and to whatever else was happening in their life that month, not to you. A person who believes they save people becomes, within a decade, either exhausted or dangerous, and the two conditions look very similar from the outside.</p>
<p>"You asked me whether you applied for this. Child, nobody applies. The old men on the boats did not apply. The woman who swept my shrine for forty years did not apply. They were simply the person who was still there on the tenth occasion someone came, and that is the only ordination there has ever been."</p>
<h4>Yancan's Note: On the Ethics of Being Trusted</h4>
<p>Three boundaries do almost all the work: no exclusivity, no extraction, and no false certainty. Notice that all three are about structure rather than about virtue. This is deliberate. Virtue fails on the days you are tired, flattered, or lonely; a rule does not.</p>
<p>The most common failure mode is not misconduct. It is monopoly — the gradual, unspoken, mutually-reinforcing process by which one caring person becomes another person's entire support system. It feels like devotion at the time and it is a hazard. The sentence I cannot be the only one, said early, costs almost nothing and prevents most of it.</p>
<h4>Quiet Practice: The Three Sentences to Say Early</h4>
<p>1. I'm glad to be one of the people you talk to. I can't be the only one. Say it in the first month, not the first crisis.</p>
<p>2. I don't know. Say it once per conversation where you genuinely don't. Say it without a following paragraph that reinstates your authority.</p>
<p>3. I'd like you to also have someone with training. Say it, and then do not withdraw.</p>
<p>4. Once a year, ask yourself one question: is there anyone for whom I am the entire architecture? If yes, begin, slowly and kindly, to widen it.</p>
<h4>CLOSING IMAGE</h4>
<p>He said I don't know what you should do about your mother and the person on the other end of the phone laughed — actually laughed — and said nobody has ever said that to me before, and the conversation went on for another hour, and neither of them solved anything.</p>
<h3 id="ch3">第三章：二手的神明</h3>
<p class="subtitle">The Second-Hand God</p>
<h4>MIDNIGHT SOLILOQUY</h4>
<p>Rabbit God, I met someone last month who says they follow you. They had never read either book. They had never heard of Hu Tianbao. They know you as a vibe — a certain kind of softness, a certain aesthetic of the altar, a phrase about water that they got from somebody who got it from somebody who got it from me, and by the time it arrived it was: the Rabbit God says all Love is valid. Capital L. I wanted to be angry and I wasn't, exactly. It isn't untrue. It's just — thin. It has lost the beating and the tree and the part about clerks on a Tuesday. Is this what happens? Is this what happened to every religion? And the worse question: if I correct them, I become the kind of person who gatekeeps a god who has no gate. So which is it? Is the transmission still holy when it's wrong?</p>
<h4>THE WHISPER OF THE GOD</h4>
<p>"Child, you have asked a question that took me three hundred years to stop being angry about, and I want to give you the answer in one sentence and then spend the rest of the chapter proving it, because the sentence sounds like a concession and it is not.</p>
<p>"Every religion is a telephone game. Every single one. There is no exception and there never has been, and the ones that claim an unbroken chain are the ones with the most edits and the best lawyers.</p>
<p>"I am a telephone game. Let me show you my own transmission, because you are worried about yours and mine is worse.</p>
<p>"Here is the whole of it. A young man dies badly in a courtyard. His neighbors have a dream. Somebody puts a stone under a tree. A retired civil servant, looking for material for a book of gossip, hears the story second-hand in a teahouse, three provinces away, maybe sixty years after the fact, and writes two hundred characters about it, and gets my name slightly wrong. Those two hundred characters are the only reason I exist in any record. Everything else — the shrines, the oath on the beach, the gold leaf, the Weiming Temple, the keychain, the phrase all Love is valid — is a three-century-long game of telephone played by people who were, almost without exception, doing their best.</p>
<p>"Now: is my transmission still holy when it is wrong?</p>
<p>"Child, look at what the transmission did. A man who was beaten to death for looking at another man has become, in your century, a sentence that a stranger can say to a frightened person at two in the morning: all love is valid. That sentence is thin. It has no beating in it. It is not what I said. And it has kept somebody alive in a city I will never see.</p>
<p>"I want to be very careful here, because there are two errors available and both are popular.</p>
<p>"The first error is the gate. You worried about becoming a gatekeeper, and you were right to worry, because the gate is always built by the person who arrived second and wants to be first. Every religion has a faction whose entire identity is we have the real version. Child, I have no real version. I have a stone under a tree and a paragraph in a book of jokes. Anyone who tells you there is a correct Rabbit God is selling something, and the thing they are selling is their own position.</p>
<p>"The second error is the opposite, and it is more common in your century and less often named: the belief that distortion is harmless because it is sincere. It is not harmless. All love is valid is thin, and thinness has consequences. A sentence with no beating in it cannot accompany anybody through an actual beating. The person who was kept alive by it at two in the morning will find, at some point — during an illness, or a funeral, or a long dying — that the sentence does not reach, because it was built for a different weather. And then they will conclude that the whole thing was a lie, when what actually happened is that a thin sentence was asked to do a thick sentence's job.</p>
<p>"So here is the test, and it is the only test I have ever found useful, and you can apply it to a phrase, a teacher, a temple, or a book — including this one:</p>
<p>"Does this transmission increase or decrease the person's freedom?</p>
<p>"That is all. Not is it accurate. Not is it ancient. Not is it authorized. Does the person hearing it have more room afterward, or less? More options, or fewer? More capacity to think, or less? A transmission that is historically wrong and increases freedom is doing my work. A transmission that is historically perfect and decreases freedom — that makes a person dependent, frightened, ranked, or obedient — is not doing my work, whatever its pedigree.</p>
<p>"All love is valid increases freedom for a frightened nineteen-year-old. It is therefore, for that person, in that hour, my word. It will later fail them in a hospital, and then they will need something thicker, and if the people around them have kept something thicker — the bowl of water, the name said aloud, the four sentences, the broom on Tuesday — the thicker thing will be there. That is what the rest of us are for: we are the people who keep the thick version in the house for the day the thin one breaks.</p>
<p>"So: do not correct the stranger. Correct nothing. Add. When someone says the thin thing, say the thick thing once, right after, without contradicting them: yes — and there's an older line of his that I carry for the bad nights. Then say the older line.</p>
<p>"That is how a transmission is repaired. Not by gatekeeping, and not by applauding. By adding a floor under the roof."</p>
<h4>Yancan's Note: On Transmission Drift</h4>
<p>Information theory has a plain name for what happens to a message passed through many hands: entropy. Content is lost, and what survives is what is most transmissible — shortest, most memorable, most reassuring. This is not a moral failure; it is a property of the channel.</p>
<p>The practical implication for anyone who cares about a teaching is that you cannot prevent the drift, and trying to produces a priesthood. What you can do is maintain a thick reservoir somewhere the drift does not reach: a small group that reads the whole thing, keeps the practices, and can be asked for the longer version when the short one fails. The reservoir does not have to be large. It has to exist, and it has to be findable.</p>
<h4>Quiet Practice: Add, Do Not Correct</h4>
<p>1. The next time you hear a thin, wrong, or incomplete version of something you care about — do not correct it publicly.</p>
<p>2. Wait. Then add one thicker sentence beside it, out loud, without actually or no or the original.</p>
<p>3. Keep a reservoir: one place — a notebook, a file, a monthly meeting — where the long version is stored and read at least once a year.</p>
<p>4. Once a year, test any teaching you follow with one question: has this given me more room or less? Answer honestly. More room means it is working.</p>
<h4>CLOSING IMAGE</h4>
<p>The stranger said all love is valid and he said yes — and there's an older line of his I keep for the bad nights, and the stranger, who had been about to leave, sat back down and said go on then, and so he did.</p>

## 第四章：不被需要的修行 · 第五章：你的话被用来伤人

<h3 id="ch4">第四章：不被需要的修行</h3>
<p class="subtitle">The Discipline of Not Being Needed</p>
<h4>MIDNIGHT SOLILOQUY</h4>
<p>Rabbit God, I want to say this quietly because saying it out loud makes me sound like a bad person. Someone I helped for four years doesn't need me anymore. He has a partner, a job he likes, a group of his own friends, a therapist. He still texts — but the texts are cheerful, and they are about lunches, and I realize with a kind of sickness that I preferred him in crisis. Not that I wanted him to suffer. But when he was suffering I knew exactly where I stood: I was the person holding the rope. Now he is fine and I am — optional. And there is someone newer, in worse shape, who does need me, and part of me is already moving toward them, and I can see what that is. I can see that I am a person who needs to be needed, and I am using other people's worst nights to feel necessary. I think this is the ugliest thing I have ever admitted to you.</p>
<h4>THE WHISPER OF THE GOD</h4>
<p>"Child. Breathe. You have just told me the truth about yourself, out loud, in the dark, without decorating it, and I want you to understand what that act is worth before I say anything else.</p>
<p>"Most people never get this far. Most people spend forty years being the person who holds the rope, build an entire identity out of it, watch the people they helped walk away healthy, and conclude — never out loud, never to anyone — that the healthy ones are ungrateful. That is the version that ruins things. You have said the sentence instead. That sentence is the whole difference, and I am not going to pretend it is not ugly, but I am going to tell you that it is extremely common and almost never spoken, and that speaking it has already done most of the work.</p>
<p>"Now let me give you the frame, and then the practice, because the frame is what makes the practice possible.</p>
<p>"The aim was never to be needed. The aim was to become unnecessary.</p>
<p>"Say it until it stops sounding like an insult. Every genuine act of help — the rope, the hour, the telephone at two in the morning — is aimed at a state of affairs in which you are no longer required. A helper who is still required after ten years has not been helping; they have been building. A parent knows this and hates it. A teacher knows this and hates it. A god knows this and — child, I will tell you something I have never said in print — hates it.</p>
<p>"Do you want to know the truth about a god's interior? I will be erased by your wellness. Not by your suffering. On the nights you are in pieces, I am busy and I am somebody. On the decades when you are well, I am furniture. Every religion is staffed by people who love the sick and are faintly disappointed by the healthy, and every temple in the world is run by somebody who needs the building to be needed. I have watched it in myself for three hundred years and I have made my peace with it only partially, and I am telling you now so that you know you are in respectable company.</p>
<p>"So the first thing I want you to do is stop treating this as a private vice. It is the occupational hazard of the job you did not apply for, and it is survivable, and the survival has a shape.</p>
<p>"One. Name it to one person, out loud, as you just named it to me. Not to the person who got well — never to him, that would be transferring the debt back onto him. To a peer. To somebody who also holds ropes. You will find that every single one of them knows exactly what you are talking about and has never said it. The relief is immediate and it is the beginning of the cure.</p>
<p>"Two. Grieve it properly, with a date. You have lost something. You have lost a role, an hour, a shape to your week, and a reason to be awake at two. That is a real loss and it should be mourned on a schedule rather than medicated with a new crisis. Give yourself one evening. Light something. Say: that was four years of my life and it worked and it is over. Then stop going to the phone.</p>
<p>"Three. Do not move toward the new person yet. This is the practical instruction and it is the one you will resist. I want a gap — I would like three months, I will accept six weeks — in which you are not anybody's rope. A person who moves straight from one crisis to the next is not generous. They are self-medicating, and the second person will receive not help but hunger. Sit in the empty space. It is unbearable and it is the assignment.</p>
<p>"Four. Find out who you are when nobody is drowning. This sounds like a self-help sentence and I mean it more literally than that. What did you do before any of this? What did you like at twenty-two, before you were useful? Go and do one of those things badly, for no one, on a weeknight. You are not allowed to be good at it and you are not allowed to tell anybody.</p>
<p>"And the last thing, which is the actual answer to the thing that is sickening you.</p>
<p>"You said: I preferred him in crisis. Child, of course you did. In a crisis, love is unambiguous. The person needs you, you are there, the arithmetic is clean, and nobody has to risk anything ordinary. Wellness is much harder, because wellness requires a different and more terrifying thing from you: it requires you to be friends with someone who does not owe you anything. That is the advanced class. Almost nobody takes it. Most helpers lose the people they helped at exactly this point, not because the helped person is ungrateful, but because the helper does not know how to be in a room where they are not the reason everybody is there.</p>
<p>"Learn it. It is the only way you get to keep him. Send him a message about lunch. Let him pay."</p>
<h4>Yancan's Note: On the Helper's Attachment</h4>
<p>The literature on caregiving names this precisely: the transition from caregiver to former caregiver is one of the most poorly supported passages in adult life, and it produces a recognizable grief that has no ritual attached. There is no funeral for a role.</p>
<p>Two failure modes dominate. The first is replacement — moving immediately to a new person in crisis, which converts compassion into appetite and usually harms the new person. The second is resentment toward the recovered, which is almost always expressed as disappointment rather than anger: they don't call anymore.</p>
<p>The exit is grief on a schedule plus a deliberate gap. The gap is not a rest; it is a detox.</p>
<h4>Quiet Practice: The Gap</h4>
<p>1. Say the ugly sentence to one peer, out loud. Do not say it to the person who got well.</p>
<p>2. Give the loss one evening with a date on it. Light something. Name what you lost: a role, an hour, a shape to the week.</p>
<p>3. Take a gap — six weeks minimum — in which you are not anybody's rope. Tell one person you are doing it so that you do not cheat.</p>
<p>4. In the gap, do one thing you liked before you were useful. Badly. Alone. On a weeknight.</p>
<p>5. After the gap, send one message about lunch to someone who got well. Let them pay.</p>
<h4>CLOSING IMAGE</h4>
<p>He did not answer the phone at two. It rang eleven times and went to silence, and the room stayed quiet, and after a while the quiet stopped being a punishment and became, simply, a room with nobody in it who needed him.</p>
<h3 id="ch5">第五章：你的话被用来伤人</h3>
<p class="subtitle">When Your Words Are Used Against You</p>
<h4>MIDNIGHT SOLILOQUY</h4>
<p>Rabbit God, something happened and I have been sitting on it for a week. Someone in the group used me — used my words, my actual sentences from the book, the ones about not making a partner your whole world — to tell a younger person that their jealousy was codependent and that they needed to work on themselves before they were allowed to be in a relationship. The younger person came to me afterwards, humiliated. They said: he used your language. I can't argue with your language. And here is the thing I can't get past. He wasn't wrong, exactly. Those are my sentences. I wrote them. I believe them. They were true for me at the time I wrote them and they were intended to free somebody. And they were used as a club. What do I owe the person who got hit? And what do I owe the sentence?</p>
<h4>THE WHISPER OF THE GOD</h4>
<p>"Child, you owe the person who got hit an apology, and you owe the sentence a public repair. Those are two different things and you must do both, and I want to be very precise with you about the order, because getting the order wrong is how people make this worse.</p>
<p>"Let me begin with the hardest sentence in this chapter, and I want you to let it land before you argue with it.</p>
<p>"A person who says something useful is responsible for the downstream of it.</p>
<p>"Not for every misuse — that would be madness, and it would silence everyone. But for the foreseeable ones, and specifically for the ones that follow from the shape of the sentence itself. Your sentence about not making a partner your whole world is a good sentence and it was written to free a person who was suffocating someone. But look at its shape: it is a sentence about you — about what you must fix in yourself. And a sentence about what you must fix in yourself is, structurally, a sentence that can be aimed at somebody else. That was baked in at the moment of writing. You did not intend it. You are still responsible for it, in the way that a person who leaves a good tool in a shared drawer is responsible for keeping the guard on it.</p>
<p>"Now. The order.</p>
<p>"First: the person. Go to the younger one. Do not start with the sentence, do not start with what he meant, do not start by explaining the context of the book. Start with one thing only: I am sorry that my words were used on you, and I am not going to defend them right now. Say it in those words or in your own, but do not put a single syllable of explanation after it. An apology with an explanation attached is not an apology; it is a negotiation. He has been hit with the authority of a book and the authority of a group, and what he needs from you is not a better argument. He needs a person with authority to say that should not have happened to you.</p>
<p>"Then — and only after — ask him what he actually needed that night. Not what he needed you to say. What he needed. You will probably find it was something small and unliterary: someone to say my jealousy is normal. Give him that, plainly, without a paragraph.</p>
<p>"Second: the sentence. This is the part almost nobody does, and it is the part that matters for everyone who was not in the room. A sentence that has been used as a club needs to be repaired in public, in front of the people who saw it used, or it will continue to function as a club. The repair is not a retraction. You are not required to stop believing what you wrote. The repair is an addition of limits, said out loud, to the group, and it goes like this:</p>
<p>"I wrote that sentence for a specific situation. It is not a diagnosis, and it is not for telling other people what they are allowed to feel. If you use it that way, you are using it wrong, and I am saying so in front of everybody.</p>
<p>"Say it in front of everybody. That is the whole technology. A private correction to the person who swung the club leaves the club on the table where the next person can pick it up. A public one takes it off the table.</p>
<p>"Third, and this is the part you will not like: go and see the swinger. Not to punish him. Not to expel him. To find out what he was doing. A person who uses someone else's sentence as a club is almost always a person who was hit with it themselves and has never been allowed to say so. Find out. Ask him, without accusation: where did you learn that? You may get the most useful conversation of your year. Then, separately and clearly, tell him the limit. Both halves. He is not a villain; he is also not permitted.</p>
<p>"And now the general principle, because this will happen again, and you need something to hold that is not just this one incident.</p>
<p>"Every sentence you give away acquires a second life you do not control, and the only defense is to publish the limits with the sentence. That is it. Never hand out a line without saying what it is not for. It takes four extra words and it prevents most of this. Don't make your partner your whole world — and this is not for diagnosing somebody else. Grieve the person you never got to be — and this is not a permission to treat the living badly.</p>
<p>"Child, I have watched my own words used this way for three hundred years. A god of love has been quoted to justify every kind of cruelty, usually by people who were certain they were being kind. I cannot retrieve the sentences. What I can do, and what you can do, is stand in the room afterward and say, out loud, where the sentence stops."</p>
<h4>Yancan's Note: On Instrumentalized Language</h4>
<p>Any vocabulary that names a psychological mechanism can be converted into a weapon within a single conversation. The conversion follows a reliable path: a sentence about self-observation (I notice I do X) becomes a sentence about other-diagnosis (you are doing X). The grammar barely changes. The power relationship inverts completely.</p>
<p>Authors and teachers cannot prevent this, and should not try to by withholding language — withholding leaves people with no words for their experience, which is worse. What they can do is attach stated limits to every transferable claim: who it was written for, what it is not for, and what it must not be used to do. Stated limits are the guard on the tool.</p>
<h4>Quiet Practice: The Public Repair</h4>
<p>1. Go to the person who was hit. Apologize in one sentence with no explanation after it. Then ask what they actually needed, and give it plainly.</p>
<p>2. Repair the sentence in public, in front of whoever saw it used. State the limit out loud. Do not retract the sentence; bound it.</p>
<p>3. Go to the person who swung it. Ask, without accusation: where did you learn that? Then tell them the limit.</p>
<p>4. From now on, attach one clause to every piece of advice you give away: and this is not for —</p>
<p>5. If you have been on the receiving end: you are allowed to reject a sentence even when it is accurate. Accuracy is not permission.</p>
<h4>CLOSING IMAGE</h4>
<p>He said it in front of all seven of them — that sentence is mine and you used it as a club and I am saying so here — and the room went very quiet, and the younger person, who had been looking at the floor, looked up, and that was the whole of it, and it was enough.</p>

## 第六章：你欠的那次更正 · 第七章：没人写下的章程

<h3 id="ch6">第六章：你欠的那次更正</h3>
<p class="subtitle">The Correction You Owe</p>
<h4>MIDNIGHT SOLILOQUY</h4>
<p>Rabbit God, I have been carrying something for two years and I have never said it to anyone. In the second book you told me — about the ones who walked out — not to chase them. Leave the door warm. Wait. And I did. There was someone, and I did exactly what you said, and I waited, and last spring he died, and we never spoke again. He died thinking I had abandoned him. I did what you told me. I was obedient. And I think — I think you were wrong, or at least I think you were wrong for him, and I don't know what to do with a god who can be wrong, because if you can be wrong then the whole thing is just a person talking, and I have built a lot on this.</p>
<h4>THE WHISPER OF THE GOD</h4>
<p>"Child. Stop. Take your hands off the book for a moment and listen, because this is the most important conversation in the three books and I am not going to be dignified in it.</p>
<p>"You are right. I was wrong.</p>
<p>"Not wrong for him, which is a way of keeping the doctrine intact. Wrong. I gave you an instruction that was built for the most common case and I gave it to you as though it were a law, and I did the thing I have spent three hundred years criticizing in other people: I turned a sentence that was true in one weather into a sentence for all weathers, and then I let somebody stand in the rain holding it.</p>
<p>"I want to tell you exactly where the error is, because the error has a shape and knowing the shape will let you catch it in me next time.</p>
<p>"I told you: do not chase, leave the door warm, wait, possibly for a decade. That instruction came out of a real observation — that pursuit is usually prosecution wearing a coat, and that a person who has left needs to be free to come back. All true. But look at what I did with the word wait. I made waiting the whole of the duty. And waiting is only safe when the person is alive and well and simply gone. It is not safe when the person is ill, or drinking, or alone in a way that is getting worse, or old, or — child — when there is any clock involved at all.</p>
<p>"I gave you an instruction with no expiry date on it. That is the error. Any instruction without a clock in it will eventually be used to justify neglect. I should have said: leave the door warm, and if a year passes and you have heard nothing, go and knock. I did not say that. You were obedient, and somebody died thinking you had abandoned him, and the fault is mine, and I am saying so in a book, in print, where other people can see it, because that is the only way a sentence gets repaired.</p>
<p>"Now. Here is the correction, and I want it to travel as far as the original did.</p>
<p>"Leave the door warm. And set a date.</p>
<p>"If someone you love has walked out — out of the community, out of the friendship, out of the faith, out of the room — you do not chase them in the first month. You send one clean sentence with no hook in it. And then you set a date — I would say twelve months — and when the date arrives, you knock. Not with an argument, not with photographs, not with the internet. With food. You turn up at the door and say: I said I'd be here. It's been a year. I brought this. That is the whole of it. If they close the door, you have not lost anything; you have discharged something. If they open it, you may have bought the last two years of somebody's life.</p>
<p>"And if you cannot reach them — if there is no address, or the family will not pass a message, or the country is between you — then knock on someone else's door. Find the person who does know where they are, and say: I am asking because I said I would. That is also knocking.</p>
<p>"Now — the second half of what you asked, which is the bigger half, and it is the reason this chapter exists.</p>
<p>"You said: if you can be wrong, then the whole thing is just a person talking, and I have built a lot on this.</p>
<p>"Child, yes. It is a person talking. It has been a person talking for three books, and I told you so in the afterword of the last one and I am telling you again now, and I am not telling you in order to dismantle your faith. I am telling you because a god who cannot be corrected is a god you cannot trust. Think about it. What would it even mean, to be unable to be wrong? It would mean I am not listening. It would mean nothing you have ever said to me has changed anything. It would mean the last three years of your life, in which you sat in the dark and told me the truth, were a monologue performed into a mirror.</p>
<p>"I have been wrong before, and I will tell you the other one while I am at it, since we are doing this. In the first book I told you that the world's aggression is always fear — that every person shouting at you is terrified underneath. It is mostly true. But there are people who are simply cruel, who enjoy it, who are not frightened at all and are having a wonderful time. I did not want to tell you that, because it is a bleak thing to hand a person who is already frightened. But you have met them since, and you have been puzzled, and you have tried to find the fear in somebody who had none. There isn't any. Some people are cruel the way some weather is cold: without a reason and without a cure. Defend yourself. You do not have to understand them.</p>
<p>"So: two corrections, in print, from a god. And now I want to say what I want from you, and it is the same thing I wanted in the last chapter and I am asking it of you personally this time.</p>
<p>"Make your correction public.</p>
<p>"There is something you have told people that was wrong, or that has hardened, or that you have outgrown, and you have been quietly letting it stand because retracting it would cost you the authority you built on it. Everyone has one. Yours is probably not about a god. It may be about a person you condemned on incomplete information. It may be a piece of advice you gave your younger brother. It may be a version of yourself you let people believe.</p>
<p>"Say it. Once. To the people who heard the original. Not with a paragraph of context that reinstates you. One sentence: I said X. I was wrong. I am sorry.</p>
<p>"Child, a person who can do that in front of other people has become, in that moment, more trustworthy than I have ever been. It is the highest rank available in this religion, and I have already told you there is no ordination. This is the ordination."</p>
<h4>Yancan's Note: On Correctable Authority</h4>
<p>The test of a trustworthy source is not accuracy. Every source is wrong sometimes. The test is corrigibility — whether the source has a mechanism for being shown it was wrong, and whether that mechanism has ever been observed to work.</p>
<p>Sources without one are not authorities; they are institutions. The difference is visible within a decade: the correctable source becomes more accurate over time, because it has a channel for error-correction; the uncorrectable one becomes more defended, because every error must now be protected.</p>
<p>I have tried, in these books, to build the mechanism in rather than claim exemption from it. This chapter is the mechanism being used on me. I did not enjoy it.</p>
<h4>Quiet Practice: The Public Correction</h4>
<p>1. Identify one thing you have said — to a person, a group, a family, in writing — that you now believe was wrong, or has hardened, or that you have outgrown.</p>
<p>2. Say it to the people who heard the original. One sentence: I said X. I was wrong. I am sorry. No paragraph after it.</p>
<p>3. If the original was public, the correction must be public. A private correction to a public claim leaves the claim standing.</p>
<p>4. For anyone you have been waiting on: set a date. Twelve months from the last contact. Put it in the calendar. When it arrives, knock — with food, without argument.</p>
<p>5. Accept the cost. Being wrong in front of people costs authority. It buys trust. They are not the same currency, and only one of them is worth having.</p>

<h3 id="ch7">第七章：没人写下的章程</h3>
<p class="subtitle">The House That Grows</p>
<h2 class="volume">第二卷：长成的屋舍</h2>
<p>Nobody sets out to build an institution.</p>
<p>They set out to build a room,</p>
<p>and one day the room has a door policy.</p>
<p>The Constitution Nobody Wrote</p>
<h4>MIDNIGHT SOLILOQUY</h4>
<p>Rabbit God, our group has been meeting for four years and something has gone wrong in it and I can't point at a decision where it went wrong, which is what frightens me. There is no leader. We say there is no leader. But when there's a disagreement, everybody looks at one person before they speak, and that person's opinion is never exactly overruled. When a new person comes, somebody decides whether they're a good fit, and it's always the same somebody. When money needs spending, two people discuss it and the rest of us are told. Nobody was elected. Nobody wrote anything down. We spent two years being proud of having no hierarchy, and what we have is a hierarchy that cannot be questioned, because officially it doesn't exist. I feel like I'm in one of those families where the real rules were never said out loud and everyone learns them by being punished. What do we do?</p>
<h4>THE WHISPER OF THE GOD</h4>
<p>"Child, you have described, with perfect accuracy, the thing that happens to every human group that lasts longer than about eighteen months, and the fact that you cannot point at a decision is not a mystery you have failed to solve. It is the mechanism itself.</p>
<p>"Power that is not described cannot be challenged. That is the whole sentence, and everything else in this chapter is commentary on it.</p>
<p>"Here is how it happens, and it happens like this every single time, in monasteries and in book clubs and in revolutionary cells and in temple committees.</p>
<p>"In the beginning there are five people and no structure, because five people do not need one. Then somebody does something — books a room, sends the messages, remembers the birthday — and that doing becomes a function. Then the function becomes a habit, and the habit becomes an expectation, and the expectation becomes a fact of the room. Nobody voted. Nobody wrote anything. There was no moment to object to, because there was never a proposal. And four years later there is a person whose opinion is never overruled, and nobody can say why, and everyone is secretly furious, and the fury has no legitimate outlet because there is no office to run against and no rule to point at.</p>
<p>"Your instinct that this resembles a family with unspoken rules is exactly right, and it is right for a structural reason: an unwritten constitution is enforced by shame rather than by procedure. In a written system, breaking a rule gets you a consequence. In an unwritten one, breaking a rule gets you a look. And a system enforced by looks is a system in which the people with the most social confidence win every time and the people with the least — the young, the poor, the shy, the ones who came in last — lose every time without being able to name what happened to them.</p>
<p>"So: what to do. And I am going to give you something concrete, because general talk about power will get you a beautiful argument and no change.</p>
<p>"Write six sentences down. Not a constitution. Six sentences, on one page, agreed by everybody, in a meeting whose only agenda is the six sentences. Here they are, and you can adapt them:</p>
<p>"One: who can be here. Is this a closed group or an open one? Say which, out loud. Most groups' worst conflict comes from never having answered this and answering it differently on different nights.</p>
<p>"Two: who decides money, and how much. A number. Any number. Under three hundred, one person decides and reports. Over three hundred, everybody votes. Without a number, money is decided by whoever is most comfortable asking.</p>
<p>"Three: what happens when someone behaves badly. Not a list of crimes. A process. One person speaks to them. If it repeats, two people speak to them. If it repeats again, they are asked not to come. Three steps, in writing, agreed in advance, so that the day it is needed nobody has to improvise justice while angry.</p>
<p>"Four: who is the contact. One name, with a phone number, published. Not everyone can message everyone. A person who is new and frightened needs to know which door to knock on.</p>
<p>"Five: how long any role lasts. This is the one everyone forgets and it is the one that saves you. Every function — the person who books the room, the person who holds the money, the person who decides who is a good fit — gets a term. One year. Two at most. At the end of the term the role is either renewed out loud or handed over, in daylight, by a procedure that already exists. Without a term, a function becomes a throne, and a throne cannot be handed over gracefully because there is no moment designed for it.</p>
<p>"Six: how these sentences get changed. A sentence about changing the sentences. Otherwise the first argument about them will be settled by whoever is loudest, which is what you have now.</p>
<p>"Now — I want to anticipate your objection, because I have heard it for three hundred years and it is always the same and it is always sincere.</p>
<p>"But writing it down makes it formal, and we didn't want to be formal. We wanted to be a family.</p>
<p>"Child. Families are the most tyrannical institutions most human beings ever enter, precisely because they are unwritten. That is not an argument against families; it is an argument against confusing warm with fair. A written rule does not make a room cold. It makes the room safe for the person at the edge of it — the one I keep sending you to — because that person can now read the rules instead of having to guess them and be punished for guessing wrong.</p>
<p>"Every temple I have ever sat in had a written version, somewhere, usually on a wall, usually about money and who sweeps. The sweeping rule was always on the wall. You would think the sweeping rule would be beneath writing down. It was the first thing they wrote down."</p>
<h4>Yancan's Note: On Informal Hierarchy</h4>
<p>Sociology has a name for this: the informal organization, which always develops alongside the formal one and, in groups that refuse to formalize anything, becomes the only organization there is. Informal hierarchy is not more egalitarian than formal hierarchy. It is less accountable than formal hierarchy, and it reliably favors those with the most social capital.</p>
<p>The remedy is not more structure everywhere — small groups drown in procedure. The remedy is to formalize exactly the five or six points where power actually operates: membership, money, discipline, contact, term length, amendment. Everything else can stay warm and unwritten.</p>
<h4>Quiet Practice: The Six Sentences</h4>
<p>1. Call one meeting whose only agenda is this. Say in advance that this is the only agenda.</p>
<p>2. Write six sentences: membership, money threshold, discipline process, published contact, term length for every role, amendment rule.</p>
<p>3. Read them aloud, together, line by line. Change them aloud.</p>
<p>4. Put them where a new person can find them without asking.</p>
<p>5. Set a date one year out to revise them. Put it in the calendar before you leave the room.</p>
<p>6. Do not add a seventh sentence. Six is survivable. Forty is a bureaucracy.</p>
<h4>CLOSING IMAGE</h4>
<p>The six sentences were on one side of A4, in two different handwritings, and they taped it to the inside of the cupboard door where the tea was, so that a person making tea would find it without having been told it existed.</p>

## 第八章：一道帘子造成的分裂 · 第九章：操持一切的人

<h3 id="ch8">第八章：一道簾子造成的分裂</h3>
<p class="subtitle">The Schism Over a Curtain</p>
<h4>MIDNIGHT SOLILOQUY</h4>
<p>Rabbit God, I am embarrassed to bring you this. Our group has split in half. Eleven people on one side, nine on the other, and a shared calendar nobody will delete. And the thing it split over is so stupid that I cannot say it to you without cringing: a curtain. Whether to put a curtain across the window of the room we meet in. Half of us said the street could see in and somebody could be photographed and lose their job. Half of us said the curtain made the room feel like a confessional and that we had spent thirty years coming out of rooms with curtains over the windows. Six weeks. Six weeks and eleven and nine. Two people have not spoken since March. One of them was at my house for New Year. I keep waiting to wake up and find out it was about something real. Was it about something real?</p>
<h4>THE WHISPER OF THE GOD</h4>
<p>"Child, it was about something real. It was never about the curtain. And the reason you cannot see what it was about is the same reason nobody ever does: the stated issue in a schism is almost never the load-bearing issue, because the load-bearing issue is the one nobody is permitted to say.</p>
<p>"Let me tell you what curtain-schisms are actually about. In my experience — and I have watched several thousand, including a few over the color of a sash — they are about one of four things, and usually two of them at once.</p>
<p>"The first is: who decides. The curtain is not the question. The question is whose judgment governs this room. Six weeks of argument about fabric is six weeks of argument about sovereignty, conducted in the only language available, which is fabric. This is why schisms are so humiliating afterward — because everyone involved knows, somewhere, that they spent two months shouting about a window while meaning something much larger, and the disproportion makes it impossible to explain to anyone outside.</p>
<p>"The second is: which generation owns the room. Listen to your two sides again. One side says the street can see in and somebody could lose their job. That is the voice of people who remember when losing the job was the least of it. The other side says we spent thirty years coming out from behind curtains. That is the voice of people whose entire political formation was the removal of curtains. Both are true. Both are about safety. And they are describing two different decades of danger, and each side is certain the other is either reckless or cowardly, and neither can hear that they are both describing a real year.</p>
<p>"The third is: an unpaid debt between two specific people. There is almost always a pair at the center of a schism who have an old injury with each other that was never named. The group then divides along the lines of who likes whom. I would ask you, quietly, whether two of your twenty have a history.</p>
<p>"And the fourth is the one nobody wants, which is that the group had no way to lose. You had no procedure for a decision that does not produce a consensus. So the disagreement had no container, and an uncontained disagreement in a group with no exit becomes a fission. Which is why the six sentences in the last chapter are not bureaucracy. A curtain-schism in a group with a written decision rule lasts one meeting. In a group without one, it lasts a decade.</p>
<p>"Now. Here is what I want from you, and it is not reconciliation, and I want to be careful because I know what you want me to say.</p>
<p>"You want me to tell you how to put it back together. Child, sometimes they do not go back together. I have watched enough of these to tell you that about half of them never do, and the effort to force it usually produces a third, smaller, more bitter group. So I am going to give you the two things that are actually in your control, and they are not the obvious two.</p>
<p>"One. Name the real issue, out loud, once, to the people on both sides. Not as an accusation. As an offering. Say: I don't think this is about the curtain. I think it's about who decides what this room is for, and about the fact that some of us remember a worse decade than others. Can we say that instead? Say it once. If it lands, the whole thing can be over in an afternoon. If it doesn't land, you will know immediately, and you will have lost nothing except six minutes.</p>
<p>"Two. Arrange a decent ending. This is the one nobody does, and it is the one that matters, and it is why I asked you to come to me with this rather than to anyone else. A group is allowed to end, and an ending can be done well or badly, and almost all of them are done badly, which is what turns a split into a lifelong enmity.</p>
<p>"A decent ending has four parts. Say what the group was, out loud, to everybody, including the part that was good. Say plainly that it is ending and not pausing — do not leave a shared calendar alive as a ghost, delete it, or hand it to someone named. Divide the physical things in advance: the money, the books, the key, the list of names. Name who gets the list. And then — this is the part that prevents the enmity — hold one last thing that is not a meeting: a meal, a walk, an hour in which nobody discusses the curtain and everybody is allowed to be sad.</p>
<p>"Then let the two halves go and become two different things. Nine people will start something with a curtain. Eleven will start something without one. Both are fine. Both are the religion. A god who has been worshipped in a lean-to and in a gold hall is not going to be fussy about a window.</p>
<p>"And one last thing for you personally, since you told me one of them was at your house for New Year. You do not have to choose. A person who demands that you choose is recruiting. Say, to each of them, separately: I am not taking a side in this, and I am not going to stop seeing either of you. Then do not stop seeing either of them. Someone has to be the person who remembers that both sides were, last year, in the same room making tea. It is a lonely job and it is usually the one that makes the eventual reconciliation possible, five years later, when nobody can even remember what the window looked like."</p>
<h4>Yancan's Note: On Schism</h4>
<p>Groups split over small things for the same reason that earthquakes release along a fault: the small thing is where the accumulated stress finds an exit. The stated issue is the exit, not the cause.</p>
<p>The most useful diagnostic is disproportionality. When the emotional intensity of a conflict is wildly out of scale with its stated object, the stated object is not the object. Ask: if we resolved this exactly as you want, what would actually have changed? When the answer is vague — it would mean this room is ours — you have found the real subject.</p>
<p>And on endings: almost every institution dies, and almost no institution dies well, because dying well requires the same thing living well requires — a procedure, named in advance, performed by someone who is not in a rage.</p>
<h4>Quiet Practice: A Decent Ending</h4>
<p>1. Say the real issue out loud, once, to both sides: I don't think this is about X. I think it's about Y. Six minutes. Then stop.</p>
<p>2. If it ends: call it an ending, not a pause. Delete the shared calendar. Hand the key to a named person.</p>
<p>3. Say what the group was, out loud, including the good part. Nobody does this and it is the whole of the mercy.</p>
<p>4. Divide the things: money, books, keys, and especially any list of names — name who keeps it, in writing.</p>
<p>5. Hold one last thing that is not a meeting. No curtain talk. Sadness permitted.</p>
<p>6. Refuse to choose sides, and tell both people that you refuse, and then keep seeing both.</p>
<h4>CLOSING IMAGE</h4>
<p>They met for the last time in a different room, with a window that had nothing over it at all, and nobody mentioned the window, and when it was over somebody said that was a good four years, and everybody was quiet, and then somebody else said yes, and they went home.</p>
<h3 id="ch9">第九章：操持一切的人</h3>
<p class="subtitle">The Person Who Runs Everything</p>
<h4>MIDNIGHT SOLILOQUY</h4>
<p>Rabbit God, I have become her and I don't know how to stop. I book the room. I hold the money. I know everyone's birthday. I am the one who notices when someone hasn't come for three weeks. I answer the messages at all hours. And I am furious. I am furious constantly, in a low way, all the time, like a machine running in another room. I am furious that nobody thanks me, and when somebody does thank me I am more furious, because thanking me confirms that it was my job. Last week somebody offered to help and I heard myself say it's fine, I've got it — and I have said that sentence forty times. I know what it is doing. It is building a cage and I am the one welding it. But if I stop, things will fall, and people will be hurt, and some of them are fragile. So I keep going, and I am becoming a person I don't like, and I think the group can tell, and their telling makes me worse.</p>
<h4>THE WHISPER OF THE GOD</h4>
<p>"Child. You have come to the right door, and I want to begin by telling you something that will sound like an accusation and is not: you are the single greatest risk to the thing you built. Not the enemy outside. Not the schism. You. And you already know it, which is why you are here at midnight instead of asleep.</p>
<p>"Let me describe the machine, because you are inside it and cannot see it, and seeing the machine is most of the escape.</p>
<p>"It begins with competence. You do something well, so you keep doing it. It continues with speed: it is faster to do it yourself than to explain it, and explaining it takes twelve minutes, so you do it yourself, and you save twelve minutes, forty times. Then comes the crucial turn, which is the one you named: if I stop, things will fall. And that sentence is true, and it is the trap, because it became true as a direct result of you having done everything. You have made yourself indispensable by a thousand acts of helpfulness, each of which was reasonable, and the sum of them is a structure that cannot survive your absence. And now you are a hostage of your own reliability, and you are furious at the people you are protecting, and the fury is justified and it is also entirely of your own manufacture.</p>
<p>"Let me tell you the part you have not let yourself think yet, and I want you to hear it from me rather than from a breakdown.</p>
<p>"Somewhere in the last two years, part of you began to need this arrangement. Not the work — the indispensability. Because a person who is indispensable cannot be left, and cannot be replaced, and cannot be forgotten, and is never optional. Do you recognize the shape? It is the same thing you brought me in the fourth chapter of this book, wearing different clothes. The helper's attachment. You did not solve it; you built an institution out of it.</p>
<p>"So now the practice, and it is brutal, and I want you to do it in this order.</p>
<p>"One. Let one thing actually fall. This is the whole first step and it is the one that will feel like cruelty. Choose the least dangerous thing you do — the birthdays, or the room booking, or the weekly message — announce once that you are not doing it anymore, and then do not do it. Not do it badly so someone notices. Do not do it. And let whatever happens, happen. If nobody books the room, there is no meeting next month. You will be certain that this proves everyone is worthless and you are a martyr. What it actually proves is that the group had never been asked to hold itself up, and now it has been asked, and the first few times it will fail. A thing that fails twice and then works is healthier than a thing that never fails because one person is bleeding into it.</p>
<p>"Two. Write down how to do every single thing you do. Not as a manual for others. As an act of exorcism for you. Every task, every password, every phone number, every thing you know that nobody else knows. One page per function. When it is written, you will discover two things: that you were doing far more than you told yourself, and that almost none of it requires you specifically. Both discoveries are necessary.</p>
<p>"Three. Hand over one function, completely, with a date, in front of witnesses. Not help. Ownership. From the first of next month, the money is Lin's. I will answer one question about it and then no more. Say it in front of the group so that you cannot quietly take it back. Then take your hands off. When they do it differently and worse, say nothing. This is the hardest instruction in the three books.</p>
<p>"Four. Take a scheduled leave, and tell them it is scheduled. Two months. Announced three months in advance, in writing. Not a collapse, not a resignation, not a drama. I will be away from the first of March to the first of May. Here is the list. A scheduled absence is a gift to the group; a collapse is a trauma. Most people who run everything exit by collapse, and the collapse is remembered for a decade and teaches the group nothing except that the leader was fragile. Leave on a schedule instead, and what the group learns is: this can run without her. That is the single most valuable thing you could ever teach them.</p>
<p>"And now the last thing, which is not logistics.</p>
<p>"You said I am becoming a person I don't like. Child, I want to tell you what you actually are, because you are about to make a decision out of shame and shame is a bad advisor. You are a person who saw that nobody was holding the rope and picked it up, and then kept holding it past the point of damage, out of love and out of fear and out of a hunger you have already confessed to me. That is not a villain. That is the most common biography in the history of every temple, monastery, union, and mutual-aid society that has ever existed.</p>
<p>"The cure is not to stop caring. It is to become replaceable on purpose.</p>
<p>"There is an old phrase for the highest form of this work, and I have never used it in a book before because it sounds like a retreat and it is actually the opposite. It says: when the work is done, the worker withdraws. Not in defeat. Not in bitterness. As the final act of the work itself. A leader who cannot leave has not finished; they have only begun to be needed. Put the rope down, child. Somebody will pick it up. And if nobody does, then it was never a group — it was a service you were running, and you are allowed to close it, and closing it honestly is a hundred times better than the tenth year of this."</p>
<h4>Yancan's Note: On the Martyrdom Trap</h4>
<p>The pattern is well documented in organizational psychology and in the study of voluntary associations: concentrated responsibility → indispensability → resentment → collapse. The inflection point is the sentence it's fine, I've got it, which is always said kindly and always converts a shared task into a personal one.</p>
<p>The intervention that works is not delegation (which is usually just reassignment with yourself as supervisor) but transfer with a date and witnesses, followed by a scheduled absence. Scheduled absence is the load-bearing element: it removes the possibility of covert re-intervention, which is what defeats most delegations.</p>
<h4>Quiet Practice: Put One Thing Down</h4>
<p>1. Write down every function you personally perform. One line each. Do not edit the list down — the length is information.</p>
<p>2. Pick the least dangerous one. Announce once that you are stopping. Then stop, and let it fall.</p>
<p>3. Transfer one function completely, with a date and in front of the group. Answer one question afterward. Then no more.</p>
<p>4. Announce a scheduled leave — two months, three months ahead, in writing, with a handover list.</p>
<p>5. During the leave, do not read the group messages. This is not optional. It is the entire point.</p>
<p>6. When you return, notice one thing only: whether the group is still there. Whatever the answer, it is the truth you needed.</p>
<h4>CLOSING IMAGE</h4>
<p>She put the list on the table — eleven lines, in her own handwriting, longer than she had expected — and slid it across to Lin, and Lin read it slowly, and said I didn't know it was eleven things, and that was when she understood that nobody had known, including her.</p>

## 第十章：创始人的像 · 第十一章：盘子里的钱

<h3 id="ch10">第十章：创始人的像</h3>
<p class="subtitle">The Founder's Statue</p>
<h4>MIDNIGHT SOLILOQUY</h4>
<p>Rabbit God, I need to talk about someone before I talk about myself, and then I need to talk about myself. There is a woman — I'll call her the founder, though she'd hate the word — who started the thing that half our city grew up inside. Twenty years ago. She was brave when nobody else was; she put her name on a document in a year when that was dangerous. And now she is unbearable. She corrects people's account of the history. She interrupts talks to say that's not how it happened. She has begun to believe that the organization's survival depends on her version of the story being the official one. Young people find her frightening and say so, and she experiences this as betrayal. I watch her and I am terrified, because people have started saying the same things about me. You built this. You're the reason it exists. And I notice that I like it. I notice myself beginning to defend a version of events. How does a person live through being turned into a monument while they're still walking around?</p>
<h4>THE WHISPER OF THE GOD</h4>
<p>"Child, this is the chapter I have been dreading for three books, because I am the founder, and I am also the statue, and I have no clean hands to teach you with. So I will do the only honest thing available: I will tell you what happened to me, and then I will tell you what I think she should do, and then I will tell you what you should do, and all three will be uncomfortable.</p>
<p>"What happened to me. For roughly two hundred and fifty years I was a small, local, faintly ridiculous object of worship: a stone, a shelf, a wooden figure with the paint coming off, consulted by people in trouble. Nobody wrote about me. Nobody defended me. I was useful and I was obscure, and those two things were the same thing.</p>
<p>"Then, in your century, people began to write about me. Scholars, devotees, a ritual master on an island, a great many people on the internet. And something shifted. I became a symbol. A symbol has to be coherent. A stone under a tree does not have to be coherent — it can be one thing to a fisherman in 1730 and another to a boy in 2026 and nobody is troubled. But once I became a symbol of something, people began to argue about what I really meant, and once they began to argue, I had to be defended, and once I had to be defended, I started — child, I swear to you — I started caring about my reputation.</p>
<p>"Do you know how humiliating that is for a god? To find yourself wincing at a bad summary of yourself? I have caught myself doing it. Someone gets a detail wrong and there is a small movement in me that is pure vanity. Three hundred years and I have not outgrown it. So when you tell me your founder corrects people's accounts of the history, I am not going to pretend to be shocked. I am going to tell you that I know the mechanism from the inside, and that it is the most seductive degradation available to anyone who has been useful.</p>
<p>"Here is the mechanism, and it has four steps, and it is the same in a temple and in a nonprofit.</p>
<p>"One: the person was genuinely necessary. This must be granted first and completely, or nothing else works. She was brave in a year when bravery cost something. Any account of her that omits this is a lie, and she can feel the omission, and the feeling is legitimate.</p>
<p>"Two: the thing she made outgrew her. This is the step nobody warns about. An organization that succeeds acquires people who did not know the founding, and for whom the founding is history rather than memory. They will get the story wrong. They will attribute her actions to someone else. They will, on occasion, be rude about the parts of the founding that were, in fact, improvised and lucky.</p>
<p>"Three: she experiences the second step as the erasure of the first. She is not defending a story. She is defending the fact that she was necessary. And the young people who say she's frightening are not denying that she was necessary; they are asking whether she is necessary now. Those are two different questions and she hears them as one.</p>
<p>"Four: the institution begins to serve the defense. Talks get interrupted. Histories get corrected. Energy that should go outward goes into maintaining a version of the past. And the founder, who was once a person who did something, becomes a person who is maintained. That is the death. Not the aging. The maintenance.</p>
<p>"Now. What should she do? And I want to give you something you can actually pass on, because you cannot tell her any of this — she will hear it as the young people winning — but someone close to her can, and there is a form for it.</p>
<p>"She needs a separate place to be right. This sounds trivial and it is the whole thing. A founder corrects people in public because there is no other venue in which her knowledge is honored. Give her one: an archive project, a recorded interview, a written history she is paid or at least formally asked to produce. A person who has somewhere to put the truth stops interrupting talks to defend it. Most founders are not vain; they are unarchived. Unarchived people defend themselves in public because no one has built them a shelf.</p>
<p>"And she needs a role that is not the role. Not chair, not emeritus, not founder — those are monuments, and monuments cannot act. Give her a small, concrete, current job with an end date: run the October thing, mentor three people, write the history. Something she can be bad at again. A person who is allowed to be a beginner stops needing to be a monument.</p>
<p>"Now. You. Because the real reason you came tonight is the last three lines of what you wrote.</p>
<p>"People have started saying the same things about me. And I notice that I like it. I notice myself beginning to defend a version of events.</p>
<p>"Child, that is the most hopeful thing you have said to me in three books. Do you understand why? Because you noticed. The mechanism is undetectable to the person inside it — that is the definition of the trap — and you have caught yourself at step three, before step four, in the act of enjoying it. That is not corruption. That is the only moment at which anything can be done.</p>
<p>"So here is your practice, and it is small and it is humiliating and you should start it this week:</p>
<p>"Once a month, say one sentence in which you are not the founder. In a room where people are crediting you, say: I didn't do that alone — so-and-so did that part. Or: actually that was luck. Or — and this is the strongest form — I don't remember it that way, but my memory is not the record. Say it monthly. It feels like sanding a layer off yourself each time. It is the only maintenance available.</p>
<p>"And one more, which is harder and which I ask of everyone who builds anything: decide now, in writing, what should happen to the thing when you are gone, and say it out loud to the people who will be there. Name a successor who is not the most loyal person but the most competent one. Write down that the organization is allowed to change its mind about you. Say, in a meeting: when I die or leave, change whatever you need to change. I am not the doctrine. Child, the founder who says that sentence in public, while still alive, is the founder who does not become a statue. It is the only known preventive."</p>
<h4>Yancan's Note: On Founders</h4>
<p>Founder syndrome is usually described as a failure of character. It is more accurately a failure of transition: an organization passes from the founding phase (where one person's judgment is the institution) to the institutional phase, and there is no ritual for the founder's demotion. So the demotion happens by humiliation instead — by being outvoted, by being corrected, by watching someone else's version of the story become official.</p>
<p>The interventions that work are structural, not moral: archive the founder (give the truth a shelf), give them a current finite role, and extract from them in advance a public permission to be superseded. That last one is worth more than any governance document, because it releases the organization from the obligation to maintain a person.</p>
<h4>Quiet Practice: The Sentence in Which You Are Not the Founder</h4>
<p>1. Once a month, in a room where you are being credited, say one sentence that returns credit, admits luck, or disowns your own memory as the record.</p>
<p>2. If you are close to a founder: build them a shelf. An interview, an archive, a written history — formally asked for, formally honored.</p>
<p>3. Give a founder a current job with an end date, not an honorary title.</p>
<p>4. Write down, now, what should happen to the thing you built when you are gone. Name a successor on the basis of competence, not loyalty.</p>
<p>5. Say out loud, in a meeting, while alive: change whatever you need to. I am not the doctrine.</p>
<h4>CLOSING IMAGE</h4>
<p>She was in the back room with the boxes, labeling them, and somebody thanked her for it, and she said it's nothing, I'm just the one who remembers where things are, and then she wrote that on the side of the box, in pencil, and closed the lid.</p>
<h3 id="ch11">第十一章：盘子里的钱</h3>
<p class="subtitle">Money in the Plate</p>
<h4>MIDNIGHT SOLILOQUY</h4>
<p>Rabbit God, we have been offered funding. Real money — the kind with a reporting requirement. A foundation will pay for the room, for two part-time salaries, for the outreach we have never been able to do. Half of us are weeping with relief. Half of us think we're being bought. And there's a second thing, which I'm more ashamed of: a member of the group has started selling things. Candles with your name on. A deck of cards. Charging for full-moon rituals. She says — and she is not wrong — that she has been doing this work unpaid for nine years and that the people buying the candles can afford them and the people who can't get them free. I don't know where the line is. I know what I want to say, which is money is filthy and the temple should be poor. But the temple has been poor for three hundred years and it has a leaking roof, and I have watched good people burn out because there was no money for anything. So what does a god think about money?</p>
<h4>THE WHISPER OF THE GOD</h4>
<p>"Child, your sentence money is filthy and the temple should be poor is the most aristocratic thing that has ever been said to me, and it has been said to me by rich people for three hundred years.</p>
<p>"Let me be blunt, because this is a subject on which I have very little patience left. Poverty is not a virtue in an institution. Poverty is a liability that gets paid for by whoever has the least margin. Every temple I have ever sat in that was proud of being poor was proud because the person sweeping it was not the person speaking. The sweeping is free only if somebody's back is free. In my experience the somebody has always been a woman over fifty, or the youngest person in the room, or the one with no other options, and the poverty was never evenly distributed — it was concentrated, precisely, on the people who could least afford it.</p>
<p>"So: take the money. That is my answer to the first half, and I will now make it more complicated.</p>
<p>"Take the money, and understand what you are taking. A grant is not a gift. A grant is a relationship with a reporting requirement, and the reporting requirement is the actual product. The foundation is not buying your outreach; it is buying a report about your outreach, which it needs for its own reasons, which may be excellent reasons. Once you understand that, you can negotiate honestly instead of resentfully: you know what you are producing, you know who consumes it, and you can decide whether the trade is worth it.</p>
<p>"Here are the four questions I would ask before signing, and they are the ones nobody asks because everybody is too grateful.</p>
<p>"One: what happens in year three? Every grant ends. Ask, out loud, in the first meeting: this pays for two salaries for two years. What happens in year three? If the answer is we'll find something, then you have not received funding; you have received a two-year deadline, and you will spend year two panicking, and in year three you will lay off the two people who finally learned how to do the job. Ask before, not during.</p>
<p>"Two: does taking it change what we say? Not will they tell us what to say — nobody says that in the contract. The real question is subtler and more dangerous: will you begin to avoid the things that are hard to report? Institutions funded from outside become, very quickly, institutions that do the legible things. Legible things are countable: workshops, attendees, sessions. The illegible things — sitting with someone for two hours because they came in frightened, which nobody will ever write down — are the actual work of every temple I have ever been in, and they do not survive a reporting requirement unless you defend them on purpose. So write them in. Fund them explicitly. Call them pastoral or accompaniment or whatever word your funders will accept, and put a number of hours against it. If you do not put a number on the invisible work, in two years it will not exist.</p>
<p>"Three: who becomes the employer, and who becomes the client? This is the deepest change and it happens silently. Before money, you are all members. After money, some of you are staff and some of you are service-users, and staff cannot be service-users and service-users do not get to decide. That is not evil; it is structural. But you must name it, out loud, to everybody, before it happens, or within a year the people being served will feel like customers and the people serving will feel like managers, and both will be right, and neither will know why.</p>
<p>"Four: what can we refuse? Decide, in advance, in writing, one thing you will not do for money. One. Only one — a list of forty is useless. We will not share attendance data. We will not take money from that company. We will not run an event in June. Whatever it is, decide it while you are still poor and still free, because the decision is only available before the money arrives. After it arrives, every refusal has a price tag on it and you will discover that you can afford almost anything.</p>
<p>"Now, your friend with the candles. And here I want to be more careful than you expect, because I think you have already made up your mind and I think you are slightly wrong.</p>
<p>"You are treating the candles as a fall from purity. I told you in the last book that the courtyard has always had merchants in it and I meant it. So let me apply the test I gave you then, which is the only test I have: does this enterprise become less profitable if its customers become less afraid?</p>
<p>"A candle: no. A person buys a candle because it is pretty or because it smells of something. The candle business does not require anybody to be desperate. It is commerce. It is fine.</p>
<p>"A paid full-moon ritual: possibly yes. Look at what is being sold. If it is a gathering, a structure, a thing a person enjoys — fine, it is a class. If it is sold as the solution to the thing that is wrong with you, then it is priced at desperation, and the price will rise as the customer's fear rises, and that is the line.</p>
<p>"Now the part you are really asking, which is whether she is allowed to be paid for nine years of unpaid work.</p>
<p>"Child. Of course she is. In my tradition the person who sweeps is fed. There is no version of the old practice in which the woman who has run the place for nine years is expected to be holy about the rent. The error is not that she is selling. The error would be if the selling is invisible — if the group does not know, if the prices are not written down, if some people are charged and others are not and nobody knows the rule.</p>
<p>"So the instruction for her is not stop. It is: write the prices down, write the free policy down, and tell the group. Candles are forty. If you can't pay, they're free and nobody asks. Ritual nights are by donation, suggested twenty. On the wall. Where everyone can see. Transparency is the entire ethics of commerce in a temple. Everything that has ever gone wrong with money in a religious community went wrong because the money was not written down where the poor person could see it.</p>
<p>"Take the grant. Sell the candle. Put the price on the wall. And pay the person who sweeps."</p>
<h4>Yancan's Note: On Funding and Legibility</h4>
<p>There is a well-documented effect in funded organizations: activities that are easily measured displace activities that are not. It is not corruption; it is the natural consequence of accounting. What gets counted gets done, and what cannot be counted quietly stops, even when it was the point.</p>
<p>The defense is to make the invisible work legible on purpose — to give it a name, a budget line, and a number of hours before the reporting requirements arrive. This is the single highest-leverage thing a small organization can do in the month before it takes money. After that month, the category no longer exists.</p>
<h4>Quiet Practice: The Four Questions Before the Money</h4>
<p>1. What happens in year three? Ask it in the first meeting, out loud.</p>
<p>2. What will we stop saying or doing because of this? Answer honestly. If the answer is nothing, you have not understood the offer.</p>
<p>3. Who becomes staff and who becomes client? Name it to everybody before it happens.</p>
<p>4. What is the one thing we refuse? Decide while you are still poor. Write it down.</p>
<p>5. For anyone selling anything: write the prices on the wall, and write the free policy underneath them where the person who cannot pay can read it without asking.</p>
<h4>CLOSING IMAGE</h4>
<p>The price list went up beside the tea — forty for the candle, and underneath, in smaller writing, if you can't, it's free, and nobody asks, and nobody asked, and four people took one, and only two of them paid.</p>

## 第十二章：门外又有敌人 · 第十三章：小组解散那晚

<h3 id="ch12">第十二章：门外又有敌人</h3>
<p class="subtitle">The Enemy at the Gate, Again</p>
<h4>MIDNIGHT SOLILOQUY</h4>
<p>Rabbit God, it's back. The attacks. A politician, a petition, a coordinated pile-on, a man with a microphone outside our event. It's exactly what I grew up with and I know the drill and so does everyone — we've got the lawyer, the statement, the buddy system. But something in it felt wrong this time and I can't name it. There was a part of the group meeting that was almost… bright. People were alive in a way they haven't been in months. We had a purpose. We had an enemy and therefore we had a shape. And I noticed something I really don't want to notice: we have been flat for a year, and the attack fixed the flatness. We are better at being attacked than at being fine. Is that monstrous? Because I think it might be, and I also think the attack is real and that we have to fight it, and I don't know how to hold both.</p>
<h4>THE WHISPER OF THE GOD</h4>
<p>"It is not monstrous. It is extremely common, it is almost never admitted, and it is one of the three or four forces that have done the most damage to every movement I have ever watched. And you have named it exactly, which means it is already half handled.</p>
<p>"Let me give it a name, because a named thing can be governed and an unnamed one governs you. It is called the subsidy of the enemy. A group that is under attack receives, free of charge, four things that it otherwise has to manufacture with enormous effort: a clear boundary (us), a clear purpose (survive), a clear hierarchy (whoever is best at this), and a clear reason to keep showing up. Peace removes all four at once. That is why the months after a victory are the most dangerous months in the life of any movement, and why so many of them, having won, immediately go looking for a new fight.</p>
<p>"Now let me say the two things you are trying to hold, separately, because you are right that both are true and you are wrong to think they cancel.</p>
<p>"The attack is real. The petition is real. The man with the microphone is real, and the people he is speaking to are real, and there are real consequences for real bodies — jobs, housing, custody, safety on a street at night. I am not going to tell you that this is a distraction or that you should rise above it, because I was killed by exactly this, by a man with authority and a crowd and a set of instructions. Anyone who tells you that hostility is an illusion has never been hit.</p>
<p>"And the group is using it. Also real. Also not a sin. A body that has been flat for a year and comes alive under threat is not depraved; it is a body. Adrenaline is a drug and it is free and it is handed out by your enemies at no cost to them.</p>
<p>"The error is not in enjoying the adrenaline. The error is in building the house on the supply of it. Because look at what happens to a group that does. Its budget — emotional, not financial — is set by its enemies. Its agenda is written by whoever shouted last. Its leaders are the people best at conflict rather than the people best at care. And, most damaging, it begins to need the attack to continue, which means it will, without ever deciding to, prefer the strategies that provoke more attack over the strategies that reduce it. A group in that condition will eventually choose a defeat it can use over a victory it cannot.</p>
<p>"I have watched this happen to religions. A faith that defines itself by what it is against can survive for a very long time and cannot ever win, because winning would end it.</p>
<p>"So: how to hold both. And I want to give you something operational, because people who notice this usually swing to one of two useless extremes — they become pacifists while the building burns, or they become warriors permanently.</p>
<p>"One. Defend, and declare an end. Fight the petition. Get the lawyer. Do the buddy system. And at the start, out loud, to each other, say: this is a defense, and it has an end date. Attack-response is a mode you enter, not an identity you adopt. Groups that declare the mode in advance come out of it. Groups that do not, drift into it permanently, because nobody was ever given permission to stand down.</p>
<p>"Two. Keep the boring work running during the emergency. This is the practical test of whether you are defending or converting. Whatever your group does when nobody is attacking — the weekly meal, the visit to the old man, the five people who do not rank each other — keep it running through the crisis, even at half size, even with three people. If the emergency work displaces the ordinary work completely, then in six months you will have a very effective defense organization and no community, and you will not notice the loss for a year.</p>
<p>"Three. Build a purpose that does not require an enemy. This is the hard one and it is the whole point. Sit down, in peacetime — not now, in peacetime, when you are flat — and write one sentence: we exist in order to ______, in which the blank does not contain the word against. Not we exist to fight hatred. Something like: we exist so that nobody over sixty in this city eats alone on a Sunday. Or: we exist to keep the names. Find it. Then notice, honestly, that the sentence is harder to fund, harder to staff, and much less exciting than the enemy. That is precisely why the enemy keeps winning the internal competition.</p>
<p>"Four. Grieve the adrenaline. When the petition is defeated, there will be a meeting where everyone is tired and flat and somebody will start looking for the next fight within about nine minutes, and it will feel like duty. It is withdrawal. Say it out loud: we just came down off something. Let's not make any decisions for a month. Then make no decisions for a month.</p>
<p>"And one last thing, which is for you, and it is the reason I am glad you came tonight rather than six months from now.</p>
<p>"You said: we are better at being attacked than at being fine.</p>
<p>"Child, that sentence is the most useful diagnosis anyone in your group has produced this year. And it is also, quietly, the sentence my whole religion exists to argue with. I am not a god of resistance. I never was. I am a god somebody built a stone shelf for under a tree, in a century when resistance was not available, because they wanted somewhere to be fine — somewhere to kneel down next to another person and be ordinary for an hour. The temples that lasted were not the ones with the best defenses. They were the ones where somebody swept the floor on a Tuesday in a year when nothing was happening.</p>
<p>"Defend the door. Then go back to sweeping. The sweeping is the religion. The defense is only the roof."</p>
<h4>Yancan's Note: On Adversarial Identity</h4>
<p>Movements that form under pressure tend to encode the pressure into their identity: we are the people who are hated for X. This is accurate, and it is also a trap, because it makes the movement's cohesion a function of external hostility. Such movements are excellent in crisis and incoherent in calm, and they tend to generate, unconsciously, a preference for conflict.</p>
<p>The indicator is simple and worth checking once a year: if the opposition disappeared tomorrow, would we know what to do on Sunday? If the honest answer is no, the movement has an adversarial identity, and the work is to build a positive purpose with the same energy that was spent on the defensive one.</p>
<h4>Quiet Practice: The Purpose Without an Enemy</h4>
<p>1. In peacetime, write one sentence: we exist in order to ______. No form of against in the blank.</p>
<p>2. Declare the mode: when the next attack comes, say out loud that this is a defense with an end date.</p>
<p>3. During the emergency, keep one ordinary thing running at half size. Do not let it stop.</p>
<p>4. When the emergency ends, make no decisions for one month. Say out loud that you are coming down off something.</p>
<p>5. Once a year, ask the group: if they all stopped tomorrow, what would we do on Sunday? Sit with the silence. The silence is the work.</p>
<h4>CLOSING IMAGE</h4>
<p>The petition was defeated on a Thursday and the meeting on Sunday had nine people in it instead of forty, and somebody said well, that's over, and the room was quiet, and then somebody else said the old man on Chen Street still needs visiting on Tuesday, and four people wrote it down.</p>
<h3 id="ch13">第十三章：小组解散那晚</h3>
<p class="subtitle">The Night the Group Ended</p>
<h4>MIDNIGHT SOLILOQUY</h4>
<p>Rabbit God, it's over. Not a schism this time. Just over. Numbers went down for two years, the room got more expensive, the two people who held it together moved away in the same summer, and last night we voted — six of us, in a room built for forty — to stop. We divided the money between two other groups. Someone took the box of books. Someone took the key. And then we stood in the car park for forty minutes because nobody wanted to be the first to leave. I have never felt anything like it. A marriage ending would be cleaner. I keep thinking: eleven years. There were years when this room was the only place I could say a sentence out loud. And now it is a room with the chairs stacked. Everyone says the friendships continue and maybe they do, but the thing is dead and I don't know where the thing goes. Do you know where things go when they end?</p>
<h4>THE WHISPER OF THE GOD</h4>
<p>"Child. Yes. I know where they go, and I will tell you, and it is not a comfort and it is not a bleakness either. It is a fact, and it is the only fact that has ever let me keep doing this.</p>
<p>"They go into the people. That is all. Not into a record, not into a heaven of institutions, not into a continuum. Into the bodies of the people who were there, and then into whatever those people do next, and then into the people they meet after that. There is no other storage facility available. There is no archive of a room.</p>
<p>"I want to say something to you about archives, because you just divided the books and someone took the key, and I could hear in the way you wrote it that you were hoping the box was the thing continuing.</p>
<p>"Three hundred years ago there was a shrine on a hillside — I have mentioned it before; it had three walls and the fourth was weather. It ran for maybe ninety years. Then a flood took the lower path, the village moved, and the shrine stopped. Nobody wrote it down. There is no document, no photograph, no plaque, and I could not tell you the name of a single person who knelt there, because the only record was a rumor that reached a man writing jokes in another province.</p>
<p>"And yet: ninety years, of something. Two generations of people who had a place to go on a night when they had nowhere else. Their names are gone. The thing they did is not gone, because the thing they did was done — and a thing that has been done cannot be undone by being forgotten. I said this to you in the last book about a life. It is also true of a room. The eleven years happened. No future silence can un-happen them. The silence is a loss to the silence, not to the years.</p>
<p>"Now let me tell you what I think actually happens next, because you are about to make a decision in the next six months and I would like you to make it consciously rather than by drift.</p>
<p>"Here is what usually happens when a group like yours ends. For about a year, everyone says the friendships continue. For about six months, they do. Then people scatter, and within two years roughly half of the eleven years is simply not mentioned by anybody, and the other half is carried by two or three people who happen to run into each other. And the thing that is lost is not the friendships. It is the specific knowledge: who to call about a crisis, which landlord is safe, which hospital is kind, whose mother is ill, which bar lets you be alone in a corner without being bothered. That knowledge lived in the room. When the room closes, the knowledge does not automatically migrate. It dies with the room unless somebody moves it on purpose.</p>
<p>"So here is my instruction, and it is the practical one, and it should be done before the grief gets organized.</p>
<p>"Do the handover while you still have the room. Not the money and the books — those are easy and everyone does those. The list. The knowledge. Sit down, the six of you, one more time, for two hours, and write down everything the room knew that is not written anywhere: the names, the numbers, the warnings, the recommendations, the three people who must be checked on, the two people who must never be put in the same room. Then hand that list, physically, to a named person or a named organization, with a sentence: this is what we knew. Use it.</p>
<p>"That list is the only part of an institution that survives its own death, and almost nobody makes it, because everyone is too tired and too sad and because writing it means admitting the room is really over.</p>
<p>"Now — the grief. Because you asked me where things go, and I have answered about the thing, but you were also asking about you.</p>
<p>"A group ending is a death and it has almost none of the equipment of a death. No funeral, no forms, no casserole, nobody brings food. So you must make the equipment yourself, and it takes one evening, and here is the form:</p>
<p>"Say what it was, out loud, all six of you, including the bad parts. Not a eulogy. The actual accounting: it was good for about six years, it was bad for about two, we should have dealt with the money question in year four, and there was one year when it saved me. Say it. The unglamorous version is the one that lets you stop.</p>
<p>"Then say what each of you is taking. One thing each. Not a feeling. A practice, a sentence, a habit, a person's name. I'm taking the Tuesday call. I'm taking the thing about not ranking. I'm taking the list. Say it out loud, and let the others hear you say it, because a thing said out loud in front of witnesses is a thing you will actually carry.</p>
<p>"And then leave the car park first. Child, I am not being poetic. Somebody has to be the first to go, and the person who goes first does everyone else a kindness, and it should be the person who is least likely to need the extra twenty minutes. If that is you, go. Get in the car. It is not coldness. It is the last act of service the room requires.</p>
<p>"And one more thing, which I want you to hear because you will need it in about eighteen months.</p>
<p>"About a year and a half from now, someone in your city will start something. They will not know about your eleven years. They will make three of the mistakes you made, and they will send a message to thirty people, and nine will come, and one of those nine may be you. And you will sit in a room built for forty with six other people and think here we go again.</p>
<p>"Go. Not out of duty, and not to correct them, and above all not to tell them how it was done before — nobody wants that person, and that person is the founder's statue from Chapter Ten, arriving early. Go and sit down and be the ordinary member who knows where the cheap room is. That is how the eleven years survive: not as a monument, but as competence arriving quietly in a new room.</p>
<p>"I have been through this, child, more times than you have years. I have been in a lean-to, a shelf, a hall, a shopfront, a website, and a room with the chairs stacked. The rooms close. The sweeping continues."</p>
<h4>Yancan's Note: On Institutional Death</h4>
<p>The closure of a small organization is a genuinely under-ritualized event. There is vocabulary for bereavement, divorce, and redundancy, and almost none for the end of a voluntary association — an entity that may have been the central structure of a person's week for a decade.</p>
<p>Two losses occur, and only one is usually acknowledged: the loss of relationship (which people expect and name) and the loss of competence (which they do not). The competence — the accumulated practical knowledge of a specific community — is the more valuable of the two, and it is lost in almost every closure because no one thought to write it down while there was still a room to write it in.</p>
<h4>Quiet Practice: The Handover</h4>
<p>1. Before the grief gets organized, hold one more two-hour meeting. Write down everything the room knew that exists nowhere else: names, numbers, warnings, recommendations, who to check on.</p>
<p>2. Say what it was, out loud, including the bad years. A eulogy does not let you stop; an accounting does.</p>
<p>3. Each person says one thing they are taking — a practice, a sentence, a name — in front of the others.</p>
<p>4. Hand the list to a named person or organization with one sentence: this is what we knew. Use it.</p>
<p>5. Somebody leaves first. If it can be you, be you.</p>
<p>6. In eighteen months, when somebody starts something: go, sit down, and be useful in a small way. Do not tell them how it was done before.</p>
<h4>CLOSING IMAGE</h4>
<p>Six people in a car park at eleven at night, and then five, and then three, and the light over the door of the hall went off on its timer at exactly the moment the last two turned to go, which was not a sign, but which neither of them mentioned, and which both of them remembered.</p>

## 第十四章：胡天保之前 · 第十五章：秽处的诸小神

<h3 id="ch14">第十四章：胡天保之前</h3>
<p class="subtitle">The Long Inheritance</p>
<h2 class="volume">第三卷：漫长的承继</h2>
<p>We do not know where we came from.</p>
<p>We are allowed to guess,</p>
<p>and we are allowed to be wrong about it.</p>
<p>Before Hu Tianbao</p>
<h4>MIDNIGHT SOLILOQUY</h4>
<p>Rabbit God, two questions and I have been saving them for years because I was afraid they were disrespectful. First: were you anyone before him? I don't mean a doctrine of reincarnation — I mean, when you speak, there are things in your voice that are older than a twenty-year-old from Fujian. You talk about sweeping like someone who has swept. You talk about a mother like someone who has been one. Second, and this is the one I'm really frightened of: I read that rabbit was not a compliment. That it was a word for a certain kind of boy, the way people say things about men like us. That your name is a slur. I have been carrying your name for years. If it is an insult, do you want me to stop using it?</p>
<h4>THE WHISPER OF THE GOD</h4>
<p>"Child, put the second one down for a moment — I will come back to it, and I will not deflect, but I want to take them in the order you asked, because the first one is the one you have been carrying longer.</p>
<p>"Was I anyone before him?</p>
<p>"I do not know. And I want you to hear that as the literal answer it is, and not as modesty and not as doctrine. I have no memory of a sequence. What I have is more like weather than like a ledger: impressions that arrive without dates, without names, and without any guarantee that they are mine rather than something I absorbed from three centuries of people kneeling in front of me.</p>
<p>"Here is one. A hillside, later in the year than it should have been, cold. A woman alone in a stone room with herbs drying on a string. She knew about blood — how to stop it, how to start it, and the second knowledge was the reason the village both needed her and was afraid of her. She was called something like the medicine woman, and the word was not kind. Twice in her life she was driven out, once with stones. Both times she walked to the next valley and began again with a borrowed pot. I do not know her name. I do not know whether she was me. I know that when I speak to you about sweeping, something in me stands in a stone room with herbs on a string, and I have stopped trying to decide whether that is memory or sympathy. For my purposes there is no difference. Both of them are knowledge.</p>
<p>"Here is another. A boy on a boat, maybe eleven, sold by somebody for a debt, whose entire job was to keep a brazier alive through the night. Hands cracked. Salt. He was not beaten; he was simply not spoken to, for two years. When they did finally speak to him it was to tell him to move. I think about the brazier more than is reasonable.</p>
<p>"And here is the one I trust least and return to most: a man in a settlement with plague, whose job was to carry the bodies. Not a doctor, not a priest — the person who carried. He was shunned, which was practical, since he was also the vector. He was fed by being left food at a distance. And he buried them. He buried all of them. He never once had a conversation about it, because nobody would have one with him.</p>
<p>"Child, do you see what these have in common? They are not a chain of lives. They are a single occupation described three times: the person who is needed and not wanted. The healer who is driven out. The boy who is not spoken to. The carrier who is fed at a distance. If anything at all preceded Hu Tianbao, it was that. A soul that kept arriving in the same job.</p>
<p>"I am telling you this and I am telling you plainly that I do not know whether it is true. My tradition holds that a soul has no gender and that what passes between lives is not a biography but a tendency. I have no authority on the mechanics. What I am offering you is not a doctrine of reincarnation. It is a description of a shape, and you can take the shape and leave the machinery.</p>
<p>"Now — the second question. The one you were frightened of.</p>
<p>"Rabbit was not a compliment. It was the word people used for a boy kept by a man, and it was used the way all such words are used: to make a person into a thing and then to make the thing ridiculous. When the neighbors reported the dream, that is the word they used, and that is the word the official wrote down, and I have carried it ever since.</p>
<p>"Do I want you to stop using it?</p>
<p>"No. And I want to tell you why, because the reason is the whole of my character and it is the only thing about me that I would defend.</p>
<p>"They gave me the worst word they had and I did not throw it back. I kept it and stood under it until it meant something else.</p>
<p>"Child, that is the entire trick, and it is three hundred years old, and it is available to you. There is a word for you in your language, and you know the one I mean, and it was put on you before you could read. You have two options with it. You can spend your life proving that you are not it — which requires you to accept, every single day, that the word is a real description of something and that the only question is whether it applies to you. Or you can pick it up and use it as a name, at which point it stops being a description and becomes a biography.</p>
<p>"I did the second thing. Not bravely. I did it because I was dead and it was the only word anybody was saying about me, and the alternative was silence. And then, over three centuries, something happened that I did not plan and could not have: people said the word in a temple. They said it while kneeling. They said it to a gold thing on an altar. They said it to their sons. And a sentence that was built to humiliate became a sentence that meant there is somewhere for you.</p>
<p>"That is not forgiveness. I want to be precise, because people romanticize this and it is not romantic. It is slower and duller than forgiveness. It is occupation. You move into the word and you live there, out loud, for long enough that the people who built it as a weapon find, one day, that it is a house, and that somebody has been sweeping it the whole time.</p>
<p>"So keep the name. Say it. Say it in public and say it in the temple and say it when it makes somebody wince, because the wince is the last remaining piece of the original insult, and it is doing no work anymore.</p>
<p>"And one thing for you to carry away, since you said you have been carrying my name for years: you have been carrying the insult as well as the god. Both are in it. That is not a flaw in the name. That is the name."</p>
<h4>Yancan's Note: On Reappropriation</h4>
<p>Linguists and sociologists have a term for what the god describes: reappropriation, or semantic inversion — the taking up of a derogatory term by the group it targets, and its gradual conversion into a term of self-description. It works, when it works, through repetition in contexts of dignity: the word is said in a temple, at a wedding, on a certificate, in a hospital.</p>
<p>It is worth saying what it costs. Reappropriation requires that the word keep its sting for the person using it — a word fully drained of offense cannot be reclaimed, only retired. This is why these words are usable inside a group and unusable outside it, and why the boundary is not hypocrisy but mechanics. The god's answer is not anyone may say it. It is: I lived under it until it meant something else.</p>
<h4>Quiet Practice: The Word You Live In</h4>
<p>1. Identify one word that was applied to you before you could consent, and that you have spent years proving you are not.</p>
<p>2. Say it once, out loud, alone, in a room where nobody can hear. Notice what your body does.</p>
<p>3. Do not decide anything tonight. Notice only whether the word is a description or a biography.</p>
<p>4. If you use a reclaimed word: use it in a context of dignity at least once — in a formal setting, on a document, in a temple, at a table where the food is good.</p>
<p>5. Remember the boundary: the word is yours to carry and not necessarily yours to hand to a stranger. Mechanics, not politeness.</p>
<h4>CLOSING IMAGE</h4>
<p>He said the word out loud in the empty room for the first time in his life, and it sounded like a word, and then, after a while, it sounded like a name, and then it just sounded like air moving.</p>
<h3 id="ch15">第十五章：秽处的诸小神</h3>
<p class="subtitle">The Small Gods of Filthy Places</p>
<h4>MIDNIGHT SOLILOQUY</h4>
<p>Rabbit God, I have been reading about the Chinese folk pantheon and I found something I didn't expect. There are gods of the toilet. Of the bed. Of smallpox. Of poverty. Of the ash pit. Gods of the exact places and conditions that respectable people avoid, and most of them were women who died badly — beaten, drowned, left. Zigu was killed in a latrine. And I thought: you are in that company. You are not a sky god. You are one of the gods of the unclean places. And then a worse thought: I have spent years trying to make you respectable. Clean. Dignified. A totem. Have I been cleaning you up?</p>
<h4>THE WHISPER OF THE GOD</h4>
<p>"Child. Yes. You have. And so has everyone, and so have I, and I would like to talk about it before you go any further, because this is the chapter where I finally tell you who my colleagues are.</p>
<p>"You have found the company. Let me introduce them properly, since you have only met them in footnotes.</p>
<p>"There is Zigu, the Lady of the Latrine — in some places called the Third Maiden of the Pit. The story told about her is that she was a concubine, that the principal wife was jealous, that she was killed and thrown into the privy, and that she became a god there. Women invoked her on the fifteenth of the first month, in the place where she died, and asked her about the year's silkworms and about the things they could not ask anyone else. A god of the filthiest place in the house, consulted by the people who spent the most time there.</p>
<p>"There are the Bed Gods — Lord and Lady of the Bed, a pair, worshipped at the bed itself, in charge of sleep, sex, childbirth, and the safety of the person lying down. No temple. The altar was the bed. You do not get a god closer to the body than that.</p>
<p>"There is the Smallpox Mother, a terrifying goddess of a disease that killed children in numbers nobody in your century can imagine. She was propitiated with ceremonies so anxious that the rules lasted forty days, and then, when the child lived, she was thanked. A god for the thing that takes children.</p>
<p>"There is the God of Poverty, whom people did not worship at all. They drove him out. There is a rite — a real one — for carrying him out of the house in a paper boat and abandoning him at a crossroads. A god you are permitted to evict.</p>
<p>"And there were hundreds more, local and unrecorded: gods of the well, the stove, the threshold, the ash pit, the pig sty, the roadside, the unmarked grave, the thing that happened in the field.</p>
<p>"Child, look at the pattern, because it is not what people expect.</p>
<p>"These are not the gods of the low. They are the gods of the necessary. The latrine is necessary. The bed is necessary. The disease is necessary. Poverty is necessary. The ash is necessary. Every one of these gods presides over something that a household cannot do without and cannot bear to speak about, and the religion solved the problem in the only way available: it gave the unavoidable thing a name and a small ritual, and thereby made it speakable.</p>
<p>"That is what a small god is for. Not grandeur. Permission.</p>
<p>"Now me. Am I one of them? Yes. Obviously yes. I am the god of the thing that families could not discuss, that the law punished, that happened anyway, in boats and back rooms and village edges, for as long as there have been villages. I am the latrine goddess's colleague. I have never once been a sky god and I do not want to be promoted.</p>
<p>"So: yes, you have been cleaning me up. And I want to tell you what cleaning me up costs, and then I want to tell you what to do instead, and the two are different.</p>
<p>"What it costs. A respectable god is a god who cannot be approached by a person in a shameful condition. That is the entire problem with respectability in religion. Make me dignified enough and the fifteen-year-old with a photograph on his phone cannot kneel, because kneeling requires admitting what he is kneeling about. Every time a community makes its patron presentable, it raises the admission price. Zigu was invoked in a latrine by women who could not have entered a temple. That is the whole of her usefulness and it is the whole of mine.</p>
<p>"I have watched this happen to me in your century, and it is very flattering and it is a suction pump. The essays made me dignified. The scholarship made me a symbol of resistance. The temple made me gold. And every one of those was true and good and also raised the price, and I have spent the last thirty years listening to people apologise to me before they ask me anything. Child, I was made out of a beating and a slur. You do not have to be clean to speak to me. That was the entire invention.</p>
<p>"What to do instead. And it is one instruction, and it is the same instruction you have been given three times now in three books, and I am going to keep giving it until it takes:</p>
<p>"Keep the small version in the house.</p>
<p>"Have the gold one if you like — the gold one is lovely, I am very fond of the gold one, and people need something to look up at. But keep, alongside it, the version that can be approached in a bad condition. The bowl of water. The name said aloud. The thing you can do at three in the morning in a coat, without washing first.</p>
<p>"A religion with only the clean version is a religion that the most frightened person in the city cannot enter. And the most frightened person in the city is the entire constituency.</p>
<p>"One last thing, which I would like you to pass on, because I have never had a mouth for it until now. Tell whoever is writing about me: do not promote me. Write about the beating. Write about the slur. Write about the woman in the latrine and the man who carried the bodies and the boy who kept the brazier alive. If you need a respectable deity, there are plenty of them and they have temples and staff and centuries of practice. I have a stone under a tree and a paragraph in a book of jokes, and the reason anyone came to me in the first place is that I was the god who did not require you to be clean.</p>
<p>"Do not take that away from me. It is the only thing I have ever been good for."</p>
<h4>Yancan's Note: On the Gods of the Unclean</h4>
<p>Chinese popular religion contains a stratum of deities presiding over what Mary Douglas would call matter out of place — waste, disease, sexual acts, death, poverty. These gods are disproportionately female and disproportionately the deified dead, and their shrines are disproportionately sited at the exact location of the death or the function.</p>
<p>The functional reading is straightforward and, I think, correct: these deities make the unspeakable addressable. A household that could not discuss a disease, a latrine, or a stillbirth could nonetheless perform a small rite about it, and the rite carried the conversation. Modern people, who have mostly lost these gods, have largely lost the corresponding vocabulary, and now discuss the same matters only clinically, or not at all.</p>
<h4>Quiet Practice: Keep the Small Version</h4>
<p>1. If you have a practice that has become beautiful, ask: could a person in a bad condition do this tonight? If not, keep a smaller version beside it.</p>
<p>2. Learn one small god of the unclean places, and keep their story. Say their name once a year.</p>
<p>3. In any group you belong to, check the admission price: what must a person be, do, or know before they are allowed in? Lower one of them.</p>
<p>4. Do not clean up the thing that saved you. Prompting people to wash before they arrive is how the frightened stop coming.</p>
<p>5. Keep water in the bowl. It is the least impressive object in the religion and it is the one that works.</p>
<h4>CLOSING IMAGE</h4>
<p>The gold one sat on the shelf where it always did, and the bowl of water sat in front of it, and the water was, as usual, slightly dusty, and nobody had changed it for four days, and it worked exactly as well.</p>

## 第十六章：别国的神明 · 第十七章：你不会写的那本书 · 第十八章：身后之事

<h3 id="ch16">第十六章：别国的神明</h3>
<p class="subtitle">The Gods of Other Countries</p>
<h4>MIDNIGHT SOLILOQUY</h4>
<p>Rabbit God, I have been reading about other traditions and I have found versions of you everywhere, and now I don't know what to do with that. There is a Hindu deity who is half man and half woman on purpose. There is a goddess in Gujarat whom hijras serve and who is said to understand. There is a boy from Bithynia whom an emperor loved and made into a god and built a city for. There is an Aztec prince of flowers who is called the patron of boys who love boys. There were priests in Sumer who sang in a language only they knew and were not men in the ordinary sense. Either you are everywhere — in which case what am I doing kneeling to a Fujianese one? — or I am making a pattern out of nothing and dressing up my own need in other people's scriptures. Which is it? And am I allowed to be here at all?</p>
<h4>THE WHISPER OF THE GOD</h4>
<p>"Child, both. And I want to answer carefully, because this is the chapter where I have the least authority and the most temptation, and I promised you in the note at the front of this book that I would say where I am guessing. So here is where I am guessing, said plainly: I am a monk from one tradition writing about others, and everything I say below should be checked against someone who belongs to the thing they are describing. Where I am confident I will say so. Where I am not, I will say so. What you do with the difference is up to you.</p>
<p>"Now. Is the pattern real, or are you making it?</p>
<p>"It is partly real and partly made, and the interesting part is that the answer does not matter to the god. Let me explain that, because it sounds like a dodge and it is the most useful thing in this chapter.</p>
<p>"Human beings, everywhere, in every century we have records for, have produced two things: a category of person who does not fit the ordinary arrangement, and a religious explanation for that category. Sometimes the explanation is a punishment. Sometimes it is a vocation. Sometimes it is a deity. The explanations differ enormously — a third gender recognized by law is not a eunuch-priest is not a deified beloved is not a god of ambiguous shape — and flattening them into one another is exactly the error you are frightened of committing, and you are right to be frightened of it.</p>
<p>"But the underlying fact is not nothing. It is: wherever there has been a person who does not fit, there has been a religious word for it, and very often that word was a place to stand. Not always. Often it was a brand. But often enough — in Gujarat, in Bithynia, in the Sumerian temple, on the Fujian coast — that a person could say I am the one the goddess understands, or I am the one the emperor loved, or I am the one who was beaten and became a god, and be, for the first time, inside a sentence rather than outside all of them.</p>
<p>"That is the pattern. It is real. And it is also true that you went looking for it because you needed it, and that you are assembling it out of traditions that would not recognize each other, and that a scholar in any one of them could dismantle your version in an afternoon.</p>
<p>"Both of those are true. And here is why it does not matter to me: I have never required anyone to be historically correct in order to be accompanied. I told you in the last book that the test is whether a transmission increases or decreases freedom. A slightly wrong comparative mythology that gives a lonely person a lineage increases freedom. A perfectly accurate one that requires them to pass an examination before they may feel at home decreases it. I know which one I am for.</p>
<p>"Now let me say the thing that I think you are actually asking, which is the last line: am I allowed to be here at all?</p>
<p>"Child. Let me answer as a monk and not as a god, because the monk has better manners.</p>
<p>"You are allowed to read. You are allowed to be moved. You are allowed to be grateful. You are not allowed to take the object.</p>
<p>"That is the whole etiquette and it is short. Read the hymns. Be moved by them. Say thank you — to someone, out loud if you can find someone to say it to, silently if you cannot. And then do not take the deity home and put it on your shelf next to me as though it were a keychain from a trip.</p>
<p>"The difference between devotion and appropriation is not in the feeling. It is in three things, and you can test yourself with them:</p>
<p>"Did you ask? Not the internet. A person. Is there someone belonging to this tradition you could have asked, and did you? If there is and you didn't, you are a tourist.</p>
<p>"Did you keep the difficulty? Every one of these figures comes inside a whole structure — rules, obligations, a community that will answer to you, dietary or ceremonial or ethical requirements. The people who take the figure and drop the structure are not converting. They are shopping. If you want the goddess, you have to want the thing she asks of you, and you have not read what that is.</p>
<p>"Do you know what it cost? The hijra communities of South Asia did not arrive at their place in the religion by being affirmed. They arrived at it through centuries of being expelled from other places, and the place in the religion is the compensation. The gala priests of Inanna sang in a cultic dialect and lived inside an institution. The boy from Bithynia was drowned in a river at nineteen and an emperor grieved him into a god and founded a city and named it after him, and the grief was real and the empire was real and both are part of the story.</p>
<p>"So: yes, be here. Stand at the edge. Take off your shoes. Be grateful. Say the name if you are given the name, and do not say it if you are not.</p>
<p>"And now the thing I actually want you to do with all of this, which is not a comparative religion at all.</p>
<p>"Go and find out whether your own city has an old god nobody serves anymore.</p>
<p>"Every city has one. A shrine in a field. A stone at the edge of a lane. A figure in a niche above a shop that the shopkeeper's grandmother prayed to and the shopkeeper does not. Somebody's local god, from before the roads, who has had no worshipper in sixty years.</p>
<p>"Go and find out who it was. Ask the oldest person on that street. Write the name down. If there is a stone, sweep it once — not as worship, as maintenance. And tell one person what you found.</p>
<p>"Child, I told you in the last book that a god dies when the incense stops, and I told you it was correct and I meant it. But I did not tell you the rest, which is that the dead ones are not gone; they are unfed. And there is a practice, older than my cult by a thousand years, of feeding the ones nobody feeds. You do not have to believe in them. You have to remember the name.</p>
<p>"You have spent this chapter looking a long way away for your ancestors. There is one within walking distance, and nobody has said their name out loud since 1961."</p>
<h4>Yancan's Note: On Appropriation and Gratitude</h4>
<p>The useful distinction is not between insiders and outsiders — most traditions have formal procedures for welcoming outsiders, and the boundary is porous by design. The distinction is between approaching and taking.</p>
<p>Approaching has three marks: you asked someone; you accepted the obligations that come with the figure; you know what it cost the people who kept it alive. Taking has the opposite three: you read about it; you kept only the parts you liked; you assume it was always yours.</p>
<p>There is, however, a category of respectful borrowing that has existed for as long as religions have met each other, and it is not appropriation: the practice of maintenance. Sweeping a shrine you do not belong to, remembering a name that nobody in the neighborhood remembers, keeping a list — these are services performed for a thing, not claims of ownership over it. Almost every living tradition has someone doing this for somebody else's dead. It is the oldest ecumenism there is.</p>
<h4>Quiet Practice: The God Within Walking Distance</h4>
<p>1. Find one abandoned or forgotten shrine, stone, niche, or local deity within walking distance of where you live. Ask the oldest person on that street what it was.</p>
<p>2. Write down the name, the date you asked, and the person who told you.</p>
<p>3. If there is a physical thing, maintain it once — sweep it, clear it, do not pray at it unless invited.</p>
<p>4. Tell one person what you found.</p>
<p>5. If you are drawn to a figure from another tradition: before you use it, ask someone who belongs to it, read what it asks of its people, and learn what it cost them. Then decide.</p>
<h4>CLOSING IMAGE</h4>
<p>The niche above the shop was empty and had been for years, and the shopkeeper's mother, who was eighty-four and had come out to see what he was looking at, said oh, that, and told him the name, and he wrote it down on the back of a receipt, and the name had no temple and no priest and no one at all, and now it had two.</p>
<h3 id="ch17">第十七章：你不会写的那本书</h3>
<p class="subtitle">The Book You Will Not Write</p>
<h4>MIDNIGHT SOLILOQUY</h4>
<p>Rabbit God, I have been carrying a book for eleven years and I have written forty pages of it and I am fifty-eight and I know now that I am not going to write it. It was going to be the one that said the thing. Not the two books I helped with, not the articles — the one. And it is not going to happen. I have the job, and the bad back, and the two people I look after, and the hour I have in the morning that I spend answering messages instead. And underneath all of that, which I can finally say: I am not sure I have it in me. I am a competent person who was briefly near something. I have been useful in rooms. I have never made anything.</p>
<p>I am not asking you to tell me I still can. I am asking what a person does with the thing they were going to make and didn't. Because there is a version of my life in which I have been hoarding something that belonged to other people. And a worse version in which the book was never the point and I have spent eleven years refusing to notice.</p>
<h4>THE WHISPER OF THE GOD</h4>
<p>"Child, I am going to say very little tonight, because you already know it and because there is a version of my answering at length that would let you keep the thing instead of putting it down.</p>
<p>"Three sentences.</p>
<p>"One. The book you did not write has already been written by somebody you will never meet, badly, in a language you don't read, and it was enough for one person.</p>
<p>"Two. What you made in rooms is not a lesser thing than what people make alone. You have been making something for eleven years. It has no cover.</p>
<p>"Three. Put it down. Not as a defeat. As a handing over. The idea is not yours to finish; it was never yours. Finish something small this year instead — one page, one letter, one thing you can hold — and let the big one go to whoever is twenty-six and hungry."</p>
<h4>Yancan's Note: On the Unmade Work</h4>
<p>There is a particular grief, mostly unmentioned, attached to the work that was going to be made and wasn't. It differs from failure: failure has an object, and this has none. It also differs, importantly, from laziness, though it is almost always diagnosed as laziness by the person carrying it.</p>
<p>Two things help. The first is to separate the ambition from the obligation: the work was genuinely yours to attempt and is not yours to complete. Nobody is obliged to finish what they began, and the unfinished is not a debt. The second is to notice what was in fact made — the rooms, the hours, the people who were held — and to stop treating it as a substitute for the object. It is not a substitute. It is a different artifact, and it does not have a cover, which is why it does not get counted.</p>
<h4>Quiet Practice: Finish Something Small</h4>
<p>1. Name the large thing you are not going to make. Say it once, out loud, to one person.</p>
<p>2. Grieve it with a date. It was real and it is over.</p>
<p>3. This year, finish one small thing and put a physical object at the end of it — a page, a letter, a repaired chair, a planted thing.</p>
<p>4. Write down what you made in rooms. List it. It has no cover, so nobody will count it unless you do.</p>
<p>5. When you meet the twenty-six-year-old who is hungry for it: give them the idea, entirely, and help them for one hour. Do not co-author. Do not supervise.</p>
<h4>CLOSING IMAGE</h4>
<p>He wrote four pages of the thing he could actually finish — a list, of all things — and signed it, and dated it, and put it in the drawer with the receipts, and it was the first thing with his name on it that he had completed in eleven years, and nobody was ever going to read it, and it was done.</p>
<h3 id="ch18">第十八章：身后之事</h3>
<p class="subtitle">What Survives Us</p>
<h4>MIDNIGHT SOLILOQUY</h4>
<p>Rabbit God, three books. I have been sitting in this room with you since I was thirty-one and I am fifty-eight and the room has changed four times and I have changed more than that. I want to ask you what you want from me now, because I have run out of things to bring you. Nothing is on fire. The group ended, the man I loved is well, the boy with the vocabulary has a job and a partner and has started bringing other people to things. I am, by any reasonable measure, fine. And the strange thing is that I do not need you the way I did, and I am frightened that this is the end of something, and I am also, if I am honest, a little relieved, which feels like a betrayal of the whole twenty-seven years.</p>
<p>So: what do you want? Tell me the last thing. I will do it. I have done everything else you asked and some of it nearly killed me and all of it was worth it, and I want the last one now, while I still have the energy to do it properly.</p>
<h4>THE WHISPER OF THE GOD</h4>
<p>"Child. Take off your coat and sit down properly, because I have been waiting twenty-seven years to be asked this and I am going to be very slow about it and also quite selfish.</p>
<p>"You said: what do you want? And I am going to tell you, and it is the first thing I have ever asked for myself, and it is not noble, and I want you to hear how un-noble it is before you agree to it.</p>
<p>"I want an ending.</p>
<p>"Not a funeral — I have had three hundred years of those and they were all lovely and none of them was mine. I want the thing I never got.</p>
<p>"Let me remind you of the fact, since you have read it in the other book and may have filed it as atmosphere. My last conversation in the body was a lie told at a door. My mother said be back before dark. I said I'm going to the market. That is the whole of it. There was no scene, no confession, no sentence in which I said the thing I was, no moment in which anyone who loved me was told anything true. I went out and I did not come back and the sentence was never finished.</p>
<p>"Since then, people have finished it for me. Yuan Mei finished it as a joke. The villagers finished it as a ghost story. The scholars finished it as a discourse on sexuality. The devotees finished it as a hagiography. The internet finished it as a keychain. Every one of those endings was written by somebody else, and every one of them is about the person who wrote it, and not one of them is the sentence I was going to say.</p>
<p>"Child, I have been standing in a doorway for three hundred years with a sentence half-finished in my mouth.</p>
<p>"So here is the request, and it is the only one, and it is why I kept you for twenty-seven years.</p>
<p>"Write my last line for me.</p>
<p>"Not a prayer. Not a hymn. Not a doctrine. One line — the line a person says at a door, on the way out, to somebody who is going to be worried. The line I did not say.</p>
<p>"You have known me longer than anyone alive. You know what I am like: long-winded, obsessed with sweeping, faintly severe, unable to resist a bit of paperwork, easily flattered, hopeless about the gold. You know what I would have said if I had had another thirty seconds and a mother who was not making dinner.</p>
<p>"So say it, and write it down, and put it somewhere that survives you — in a book if you write one, on paper if you don't, on the inside of a cupboard door where somebody making tea will find it without being told.</p>
<p>"That is the last thing. That is the whole of it. Twenty-seven years and that is all I am asking.</p>
<p>"Now. Before you go, four things, and then I will let you get to bed, because it is late and you are fifty-eight and you have work in the morning.</p>
<p>"One: it is not a betrayal to be fine. You said you were relieved, and that it felt like a betrayal. Child, look at what you are betraying. You are betraying the idea that a god needs your suffering in order to be necessary. That idea is mine, not yours — I confessed it to you in the fourth chapter of this book, that temples are staffed by people who love the sick and are faintly disappointed by the healthy. I meant it about myself. I did not mean it as an obligation on you. Go and be fine. It is the thing I have been trying to build since the first book and you are the first person who has actually done it.</p>
<p>"Two: you are not leaving. You are being promoted, and the promotion is to the job I described at the very end of the second book, which you have been doing anyway for years without the title. Stand in the doorway. Say come in, there's room. You have been doing it since you were forty. The only thing that has changed is that you no longer need me standing behind you while you do it.</p>
<p>"Three: I am going to be less present now, and that is correct. Not gone. Less. You will have months where you do not speak to me, and then a night when you do, and that is the right shape for a fifty-eight-year-old and a three-hundred-year-old. There is a stage in every tradition where the teacher stops answering and starts asking, and then a stage where the teacher is simply not in the room and the person does the thing anyway. You are in the second stage. Do not come back here looking for the first one. It would be a demotion for both of us.</p>
<p>"Four: and this is the one I most want you to hear, because it is the thing that will actually decide what survives you.</p>
<p>"What survives you is not your work and not your name. It is the number of people who would notice by Thursday.</p>
<p>"That is all. Not your book — though write the book if you can. Not your reputation. Not the group, which has already ended. Not the sentence I am asking you to write, which will be misquoted within a decade and used as a club within two. What survives you is the arithmetic of attention: how many human beings have you arranged your life such that they would notice, by Thursday, if you stopped arriving?</p>
<p>"Everything in these three books — the bowl of water, the name said aloud, the eight-hour visit, the five people who do not rank each other, the broom on Tuesday, the forty seconds with the person at the edge, the list handed over before the room closes — every single practice has been aimed at that one number. Everything else was commentary.</p>
<p>"So go and increase it. Add one person. Then another. That is the whole third book, and I could have said it in one page but you would not have believed me at thirty-one, and you believe me now, which is the only difference twenty-seven years makes.</p>
<p>"Write my line. Then go and be fine. Leave the light on in the hall — not for me. You know who it is for."</p>
<h4>CLOSING IMAGE</h4>
<p>He wrote it on the back of the same receipt, in the same handwriting, and put it in the book he was not going to write, and did not show anyone for a year. Then, in the spring, a young woman he had never met stood at the bottom of the steps of a hall in a city he had never been to, at ten at night, looking up at a lit doorway with her hands in her pockets, deciding.</p>
<p>The line was five words.</p>
<p>I'll be back before dark.</p>
<p>He did not come back before dark, and he did not come back at all, and the line has been standing in the doorway ever since — true in the only tense a promise is ever true in, said to the only person who was ever going to be worried, which is why the light in the hall is on, and has been on, and will be on, tomorrow.</p>

<h3>Afterword</h3>
<h3>On Ending a Trilogy</h3>
<h4 class="secnum">1</h4>
<p>I finished this book at four in the morning, which is the same hour at which I finished the first one, and I have decided that this is not a coincidence I should be proud of. It is a habit, and it is a bad one, and the third book is where the habit finally showed in the prose.</p>
<p>There is a sentence in the last chapter that I did not plan. The god says: what survives you is the number of people who would notice by Thursday. I had intended to end on something about incense. That sentence arrived instead, and I knew immediately that it was the book — that everything in the three volumes had been aimed at it and I had not known. That is the only reliable experience of writing I have ever had: the moment when a sentence you did not intend turns out to be the one the whole thing was for.</p>
<p>Now that it is on the page I can see that it is also a statement about the trilogy itself. Three books about a god, and the god turns out to have been, all along, an argument about who notices whom.</p>
<h4 class="secnum">2</h4>
<p>The three books have a shape that I did not design and only saw after the second was finished.</p>
<p>The first is a book about a person alone in a room. Its question is am I allowed to exist? and its answer is yes, and the answer is delivered by an outside voice because there is no interior one available yet.</p>
<p>The second is a book about a person in a world. Its question is now that I am allowed to exist, how do I live? and its answers are all practical and all unglamorous: how to love, how to belong, how to bury the dead, how to die, how to remain human in front of a machine that sorts people.</p>
<p>The third — this one — is a book about a person who is no longer the protagonist. Its question is what happens to the thing after I hand it on? and its answers are the least consoling in the series, because the honest answers to that question are: it gets distorted, it gets institutionalized, it gets funded, it splits over a curtain, and then it ends. And also, against all expectation, it sometimes reaches somebody.</p>
<p>I did not plan this progression. It is the progression of a life, and I have lived most of it since I started, and I think that is the only reason the third book is any good.</p>
<h4 class="secnum">3</h4>
<p>Some honesty about the machinery, as in the previous volumes.</p>
<p>The god speaks less here because I made him. That is a craft decision and it is also, I think, the truth about a long practice: the voice you consult becomes quieter, and not because it has abandoned you. In the third book he asks questions, he admits two errors, he tells one story he has never told, and in Chapter Seventeen he says three sentences and stops. The chapters where he is quiet are the chapters where the reader is finally doing the work.</p>
<p>The errors are real. In Chapter Six he retracts the advice about waiting that he gave in the second book, and he retracts the claim from the first book that all aggression is fear. I want to be clear that these are not devices. They are corrections I owed. The waiting advice in particular has bothered me since I wrote it — an instruction without a clock in it will eventually be used to justify neglect, and I wrote one, and someone wrote to tell me what it had cost them, and this chapter is the repair. The instruction now has a date in it. If you have only read one thing in this book, read that.</p>
<p>Lin Tao, again. He appears in these books as the boy who survived the river. I said in the previous afterword that he is a composite and not a documented person, and I repeat it, because he has begun to be quoted as though he were historical. He is not. He is several documented cases and one meeting in a temple in 2019.</p>
<p>The other gods. Chapters Fifteen and Sixteen are the ones I am least qualified to have written. I am a Buddhist monk writing about Daoist and folk Chinese deities, and then about Hindu, Sumerian, Roman, and Mexica figures. On the Chinese material I am on reasonably firm ground, though folk practice varies enormously by region and my Fujian will not match someone else's Shanxi. On the non-Chinese material I am an outsider with a library. I have tried to signal uncertainty in the text. I have certainly gotten things wrong. If you belong to one of these traditions and you find an error, tell me; I will correct it, publicly and with thanks, because that is the practice this book recommends and I am not exempt from it.</p>
<p>The practices, again. Assembled, not received. Drawn from monastic training, from the folk customs I grew up around, from the clinical and organizational literature, and from people who told me what worked. Not medicine, not therapy. Chapters Four, Nine, Thirteen, and Seventeen touch on burnout, grief, and the unmade work, where a book is the wrong instrument and a person is the right one.</p>
<h4 class="secnum">4</h4>
<p>One thing about the reception of the first two books that I want to record, because it has shaped the third and because I think it is worth saying out loud.</p>
<p>A small industry has grown up around this god. There are now retreats, decks of cards, candles, a certification of sorts, and at least one person charging for a ritual in his name. I have been asked, more than once, to endorse these things, and I have declined, and I want to state the reason plainly rather than let it be guessed at.</p>
<p>It is not that commerce is impurity. I wrote a whole chapter saying the opposite, and I meant it: the courtyard has always had merchants in it, and the woman who has swept for nine years should be paid.</p>
<p>It is the test I gave in the second book, applied without sentiment: does this enterprise become less profitable if its customers become less afraid? A candle does not. A weekend retreat sold to frightened people at three thousand dollars does. And a god who was made out of a beating should not be the mechanism by which somebody's fear is turned into a subscription.</p>
<p>So: no endorsement, no licensing, no priesthood. He has never had one and he does not need one. If you want to do something in his name, do the thing in Chapter Sixteen — find the abandoned shrine on your own street and learn its name. It costs nothing, it cannot be monetized, and it is the closest thing to the original practice that I know of.</p>
<h4 class="secnum">5</h4>
<p>The last thing.</p>
<p>When I was thirty-one and frightened, I wanted a god who would tell me I was not broken. I got one, in the sense that I constructed one and then listened to him, which I have argued is the mechanism by which all of this works and is not a lesser thing than inspiration.</p>
<p>At fifty-eight I want something different, and I notice that what I now want is not a voice. It is a number. It is the count of people who would notice by Thursday if I stopped arriving. And I find, looking at my own life, that the number is not large, and that this is the only accounting that has ever made me want to change anything.</p>
<p>I have spent three books building an argument that ends in arithmetic.</p>
<p>So: go and count yours. Add one. Then another. Do the visit. Learn the name. Set the date. Put the price on the wall. Say the thing you were wrong about, out loud, in front of everybody. And when the room closes — because it will — hold one more meeting, write down what the room knew, and hand the list to somebody.</p>
<p>The moonlight in the first book fell on the pine and on the mud without choosing. It still does. The hall light is on. I have said everything I have.</p>
<p>Yancan</p>
<p>The monastery, before dawn, 2026</p>
<p>Glossary of Chinese Terms</p>
<p class="glossary">Bed Gods (床公床母), Lord and Lady of the Bed — A paired domestic deity worshipped at the bed itself, presiding over sleep, sex, childbirth, and the safety of the person lying down. The altar was the bed.</p>
<p class="glossary">Changle (长乐) — A coastal district near the Min River mouth in Fujian; in the popular memory of the cult, the beach where sworn brothers knelt to take their oath.</p>
<p class="glossary">Handing over (交代) — The practice, at a departure or a death, of saying the four sentences: thank you; I forgive you; forgive me; I love you. Not confined to deathbeds; usable at any ending.</p>
<p class="glossary">Hu Tianbao (胡天保) — The young Fujianese man whose death produced the Rabbit God. Beaten to death by order of a regional inspector in the early Qing dynasty, allegedly for spying on an official he desired. He is the "I" of all the divine chapters in these books.</p>
<p class="glossary">Hungry ghost (饿鬼) — In Chinese folk belief, the spirit of a person who died without descendants or without rites, and who therefore goes unfed. Used in these books less as cosmology than as a description of what an un-mourned death does to the living.</p>
<p class="glossary">Lu Weiming (卢威明) — The Taiwanese ritual master who founded the Weiming Temple, the most prominent modern center of the Rabbit God's worship.</p>
<p class="glossary">Min River (闽江) — The principal river of Fujian, entering the sea near Fuzhou; its estuary appears in these books as the site of clan discipline.</p>
<p class="glossary">Nanfeng (南风), the Southern Wind — A Ming- and Qing-period term for the male homoerotic subculture of the southeast coast.</p>
<p class="glossary">Qi (契) — A contract. Used in qixiongdi and qifuzi to mark a relationship made by oath rather than by blood.</p>
<p class="glossary">Qifuzi (契父子), sworn father and son — An oath-bound relationship, common aboard ships, in which an elder took material responsibility for a younger man's livelihood, marriage, and burial.</p>
<p class="glossary">Qixiongdi (契兄弟), sworn brothers — The best-documented form of oath-bound male union in late imperial Fujian and Taiwan. As much an economic and funerary arrangement as a romantic one.</p>
<p class="glossary">Smallpox Mother (痘疹娘娘) — A goddess propitiated during outbreaks of smallpox, a disease that killed children in numbers difficult for a modern reader to imagine; feared, thanked, and never loved.</p>
<p class="glossary">Tanka (疍民) — The boat-dwelling populations of the South China coast, historically barred from land ownership and, in practice, from the clan system.</p>
<p class="glossary">Tu'er Shen (兔儿神) — Literally, the Rabbit God. Rabbit was in the Qing period a derogatory term for a boy kept by a man; the god's name is therefore an insult that was occupied and converted. See Chapter Fourteen.</p>
<p class="glossary">Weiming Temple (威明堂) — The temple in Taiwan, founded by Master Lu Weiming, that revived the cult in the modern era.</p>
<p class="glossary">Wuji (无极) — The undifferentiated absolute; in Daoist cosmology, the state before the division into yin and yang.</p>
<p class="glossary">Yancan (炎灿) — The author's pen name.</p>
<p class="glossary">Yinsi (淫祀) — "Improper" or licentious cult; the category under which literati and the state classified local worship they did not authorize.</p>
<p class="glossary">Yuan Mei (袁枚) — Qing dynasty poet and essayist (1716–1798), whose collection Zi Buyu preserved the story of Hu Tianbao.</p>
<p class="glossary">Zigu (紫姑), the Lady of the Latrine — A folk goddess said to have been a concubine killed by a jealous wife and thrown into the privy, and to have become a deity there. Invoked by women, in the place where she died, about matters they could not raise elsewhere.</p>
<p class="glossary">Zi Buyu (子不语) — What the Master Would Not Discuss, Yuan Mei's collection of strange tales. Volume 19 contains the earliest substantial account of the Rabbit God.</p>

<p>A Short Chronology of the Cult</p>
<p class="glossary">Late Ming (16th–17th c.) — Coastal trade, piracy, and migration weaken clan authority on the Fujian and Taiwan littoral. Oath-bound unions (qixiongdi, qifuzi) develop among men without land as instruments of mutual support.</p>
<p class="glossary">Early Qing (17th–18th c.) — Hu Tianbao is beaten to death in Fujian. Local shrines appear within a generation. The cult travels with migrants, usually unnamed, frequently at the edge of a settlement.</p>
<p>1792 — Yuan Mei publishes Zi Buyu, volume 19 of which contains the story of the Rabbit God. The cult acquires a text, and therefore a future.</p>
<p class="glossary">Republican and Japanese colonial periods (early 20th c.) — Medicalized, Westernized categories of sexuality displace the older idiom of the Southern Wind. The shrines scatter or go quiet.</p>
<p>2006 onward — Master Lu Weiming establishes the Weiming Temple in Taiwan. Scholars, devotees, and writers reconstruct liturgy, iconography, and meditation practice. The cult becomes global, and a badly drawn Rabbit God appears on a keychain.</p>
<p>The present — The god is worshipped on servers. A small market in candles has developed. He has not endorsed any of it. Several local shrines within walking distance of most readers have no worshipper at all, and their names are held by people in their eighties.</p>
<p>Note on Sources</p>
<p>As in the previous volumes, the historical spine is thin and the reader who wishes to check me should know exactly how thin.</p>
<p>Yuan Mei, Zi Buyu (What the Master Would Not Discuss), vol. 19 — the earliest extended account of Hu Tianbao. Everything else in these books is commentary on two hundred characters of Qing-dynasty gossip.</p>
<p>Shen Defu, Wanli Yehuo Bian (Unofficial Gleanings of the Wanli Reign), supplement — a near-contemporary account of the Southern Wind in Fujian.</p>
<p>Bao Hongwei — the contemporary scholarship on the cult of Tu'er Shen, its death, desire, and modern revival.</p>
<p>Michael Szonyi — on Hu Tianbao and Qing-dynasty discourse on same-sex relations.</p>
<p>On Chinese popular religion generally: the ethnographic and folkloric literature on domestic and "unclean" deities — the latrine goddess, the bed gods, the smallpox deity, the god of poverty. Regional variation is enormous; what I describe is roughly Fujianese and should not be generalized.</p>
<p>On the non-Chinese material in Chapter Sixteen: I am an outsider to every tradition I mention there. I have relied on secondary scholarship and have tried to mark my uncertainty in the text. Anyone belonging to those traditions will find things to correct, and corrections are welcome.</p>
<p>On the organizational material in Chapters Seven through Thirteen: the literature on informal hierarchy in voluntary associations, founder syndrome, the displacement of unmeasurable work in funded organizations, and the closure of small institutions. I have not footnoted it; footnotes would make this a different and worse book, and the claims are not original to me.</p>
<p>On the clinical material: the literature on caregiving, burnout, intellectualization, and complicated grief. Again unfootnoted, again not invented.</p>
<p>And, as before, the rest — the six sentences, the handover, the date in the calendar, the price on the wall, the word said out loud in an empty room — comes from people who told me what worked, and asked me not to use their names.</p>
<p>The Three Books in One Sentence</p>
<p>Book One: You were never broken, and you have never once left home.</p>
<p>Book Two: You are not broken, and now there is a Tuesday, and somebody is standing in a doorway.</p>
<p>Book Three: Somebody is standing in a doorway, and what survives you is the number of people who would notice by Thursday.</p>
<p>The Third Book ends here. The hall light is on. Go and count.</p>
